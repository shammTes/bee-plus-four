## Chapter Content (English)

## Gerunds, Infinitives & Participles

**Definition:** **Gerunds, Infinitives & Participles** (ገርንድታት፡ ኢንፊኒቲቭታትን ተሳታፊ ግሶችን) are non-finite verb forms that do not inflect for tense or person. Instead, they function syntactically as nouns (gerunds), nouns/adverbs/adjectives (infinitives), or adjectives and clause-reducers (participles).

In English, lexical verbs are highly selective regarding the non-finite structures they govern. They are categorized into verbs followed strictly by a gerund ($$\text{Verb} + \text{V-ing}$$, e.g., *avoid, enjoy, deny, postpone, practice*) and those followed strictly by a to-infinitive ($$\text{Verb} + \text{to} + \text{V}_{\text{base}}$$, e.g., *agree, decide, hope, promise, refuse*). Tigrinya does not possess morphological equivalent categories; instead, it uses a single verbal noun (ሽም ግሲ - e.g., "ምጽሓፍ") or an infinitival prefix ("ክ-") to express both ideas depending on context. This structural overlap causes Tigrinya speakers to rely heavily on direct translation, leading to errors like "I enjoy to read" instead of "I enjoy reading" because "to read" maps directly to their internal translation of "ክነብብ".

Advanced matriculation exams focus heavily on verbs that govern both structures but undergo significant semantic shifts (ለውጢ ትርጉም ምስ ፍሉያት ግሶች). These include *remember, forget, regret, stop,* and *try*. For example:
- `$$\text{Remember/Forget/Regret} + \text{to-infinitive}$$` refers to a future duty or obligation (e.g., "Remember *to lock* the door").
- `$$\text{Remember/Forget/Regret} + \text{Gerund}$$` refers to a past memory or recollection of an action already performed (e.g., "I remember *locking* the door").
- `$$\text{Stop} + \text{to-infinitive}$$` means pausing an action *in order to* perform another (e.g., "He stopped *to smoke*").
- `$$\text{Stop} + \text{Gerund}$$` means completely ceasing or quitting an activity (e.g., "He stopped *smoking*").
- `$$\text{Try} + \text{to-infinitive}$$` means making an effort to do something difficult (e.g., "I tried *to lift* the box").
- `$$\text{Try} + \text{Gerund}$$` means experimenting with a method to see if it solves a problem (e.g., "Try *taking* an aspirin").

Another high-frequency test target is the **Prepositional Gerund Rule**. All prepositions must be followed strictly by gerunds ($$\text{Preposition} + \text{Gerund}$$, e.g., "She is interested *in studying* chemistry"). This rule introduces a major syntactic trap involving the word **"to"**. In certain phrasal verbs and adjectives (e.g., *look forward to, object to, be used to, adapt to, addition to*), "to" is a preposition, not part of an infinitive. Consequently, it must be followed by a gerund ($$\text{look forward to} + \text{V-ing}$$, e.g., "I look forward to *meeting* you"). Students almost always select the base verb ("meet") on exams, failing to recognize "to" as a preposition.

Participle clauses (ተሳታፊ ሓረጋት) are used to reduce active, passive, or sequential adverbial clauses, making academic writing more concise. Present participle clauses ($$\text{V-ing} + \dots$$, e.g., "*Seeing* the police, the thief ran") replace active clauses. Past participle clauses ($$\text{V}_3 + \dots$$, e.g., "*Built* in 1950, the school is old") replace passive clauses. Perfect participle clauses ($$\text{Having} + \text{V}_3 \dots$$, e.g., "*Having completed* his exam, Dawit left") indicate that one past action was fully finished before another began. Perfect participle clauses are heavily tested in sentence completion sections.

A critical error associated with participle clauses is the **dangling modifier** (ዝተንጠልጠለ ቅጽል). In English, the implicit subject of a participle clause must match the explicit subject of the main clause. If they do not match, the sentence is grammatically incorrect (e.g., $$\times \text{Walking down the road, the library appeared}$$ is incorrect because the library cannot walk). Tigrinya speakers often overlook dangling modifiers because Tigrinya allows more flexible clause-linking structures. To avoid this trap, students must apply the **Subject-Matching Strategy**: locate the main clause subject and verify if it can logically perform the action of the participle clause.

| Verb Pattern | Syntactic Rule / Usage | Tigrinya Concept Equivalent / Note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $$\text{Verb} + \text{Gerund}$$ | Governed by verbs like *avoid, enjoy, deny*. | ምግባር (Verbal noun functioning as object) |
| $$\text{Verb} + \text{to-infinitive}$$ | Governed by verbs like *decide, promise, hope*. | ክገብር (Infinitival desire/plan) |
| $$\text{Preposition} + \text{Gerund}$$ | Mandatory gerund after all prepositions. | ድሕሪ መስተዋድድ ወትሩ ገርንድ ይስዕብ |
| $$\text{look forward to} + \text{V-ing}$$ | Phrasal preposition requiring a gerund. | 'to' ኣብዚ መስተዋድድ እዩ (highly tested trap) |
| $$\text{Having} + \text{V}_3 \dots$$ | Perfect participle: completed prior action. | ምስ ወድአ / ሕሉፍ ተግባር (Completed sequence) |

> **💡 Did You Know?**: The word *gerund* comes from the Latin word *gerundum*, which means "to be carried out"! Even though gerunds look exactly like present participles (both end in *"-ing"*), they have completely different grammatical jobs: a gerund acts as a **noun**, while a participle acts as an **adjective** or a **verb-modifier**! 

> **🧠 Pro-Tip**: To easily solve **remember/forget/stop** semantic questions under intense exam time pressure, apply the **Timeline Test**:
- If the action in the non-finite verb happens **before** the main verb, choose the **Gerund** ($$	ext{V-ing}$$) because it is a past memory (e.g., *I remember visiting Axum* - I visited first, then I remembered it now).
- If the action in the non-finite verb happens **after** the main verb, choose the **to-infinitive** because it is a future duty (e.g., *Remember to visit Axum* - I must remember first, then I will visit later). This timeline check works every time!

### 📝 Topic Challenge (Moderate to Hard)

1. Choose the grammatically correct option: "The driver managed to avoid ________ the pedestrian on the narrow road."
A. to hit
B. hitting
C. hit
D. having hit
*Answer: B. "Avoid" is a verb that must strictly be followed by a gerund ($$\text{avoid} + \text{Gerund}$$). This makes B correct.*

2. Fill in the blank: "We are extremely looking forward to ________ the ancient ruins of Axum next month."
A. visit
B. visiting
C. to visit
D. visited
*Answer: B. In "look forward to", "to" is a preposition, not an infinitive marker. Therefore, it must be followed by a gerund ("visiting"). Option A is a classic matriculation trap.*

3. Choose the correct verb form: "I clearly remember ________ the laboratory door, but I will double-check to be sure."
A. to lock
B. locking
C. lock
D. having to lock
*Answer: B. The context indicates a past memory (I locked the door first, and now I recall doing it). This requires the gerund "locking". "To lock" would incorrectly imply a future duty.*

4. Complete the sentence: "While walking to the library, he stopped ________ some traditional bread from the local bakery."
A. buying
B. to buy
C. buy
D. having bought
*Answer: B. The context indicates that he paused his walk *in order to* buy bread (purpose). This requires "to buy". "Buying" would mean he quit the habit of buying bread.* 

5. Identify the error in this sentence: "Having completed his final chemistry experiment, the laboratory keys were returned to the teacher."
*Answer: This is a dangling modifier error. The subject of the main clause ("the laboratory keys") cannot logically perform the action of the perfect participle clause ("Having completed his experiment"). The subject of the main clause should be "Dawit" or "the student" (e.g., "Having completed..., Dawit returned the keys...").*

### 29. Complete the sentence: 'The manager refused ________ (sign) the contract.'

*Type:* WORKOUT  |  *Topic:* Gerunds vs. Infinitives

- signing
- to sign
- sign
- having signed

**Answer:** to sign

**Explanation:**

**Step 1: Analyze the grammatical structure**
The lexical verb 'refuse' strictly governs a to-infinitive: $$\text{refuse} + \text{to-infinitive}$$.

**Step 2: Identify the matriculation trap**
Students often choose 'signing' (Option A) due to direct translation. Only the to-infinitive 'to sign' (Option B) is grammatically correct.

**Step 3: Tigrinya Explanation (ትርጉም)**
'Refuse' (ምእባይ) ዝብል ግሲ ወግዒ ወትሩ ብ 'to-infinitive' እዩ ዝስዕብ። ስለዚ ድሕሪኡ ዝመጽእ ግሲ 'to sign' (Option B - ክፈርም) ክኸውን ኣለዎ።


### 30. Complete the sentence: 'She tried ________ (lift) the heavy box, but it was too difficult.'

*Type:* WORKOUT  |  *Topic:* Semantic Shifts

- lifting
- to lift
- lift
- to be lifting

**Answer:** to lift

**Explanation:**

**Step 1: Analyze the grammatical structure**
The context 'but it was too difficult' indicates making a strong physical effort to perform a hard task. This requires a to-infinitive: $$\text{try} + \text{to-infinitive}$$.

**Step 2: Identify the matriculation trap**
Students often choose 'lifting' (Option A). However, 'try lifting' would mean experimenting with lifting the box as a solution to a problem. Since she struggled with the effort, 'to lift' (Option B) is correct.

**Step 3: Tigrinya Explanation (ትርጉም)**
'Try' ምስ 'to-infinitive' ክስራዕ ከሎ 'ጻዕሪ ምግባር' (make a physical effort) ዝብል ትርጉም ይህብ። እቲ ሳጹን ሓጺን ከቢድ ስለ ዝነበረን ንምልዓሉ ጻዕሪ ስለ ዝገበረት፡ ቅኑዕ መልሲ 'to lift' (Option B - ንምልዓል) እዩ።
