## Chapter Content (English)

## Modals, Semi-Modals & Modal Functions

**Definition:** **Modal Auxiliaries and Semi-Modals** (ሞዳልን ፍርቂ-ሞዳልን ግሶችን ስርሓቶምን) are a specialized class of helping verbs used to express the speaker's attitude or evaluation regarding ability, permission, obligation, possibility, logical deduction, and past regrets. Unlike lexical verbs, they do not have separate infinitive or participle forms and are always followed by a bare infinitive verb pattern.

English modal auxiliaries (*can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, would*) and semi-modals (*have to, ought to, used to, need to, dare*) possess unique syntactic characteristics that differentiate them from standard verbs. They are structurally invariant: they do not take an "-s" suffix in the third-person singular present ($$\times \text{she musts}$$, $$\times \text{he cans}$$), they lack participle or infinitive forms ($$\times \text{to must}$$, $$\times \text{musting}$$), and they are always followed by a bare infinitive ($$\text{Subject} + \text{Modal} + \text{V}_{\text{base}}$$). Contrast this with Tigrinya modal functions, which are expressed through highly inflected lexical verbs such as "ክእል" (to be able to) or "ኣለዎ" (to have to) that conjugate fully for gender, person, and number. This mismatch is a major source of early-stage writing errors for Tigrinya-speaking students, who often try to conjugate English modals or mistakenly insert "to" after core modals (e.g., writing "He should to study" instead of "He should study").

Modals of ability (ክእለት) and permission (ፍቓድ) include *can, could, be able to, may,* and *might*. *Can* expresses general present ability, while *could* expresses general past ability. However, national examinations frequently test a subtle syntactic distinction: for a specific, successful single action in the past (ብፍሉይ ኣጋጣሚ ኣብ ሕሉፍ ግዜ ዝተዓመመ ዓወት), English requires the use of *was/were able to* or *managed to* instead of *could* ($$\text{Subject} + \text{was/were able to} + \text{V}_{\text{base}}$$). For example, "The fire was intense, but the firefighters *were able to* save the building" is correct, whereas "could save" is incorrect in this context. For permission, *may* represents formal permission (ዝለዓለ ክብርን ወግዓውነትን ዘለዎ), while *can* represents informal permission. *Might* is extremely polite and hesitant, often used in indirect requests.

Modals of obligation (ግዴታ), necessity (ኣድላይነት), and prohibition (ክልከላ) include *must, have to, should, ought to, had better,* and *must not* vs. *don't have to*. There is a critical semantic and syntactic contrast between *must not* and *don't/doesn't have to*. *Must not* ($$\text{S} + \text{must not} + \text{V}_{\text{base}}$$) expresses strict prohibition (ምኽልካል) — the action is forbidden. Conversely, *don't/doesn't have to* ($$\text{S} + \text{don't/doesn't have to} + \text{V}_{\text{base}}$$) indicates a complete lack of obligation (ግዴታ ዘይምዃን) — the action is optional. For example, "You *must not* smoke in the library" (forbidden) vs. "You *don't have to* buy a ticket; entrance is free" (optional). Because Tigrinya often uses negative structures that overlap these concepts (e.g., "ክትገብሮ የብልካን"), students frequently mistake *don't have to* for a strict prohibition on exams, falling into a classic examiner's trap.

Logical deduction (ስነ-ሞጎታዊ መደምደምታ) in the present tense is expressed using *must be* (strong positive certainty — 90% positive assurance), *can't be* (strong negative certainty — 90% negative assurance), and *may/might/could be* (uncertainty or possibility — 50% assurance). Underline that *must not* is NEVER used for negative deduction in English. For example, if you see someone with a heavy winter coat in the summer, you say, "He *must be* crazy," and if you see someone who is clearly awake, you say, "He *can't be* sleeping." Writing "He must not be sleeping" to express negative certainty is a major logical and syntactic error on matriculation exams. The modal *can't* acts as the direct negative equivalent of *must* for epistemic deduction.

Past logical deduction (ናይ ሕሉፍ እዋን ስነ-ሞጎታዊ መደምደምታ) requires a perfect infinitive structure: $$\text{Subject} + \text{Modal} + \text{have} + \text{V}_3$$. Positive past certainty is expressed by $$\text{Subject} + \text{must have} + \text{V}_3$$ (e.g., "The ground is wet; it *must have rained* last night"). Negative past certainty is expressed by $$\text{Subject} + \text{can't/couldn't have} + \text{V}_3$$ (e.g., "He *can't have stolen* the money; he was with me in the library all afternoon"). Past possibility is expressed by $$\text{Subject} + \text{might/may/could have} + \text{V}_3$$ (e.g., "She didn't answer the phone; she *might have left* it in her office"). Matriculation exams heavily test these structures using context clues that indicate whether the action was certain, impossible, or merely possible.

Modals of past regret (ጣዕሳ) and missed opportunities are expressed using $$\text{Subject} + \text{should have} + \text{V}_3$$ and $$\text{Subject} + \text{ought to have} + \text{V}_3$$. These structures criticize past actions or express regret for actions that were advisable but did not happen. For example, "I failed the physics exam; I *should have studied* harder" implies that the student did not study and regrets it. Conversely, $$\text{Subject} + \text{shouldn't have} + \text{V}_3$$ criticizes a past action that did happen but was undesirable (e.g., "You *shouldn't have eaten* that street food; now you are sick"). Tigrinya speakers frequently struggle to distinguish between *must have* (past logical deduction of fact) and *should have* (past regret/unrealized advice), which is one of the most highly penalized conceptual errors in national English examinations. Mastering these semantic shifts is essential for achieving a high score.

| English Form/Pattern | Usage/Function | Tigrinya Concept Equivalent / Note |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $$\text{S} + \text{must not} + \text{V}_{\text{base}}$$ | Prohibition: Forbidden action (e.g., "You must not cheat"). | ብፍጹም ክግበር የብሉን / ክልኩል እዩ (Strict prohibition) |
| $$\text{S} + \text{don't have to} + \text{V}_{\text{base}}$$ | Lack of obligation: Optional action (e.g., "You don't have to go"). | ግዴታ ኣይኮነን / ምርጫዊ እዩ (Optional/no obligation) |
| $$\text{S} + \text{must have} + \text{V}_3$$ | Past Logical Deduction: High certainty (e.g., "It must have rained"). | ብርግጽ ከምኡ ኮይኑ ክኸውን ኣለዎ (Positive past certainty) |
| $$\text{S} + \text{can't have} + \text{V}_3$$ | Past Logical Deduction: High negative certainty (e.g., "He can't have done it"). | ብርግጽ ከምኡ ክኸውን ኣይኽእልን (Negative past certainty) |

> **💡 Did You Know?**: The semi-modal *had better* ($$	ext{Subject} + 	ext{had better} + 	ext{V}_{	ext{base}}$$) is used to give strong, urgent advice that carries a negative consequence if ignored! Even though it contains the past tense auxiliary *had*, it strictly refers to the present or future. For example, "You *had better* study for the exam [or you will fail]"! In informal speech, people often drop the *had* and just say "You better," but this is grammatically incorrect in your matriculation exams!

> **🧠 Pro-Tip**: To easily differentiate between **must have** and **should have** under intense exam time pressure, look for emotional or evaluative clues in the context:
- If the sentence contains words like *"I regret," "unfortunately," "it was a mistake,"* or *"I failed,"* always choose **should have** ($$	ext{should have} + 	ext{V}_3$$) because it expresses regret.
- If the sentence contains words of logical evidence like *"because," "look at the," "I am sure,"* or *"clearly,"* always choose **must have** ($$	ext{must have} + 	ext{V}_3$$) because it expresses a logical deduction! 

### 📝 Topic Challenge (Moderate to Hard)

1. Choose the grammatically correct option: "The physics classroom was locked, but the lab assistant ________ find the spare key to let us in."
A. could
B. was able to
C. can
D. should have
*Answer: B. This sentence describes a specific, successful action in a past situation. In English, we must use "was able to" or "managed to" instead of "could" for specific past successes. "Could" is only used for general past ability. This makes B correct.*

2. Fill in the blank: "We ________ buy any fresh vegetables today because my brother had already bought plenty of them yesterday."
*Answer: "didn't have to" (or "needn't"). This sentence describes a lack of obligation or necessity (it was optional because we already had vegetables), not a prohibition. "Must not" would incorrectly imply that buying vegetables was strictly forbidden.*

3. Choose the correct modal form: "Aster failed her college entrance examination; she ________ studied harder instead of playing games."
A. must have
B. should have
C. can't have
D. would have
*Answer: B. This sentence describes a past regret or criticism of an unrealized past action (she did not study, and now she regrets it). This strictly requires the past regret modal "should have" ($$\text{should have} + \text{V}_3$$).*

4. Complete the sentence: "The primary document has vanished from the safe; someone ________ taken it during the lunch hour."
A. should have
B. must have
C. ought to have
D. can't have
*Answer: B. This is a past logical deduction based on clear evidence (the document has vanished). This requires "must have" ($$\text{must have} + \text{V}_3$$) to show positive past certainty.*

5. Identify the error: "He had better to not miss the final revision class tomorrow if he wants to pass."
*Answer: The error is "to not". "Had better" is a semi-modal followed strictly by a bare infinitive and must be negated as "had better not" ($$\text{had better} + \text{not} + \text{V}_{\text{base}}$$). The corrected sentence should read: "He had better not miss...".*

### 1. I failed the national biology matriculation exam; I ________ harder instead of wasting my time on social media.

*Type:* MC  |  *Topic:* Modals of Past Regret

- must have studied
- should have studied
- can't have studied
- ought to study

**Answer:** should have studied

**Explanation:**

**Step 1: Analyze the grammatical structure**
The context 'I failed...; I [blank] harder' indicates a past regret or criticism of an action that was advisable but did not happen. This requires the past regret modal structure ($$\text{should have} + \text{V}_3$$).

**Step 2: Identify the matriculation trap**
Students often confuse 'must have studied' (logical deduction of fact) with 'should have studied' (regret). Option A implies the speaker is deducing that they studied, which contradicts 'I failed'. Option B is correct because it expresses regret.
