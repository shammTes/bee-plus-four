# Authored Grade 11 geography top-ups (facts from the Grade 11 Geography textbook)
@ geo11-u4
Q: According to the textbook, what are the three basic components of any human settlement?
+ shelter, infrastructure and services
- roads, rivers and mountains
- farms, forests and deserts
- schools, churches and markets only
E: The textbook says every settlement consists of shelter (buildings), infrastructure (networks of roads, water, sewerage, energy, information) and services (the support a community needs, such as health and education).

Q: According to the textbook, towns are mainly distinguished from villages by:
+ the activities of their inhabitants, such as trade and commerce
- the colour of their houses
- always having more than one million people
- being located only on the coast
E: The textbook explains that towns are not distinguished by size but by the activities of their people. An urban settlement is a nucleated, non-agricultural settlement where trade and commerce are the main occupations.

Q: Why is it difficult to compare urban populations of different countries?
+ Each country uses its own definition of "urban".
- Urban populations never change.
- No country counts its towns.
- All countries use exactly the same definition.
E: There is no universal definition of "urban". For example, the minimum population for a town is 200 in Denmark, 2500 in the USA and 30 000 in Japan, so the figures are not directly comparable.

Q: In which year did the United Nations announce that 50% of the world's population lived in urban settlements?
+ 2008
- 1900
- 1950
- 1980
E: The textbook states that the urban share was 14% in 1900, 29% in 1950, 41% in 1980 and 50% in 2008.

Q: In 2008, which world region had the lowest share of urban population according to the textbook (Table 4.3)?
+ Africa (38%)
- Europe (79%)
- North America (79%)
- Latin America and the Caribbean (77%)
E: Table 4.3 shows Africa at 38%, Asia 42%, Oceania 70%, Latin America 77%, and Europe and North America 79% each.

Q: A town located at a point where goods must be moved from one type of transport to another (for example from ship to train) is called a:
+ break-of-bulk town
- railhead town
- mining town
- waterpower town
E: At a break-of-bulk point, the journey of people and goods is interrupted and cargo is unloaded and reloaded, which creates jobs and trade. A railhead is where a railway ends.

Q: The difference between the "site" and the "situation" of a town is that:
+ site is the actual land the town is built on; situation is its position in relation to surrounding areas
- site and situation mean exactly the same
- site is the population; situation is the area
- site is the history of the town; situation is its name
E: Site refers to the physical features of the place a town occupies (terrain, water supply, defence). Situation refers to its location relative to other places, routes and resources.

Q: When cities grow and merge into one huge continuous urban area (for example on the east coast of the USA), it is called a:
+ megalopolis
- hamlet
- village
- dispersed settlement
E: The merging of neighbouring cities is conurbation; a very large region formed this way is called a megalopolis. A hamlet is a tiny rural settlement.

@ geo11-u7
Q: According to the textbook, which Eritrean river is perennial (flows all year)?
+ Setit
- Anseba
- Barka
- Haddas
E: Most Eritrean rivers are seasonal: they flow in the rainy season and are dry the rest of the year. The Setit (Tekeze) is the exception.

Q: Lake Bordole, a seasonal salty lake where salt is extracted, is located in the:
+ Buri Peninsula
- Central Highlands near Asmara
- Gash-Barka plains near Tesseney
- Dahlak Islands
E: The textbook says Lake Bordole is in the Buri Peninsula. Lake Bada and Lake Aba Adad are in the Danakil Depression. All are salty lakes that dry up in the dry season.

Q: Why do most Eritrean rivers fail to reach the Red Sea?
+ They are seasonal, and their water sinks into the sandy surface of the lowlands.
- They flow uphill.
- They are frozen most of the year.
- They all flow into the Nile instead.
E: Eritrean rivers flow only after rains. When they reach the hot, sandy lowlands, much of the water evaporates or sinks into the sand, so most of them do not reach the sea.

Q: Why do Eritrean rivers have high erosive power?
+ They flow down steep slopes from the highlands.
- They flow very slowly across flat land.
- They carry no sediment.
- They are fed by glaciers.
E: Rivers that start in the wet highlands drop steeply towards the lowlands. Fast water erodes strongly, and deposition happens mainly in the lower parts of the river courses.
