@ eng10-u1
Q: Choose the best modal: "All students ____ wear their school uniform. It is a school rule."
+ must
- might
- could
- would
E: "Must" expresses strong obligation, such as a rule. "Might" and "could" express possibility, and "would" is used for conditions or polite requests.

Q: Choose the best modal: "You look ill. You ____ see a doctor."
+ should
- mustn't
- can't
- needn't
E: "Should" is used to give advice. "Mustn't" means it is forbidden, "can't" means impossible, and "needn't" means it is not necessary, which do not fit advice to see a doctor.

Q: "You mustn't use your phone during the exam" means:
+ It is not allowed.
- It is not necessary.
- It is a good idea.
- It is possible.
E: "Mustn't" (must not) means something is forbidden. "Not necessary" is "don't have to" or "needn't".

Q: "You don't have to come tomorrow; it's a holiday" means:
+ It is not necessary for you to come.
- You are not allowed to come.
- You must come.
- You will probably come.
E: "Don't have to" means there is no obligation. It is different from "mustn't", which means it is forbidden.

Q: Choose the best option: "Yesterday I ____ walk to school because the bus broke down."
+ had to
- must
- should
- have to
E: "Must" has no past form. To talk about obligation in the past we use "had to". "Have to" is present, and "should" is advice.

Q: Modals for inference: "The lights are off and nobody answers the door. They ____ be at home."
+ can't
- must
- should
- will
E: We use "can't" when we are almost sure that something is NOT true, based on evidence. The evidence (lights off, no answer) shows they are not at home.

Q: Modals for inference: "Helen has been studying all night. She ____ be very tired."
+ must
- can't
- mustn't
- needn't
E: "Must" for inference means we are almost sure something IS true, based on evidence. Studying all night is good evidence that she is tired.

Q: Modals for inference: "I'm not sure where Daniel is. He ____ be in the library."
+ might
- must
- can't
- has to
E: "Might" (or may/could) shows that something is possible, but we are not sure. "Must" and "can't" show certainty, and "has to" is obligation.

Q: Choose the best option: "He ____ have forgotten the meeting; he never misses meetings."
+ can't
- must
- should
- would
E: "Can't have + past participle" means we are almost sure something did NOT happen in the past. Because he never misses meetings, we are sure he did not forget.

Q: Choose the best option: "The ground is wet. It ____ have rained last night."
+ must
- can't
- shouldn't
- needn't
E: "Must have + past participle" means we are almost sure something happened in the past, because of the evidence (wet ground).

Q: Which sentence gives advice?
+ You ought to save some of your pocket money.
- You must not enter.
- You can't be serious.
- You might be right.
E: "Ought to" has the same meaning as "should" and is used for advice. "Must not" is prohibition, and the last two are inferences.

Q: A "responsible" person is someone who:
+ can be trusted to do what they should
- never does any work
- always blames other people
- is always late
E: A responsible person does their duties and can be trusted. The other options describe irresponsible behaviour.

Q: Which word is the noun form of "responsible"?
+ responsibility
- responsibly
- respond
- responsive
E: "Responsibility" is the noun. "Responsibly" is the adverb, "respond" is a different verb, and "responsive" is a different adjective (reacting quickly).

According to the text, which is part of being responsible?

Q: Read the text and answer.
"Being responsible means more than doing your homework. It means taking care of your health, helping your family at home, and keeping your promises. Responsible citizens also respect the law and protect the environment."
The main idea of the text is that responsibility:
+ includes many areas of life, not only school work

E: The first sentence says it "means more than doing your homework" and then lists health, family, promises, law and environment. So responsibility covers many areas of life.

Q: Choose the best option: "Students ____ not run in the corridors. It's dangerous."
+ must
- need
- have
- ought
E: "Must not" (mustn't) means it is forbidden. "Need not" means it is unnecessary. "Have not" and "ought not" without "to" are not correct here.

Q: "Needn't" in "You needn't bring food; lunch is provided" means:
+ It is not necessary to bring food.
- You are not allowed to bring food.
- You should bring food.
- You must bring food.
E: "Needn't" means there is no need; it is not necessary. The reason given (lunch is provided) supports this meaning.

Q: Which is the correct way to make a polite request for permission?
+ May I leave the class early today, please?
- I leave the class early today.
- Must I to leave early?
- I can leave early, OK?
E: "May I ...?" is a polite way to ask permission. "Must I to" is grammatically wrong (no "to" after must), and the other options are not polite requests.

Q: Which word is an antonym of "honest"?
+ dishonest
- truthful
- sincere
- frank
E: "Dishonest" means not honest. "Truthful", "sincere" and "frank" are similar in meaning to "honest".

Q: "If you make a promise, you ____ keep it." Which modal shows the speaker's strong personal opinion about duty?
+ must
- might
- may
- could
E: "Must" is used when the speaker feels something is very important or necessary. "Might", "may" and "could" only express possibility.

Q: Choose the correct form: "I enjoy ____ in the mountains."
+ hiking
- to hike
- hike
- hiked
E: Some verbs are followed by a gerund (-ing form), not an infinitive. "Enjoy" is one of them: enjoy hiking. Others are finish, avoid, mind, suggest, keep.

Q: Choose the correct form: "They decided ____ the Red Sea islands."
+ to visit
- visiting
- visit
- visited
E: "Decide" is followed by the to-infinitive: decided to visit. Other verbs like this are want, hope, plan, agree, refuse, promise.

Q: Choose the correct form: "____ is a good way to keep fit."
+ Swimming
- Swim
- Swam
- Swum
E: A gerund (-ing form) can be the subject of a sentence: Swimming is a good way to keep fit. The other forms cannot be used as a subject.

Q: Choose the correct form: "She is interested in ____ new places."
+ exploring
- to explore
- explore
- explored
E: After a preposition (in, on, at, of, about, without...) we use a gerund: interested in exploring.

Q: Choose the correct participle adjective: "The journey through the desert was ____."
+ exciting
- excited
- excite
- excitement
E: "-ing" adjectives describe the thing that causes the feeling (the journey caused excitement), and "-ed" adjectives describe how a person feels. So the journey was exciting; the travellers were excited.

Q: Choose the correct participle adjective: "The climbers felt ____ after the long climb."
+ exhausted
- exhausting
- exhaust
- exhaustion
E: The climbers are people who feel something, so we use the -ed adjective: exhausted (very tired). "Exhausting" would describe the climb itself.

Q: Which sentence uses a present participle phrase correctly?
+ Walking along the beach, we saw some beautiful shells.
- Walked along the beach, we saw some beautiful shells.
- Walk along the beach, we saw some beautiful shells.
- To walking along the beach, we saw some beautiful shells.
E: A present participle phrase (Walking along the beach) describes an action happening at the same time as the main verb, and its subject is the same as the main clause (we). The other forms are incorrect.

Q: Choose the correct form: "Remember ____ the door when you leave."
+ to lock
- locking
- lock
- locked
E: "Remember + to-infinitive" means remember to do something in the future (a duty). "Remember + -ing" means remember something you did in the past.

Q: "I stopped to buy some water." This means:
+ I stopped (what I was doing) in order to buy water.
- I no longer buy water.
- I never buy water.
- I bought water and then stopped buying it forever.
E: "Stop + to-infinitive" gives the purpose of stopping. "Stop + -ing" (I stopped buying water) means you no longer do that activity.

Q: Future continuous: "This time tomorrow, we ____ to Keren."
+ will be travelling
- will travel
- travelled
- have travelled
E: The future continuous (will be + -ing) describes an action that will be in progress at a certain time in the future: "this time tomorrow".

Q: Future continuous: "Don't call me at 8 p.m. I ____ dinner then."
+ will be having
- will have had
- had
- have
E: At 8 p.m. the action will be in progress, so we use the future continuous: will be having.

Q: Which sentence is in the future continuous tense?
+ At noon tomorrow, they will be climbing Emba Soira.
- They will climb Emba Soira tomorrow.
- They climbed Emba Soira yesterday.
- They have climbed Emba Soira.
E: The future continuous form is will + be + -ing (will be climbing). The other sentences are future simple, past simple and present perfect.

Q: An "adventure" is:
+ an unusual, exciting and sometimes dangerous experience
- a boring daily routine
- a type of food

E: An adventure is an exciting experience, often in a new or dangerous place, like exploring a cave or crossing a desert.

Q: Identify the noun clause: "I know that she writes beautiful poems."
+ that she writes beautiful poems
- I know
- beautiful poems
- she writes
E: A noun clause works like a noun. Here it is the object of "know": I know [something] = I know [that she writes beautiful poems]. A noun clause often begins with that, what, where, how, whether or if.

Q: Choose the correct option: "____ he said surprised everyone."
+ What
- That what
- Which
- Who
E: "What he said" is a noun clause used as the subject of the sentence. "What" means "the thing that". "Which" and "who" do not fit here, and "That what" is incorrect.

Q: Choose the correct word order: "Can you tell me where ____?"
+ the library is
- is the library
- the library is it
- is it the library
E: In an embedded question (a noun clause inside another question), we use statement word order: subject + verb. So "where the library is", not "where is the library".

Q: Choose the correct option: "I wonder ____ she will come to the party."
+ whether
- what
- that
- who
E: "Whether" (or "if") introduces a noun clause from a yes/no question: Will she come? → I wonder whether she will come.

Q: In "The fact that he lied made me angry", the noun clause is:
+ that he lied
- The fact
- made me angry
- me angry
E: "That he lied" explains "the fact". It is a noun clause in apposition to "the fact".

Q: What is the function of the noun clause in "Whoever finishes first will get a prize"?
+ subject of the sentence
- object of the verb
- adverb of time
- adjective
E: "Whoever finishes first" is the subject of the verb "will get". Ask "Who will get a prize?" and the answer is the noun clause.

Q: What is the function of the noun clause in "My hope is that I will become a writer"?
+ complement after the verb "be"
- subject
- object of a preposition
- adverb
E: After the linking verb "is", the noun clause completes the meaning of the subject "My hope". It is a subject complement.

Q: Choose the correct option: "She was worried about ____ she would say in her speech."
+ what
- that
- if that
- which that
E: After a preposition (about), a noun clause can begin with "what". "That" clauses cannot follow a preposition directly.
