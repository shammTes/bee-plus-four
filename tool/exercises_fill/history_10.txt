# Authored Grade 10 history top-ups (units 8-11; facts from the Grade 10 History textbook and standard history)
@ hist10-u8
Q: The French Wars of Religion (1562–1598) were fought mainly between:
+ French Catholics and French Protestants (Huguenots)
- France and the Ottoman Empire
- England and Spain
- Russia and Sweden
E: The French Wars of Religion were civil wars between Catholics and Calvinist Protestants, called Huguenots. Many nobles became Huguenots, which made the conflict political as well as religious.

Q: What did the Edict of Nantes (1598) do?
+ It gave the Huguenots the right to worship and political rights in France.
- It made Protestantism illegal in France.
- It ended the Thirty Years' War.
- It made England a republic.
E: King Henry IV issued the Edict of Nantes in 1598 to end the religious wars. It allowed Huguenots to worship and gave them political rights, while Catholicism stayed the official religion.

Q: In 1588 the Spanish Armada, a fleet of more than 200 warships sent by Philip II, tried to invade:
+ England
- France
- the Netherlands
- Portugal
E: Philip II of Spain sent the Armada against Protestant England under Queen Elizabeth I. It suffered a devastating defeat, which weakened Spain and strengthened England.

Q: The Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) began in the lands of the:
+ Holy Roman Empire (Germany)
- Ottoman Empire
- Kingdom of England
- Russian Empire
E: The war began in 1618 in the Holy Roman Empire as a religious conflict between Catholic and Protestant states. Later Denmark, Sweden, France and Spain joined, and it became a struggle for power in Europe.

Q: Which treaty officially ended the Thirty Years' War in 1648?
+ the Peace of Westphalia
- the Peace of Augsburg
- the Edict of Nantes
- the Treaty of Versailles
E: The Peace of Westphalia (1648) ended the war and gave territories to the main powers. After it, Germany was divided into more than 300 separate states. The Peace of Augsburg (1555) came much earlier.

Q: One reason religious disputes continued in Germany after the Peace of Augsburg (1555) was that:
+ Calvinism had not been recognised by the settlement.
- Catholicism had been banned.
- all German princes had become Muslims.
- the Pope had moved to Germany.
E: The Peace of Augsburg allowed each prince to choose Lutheranism or Catholicism for his lands, but it did not recognise Calvinism. This was one cause of later conflict, including the Thirty Years' War.

Q: Who led the Parliament's army to victory in the English Civil War and later ruled England until 1658?
+ Oliver Cromwell
- James II
- William of Orange
- Charles II
E: Oliver Cromwell led the Parliamentarian army. After Charles I was executed in 1649, England became a republic (Commonwealth), and Cromwell ruled until his death in 1658.

Q: In which year was the monarchy restored in England under Charles II?
+ 1660
- 1649
- 1688
- 1603
E: More than a year after Cromwell's death (1658), Parliament restored the monarchy in 1660, and Charles II became king. 1649 was the execution of Charles I, 1688 the Glorious Revolution, and 1603 the end of the Tudor dynasty.

Q: The English Bill of Rights (1689) was important because it:
+ limited the power of the monarch and protected the rights of Parliament
- gave the king absolute power
- made Catholicism the official religion
- abolished Parliament
E: After the Glorious Revolution, William and Mary accepted the Bill of Rights. It stated that the monarch could not suspend laws, raise taxes or keep an army in peacetime without Parliament's consent, making England a limited monarchy.

Q: Louis XIV built a magnificent palace to show his power and to keep the nobles under his control. Where was it?
+ Versailles
- Westminster
- St. Petersburg
- Escorial
E: Louis XIV's palace at Versailles, near Paris, became the centre of royal power. Nobles were expected to live at court, where the king could watch them.

Q: What did Louis XIV leave France with when he died in 1715?
+ great debts from his many wars
- a large surplus of money
- a new republican government
- peace with no wars fought during his reign
E: Louis XIV fought four major wars between 1667 and 1713. His ambitions led to European alliances against France, and when he died in 1715 France had great debts.

Q: Which two German states emerged as great powers after the Thirty Years' War?
+ Prussia and Austria
- Bavaria and Saxony
- Spain and Portugal
- Poland and Hungary
E: Out of more than 300 German states, Prussia and Austria became the most powerful in the 17th and 18th centuries. Frederick William, the Great Elector, laid the foundation of Prussian power with a large standing army.

Q: The English philosopher Thomas Hobbes argued in his book "Leviathan" (1651) that:
+ people need a strong ruler with absolute power to keep order
- people should overthrow every government
- government should have no power at all
- people are naturally peaceful and need no laws
E: Hobbes believed that without government, life would be "nasty, brutish and short". He argued that people give up their rights to a strong ruler in exchange for law and order. John Locke, in contrast, believed in natural rights and limited government.

Q: The ornate style of art and architecture of the 17th century, seen in grand churches and palaces, is called:
+ Baroque
- Gothic
- Romanesque
- Impressionism
E: Baroque art (about 1600–1750) used drama, movement and rich decoration to impress and inspire. Gothic and Romanesque are medieval styles, and Impressionism is a 19th-century style.

@ hist10-u9
Q: Who founded the Ottoman state at the end of the 13th century?
+ Osman
- Suleyman
- Mehmed II
- Babur
E: The Ottoman Empire takes its name from Osman, a Turkish leader in Anatolia (about 1300). Mehmed II conquered Constantinople (1453), Suleyman ruled at the height of the empire, and Babur founded the Mughal Empire in India.

Q: Constantinople became the Ottoman capital after 1453 and was renamed:
+ Istanbul
- Ankara
- Baghdad
- Damascus
E: After Mehmed II captured Constantinople in 1453, it became the Ottoman capital and came to be called Istanbul. Ankara is the capital of modern Turkey.

Q: The Ottoman sultan known in Europe as "the Magnificent" and in his empire as "the Lawgiver" was:
+ Suleyman I
- Osman I
- Selim I
- Akbar
E: Suleyman I (ruled 1520–1566) expanded the empire into Europe and the Middle East and reformed its laws, so he was called "Kanuni" (the Lawgiver). Akbar was a Mughal emperor.

Q: In 1529 the Ottoman army under Suleyman besieged, but failed to capture, which European city?
+ Vienna
- Rome
- Paris
- London
E: Suleyman's siege of Vienna in 1529 marked the furthest Ottoman advance into central Europe at that time. The city held out, and the Ottomans withdrew.

Q: The Ottoman system of taking Christian boys, converting them to Islam and training them as soldiers or officials was called:
+ devshirme
- jizya
- millet
- sharia
E: Under the devshirme system, boys from Christian families were trained to serve the sultan; the best became Janissaries or high officials. The millet system organised religious communities, jizya was a tax on non-Muslims, and sharia is Islamic law.

Q: The Ottoman system that allowed religious communities (such as Christians and Jews) to govern their own affairs was the:
+ millet system
- devshirme system
- feudal system
- caste system
E: In the millet system, each religious community had its own leaders who handled matters such as marriage, education and law for their people, under the sultan's authority.

Q: What was the official religion of the Safavid Empire?
+ Shi'a Islam
- Sunni Islam
- Christianity
- Hinduism
E: The Safavids, founded by Ismail in 1501, made Shi'a Islam the state religion of Persia (Iran). This set them apart from, and often in conflict with, the Sunni Ottomans.

Q: The Safavid capital, famous for its beautiful mosques built under Shah Abbas, was:
+ Isfahan
- Delhi
- Istanbul
- Cairo
E: Shah Abbas the Great (1588–1629) moved the capital to Isfahan and filled it with mosques, palaces and gardens. It became one of the most beautiful cities in the world.

Q: Which Mughal emperor followed a policy of religious tolerance and abolished the jizya tax on non-Muslims?
+ Akbar
- Aurangzeb
- Babur
- Shah Jahan
E: Akbar (ruled 1556–1605) treated Hindus and Muslims more equally and ended the jizya. His great-grandson Aurangzeb reversed many of these policies.

Q: Which Mughal emperor reintroduced the jizya and persecuted Hindus, causing revolts that weakened the empire?
+ Aurangzeb
- Akbar
- Babur
- Humayun
E: Aurangzeb (ruled 1658–1707) expanded the empire to its largest size, but his strict policies against Hindus and Sikhs caused rebellions. After his death the Mughal Empire declined.

Q: Babur defeated the Sultan of Delhi in 1526 at the first Battle of:
+ Panipat
- Plassey
- Lepanto
- Chaldiran
E: At Panipat in 1526, Babur used cannons and cavalry to defeat a much larger army and founded the Mughal Empire. Plassey (1757) was a British victory, Lepanto (1571) an Ottoman naval defeat, and Chaldiran (1514) an Ottoman victory over the Safavids.

Q: At the Battle of Chaldiran (1514), the Ottomans defeated:
+ the Safavids
- the Mughals
- the Byzantines
- the Spanish
E: Selim I defeated the Safavid ruler Ismail at Chaldiran in 1514. Ottoman firearms and artillery were decisive against the Safavid cavalry.

Q: All three Muslim empires (Ottoman, Safavid and Mughal) are sometimes called "gunpowder empires" because:
+ they used firearms and cannons to build and defend their empires
- they made and sold gunpowder to Europe only
- they banned all weapons
- they were destroyed by gunpowder explosions
E: The Ottomans, Safavids and Mughals used cannons and guns to conquer territory and control large populations, which helped them build powerful centralised states.

@ hist10-u10
Q: Who founded the Ming dynasty in 1368 after driving out the Mongols?
+ Hongwu (Zhu Yuanzhang)
- Kangxi
- Qianlong
- Kublai Khan
E: Zhu Yuanzhang, a former peasant, led the rebellion that expelled the Mongol Yuan dynasty and became the Hongwu emperor of the Ming. Kangxi and Qianlong were Qing emperors, and Kublai Khan was a Mongol ruler.

Q: The Ming emperor Yongle built a huge palace complex in Beijing known as:
+ the Forbidden City
- the Great Wall
- the Taj Mahal
- Versailles
E: The Forbidden City in Beijing was built under Yongle in the early 1400s. Ordinary people were not allowed to enter it, which is why it was called "forbidden".

Q: The Qing dynasty, which ruled China from 1644 to 1911, was founded by the:
+ Manchus
- Mongols
- Japanese
- Koreans
E: The Manchus came from Manchuria, north-east of China. They took Beijing in 1644 and founded the Qing, China's last imperial dynasty.

Q: Which Qing emperor ruled for 61 years (1661–1722) and was known as a capable and learned ruler?
+ Kangxi
- Hongwu
- Yongle
- Tokugawa Ieyasu
E: Kangxi expanded China's borders, encouraged learning and was tolerant of Jesuit scholars at his court. Tokugawa Ieyasu was a Japanese shogun.

Q: In 1793 the British envoy Lord Macartney visited China asking for more trade. What was the Qianlong emperor's response?
+ He refused, saying China had no need for British goods.
- He opened all Chinese ports to Britain.
- He made Britain a province of China.
- He declared war on Britain immediately.
E: Qianlong told King George III that China possessed all things and had no need for British manufactures. China kept trade limited to Canton (Guangzhou).

Q: In Tokugawa Japan, the real political power was held by the:
+ shogun
- emperor
- Dutch merchants
- Buddhist monks
E: Under the Tokugawa shogunate (1603–1867), the emperor in Kyoto was a symbolic figure, while the shogun in Edo (Tokyo) ruled the country.

Q: Under the Tokugawa "alternate attendance" system, daimyo (lords) had to:
+ spend every other year in Edo and leave their families there as hostages
- live permanently in China
- serve in the Dutch army
- give up all their land to farmers
E: This system kept the daimyo under the shogun's control and made them spend a lot of money travelling and keeping two homes, which reduced their ability to rebel.

Q: During Japan's period of isolation, which Europeans were the only ones allowed to trade, at Nagasaki?
+ the Dutch
- the Portuguese
- the Spanish
- the British
E: After the 1630s, Japan expelled the Portuguese and Spanish and banned Christianity. Only Dutch traders (and Chinese) were allowed to trade, on the small island of Dejima at Nagasaki.

Q: In the Tokugawa social order, which group was at the top below the shogun and daimyo?
+ samurai warriors
- merchants
- artisans
- peasants
E: The social order was samurai, then peasants, then artisans, then merchants at the bottom. In practice, many merchants became rich during the long Tokugawa peace.

Q: The dynasty that ruled Korea from 1392 to 1910 was the:
+ Choson (Joseon) dynasty
- Ming dynasty
- Tokugawa dynasty
- Qing dynasty
E: The Choson (Joseon) dynasty ruled Korea for over 500 years. King Sejong of this dynasty created the Korean alphabet, Hangul, in the 1440s.

Q: In the 1590s, Japan under Toyotomi Hideyoshi invaded which country?
+ Korea
- the Philippines
- Vietnam
- India
E: Hideyoshi invaded Korea in 1592 and 1597, hoping to conquer China. Korean resistance, including Admiral Yi Sun-sin's "turtle ships", and Chinese help forced the Japanese to withdraw.

Q: The Ming admiral Zheng He's voyages (1405–1433) reached as far as:
+ the east coast of Africa
- the Americas
- Australia
- northern Europe
E: Zheng He's treasure fleets sailed through South-East Asia and the Indian Ocean to Arabia and the east coast of Africa (for example Malindi). After 1433 the Ming stopped these voyages.

@ hist10-u11
Q: Who used a telescope to observe the moons of Jupiter and supported the idea that the Earth moves around the Sun?
+ Galileo Galilei
- Ptolemy
- Aristotle
- Thomas Hobbes
E: In 1610 Galileo observed four moons orbiting Jupiter, evidence that not everything orbits the Earth. Ptolemy and Aristotle supported the geocentric model, and Hobbes was a political philosopher.

Q: Isaac Newton's "Principia" (1687) explained the motion of planets and objects on Earth using:
+ the law of universal gravitation and the laws of motion
- the geocentric theory
- the theory of evolution
- the laws of electricity
E: Newton showed that the same force, gravity, keeps the planets in orbit and makes objects fall to Earth. His three laws of motion became the basis of classical physics.

Q: The English philosopher who promoted the scientific method based on experiments and inductive reasoning was:
+ Francis Bacon
- René Descartes
- Voltaire
- Rousseau
E: Francis Bacon argued that scientists should gather facts through experiments and observation, then draw general conclusions (induction). Descartes stressed reason and mathematics ("I think, therefore I am").

Q: "I think, therefore I am" is a famous statement by:
+ René Descartes
- John Locke
- Isaac Newton
- Adam Smith
E: René Descartes, a French philosopher and mathematician, began by doubting everything and concluded that his own thinking proved he existed. He emphasised reason as the path to knowledge.

Q: Who discovered how blood circulates around the body, pumped by the heart?
+ William Harvey
- Andreas Vesalius
- Galileo Galilei
- Johannes Kepler
E: William Harvey published his work on the circulation of the blood in 1628. Vesalius wrote an important book on human anatomy (1543), and Kepler studied planetary orbits.

Q: In "The Social Contract" (1762), Jean-Jacques Rousseau argued that:
+ legitimate government depends on the general will of the people
- kings rule by divine right
- the economy should be controlled completely by the king
- people should never take part in politics
E: Rousseau believed that people agree to form a society and that government must follow the "general will". His ideas influenced the French Revolution.

Q: Which Enlightenment writer is known for defending freedom of speech and religious tolerance, and for criticising the Church?
+ Voltaire
- Thomas Hobbes
- Louis XIV
- Peter the Great
E: Voltaire (François-Marie Arouet) used satire to attack intolerance and injustice. He strongly supported freedom of thought and religion.

Q: The Scottish economist who wrote "The Wealth of Nations" (1776) and supported free markets was:
+ Adam Smith
- John Locke
- Montesquieu
- Diderot
E: Adam Smith argued that the "invisible hand" of competition and self-interest leads to economic growth, so governments should interfere little in the economy (laissez-faire).

Q: Montesquieu's idea of the "separation of powers" divides government into:
+ legislative, executive and judicial branches
- king, church and army
- rich, middle class and poor
- national, regional and local councils only
E: In "The Spirit of the Laws" (1748), Montesquieu argued that dividing power between law-makers, those who carry out laws, and the courts prevents tyranny. This idea influenced the US Constitution.

Q: Which ruler of Austria was an "enlightened despot" who abolished serfdom and allowed religious toleration in the 1780s?
+ Joseph II
- Louis XIV
- Philip II
- Charles I
E: Joseph II of Austria tried to rule according to Enlightenment ideas: he granted religious toleration and abolished serfdom, although many reforms were cancelled after his death.

Q: Which Russian ruler corresponded with Enlightenment thinkers and is considered an enlightened despot?
+ Catherine the Great
- Ivan the Terrible
- Nicholas II
- Peter III only
E: Catherine the Great (ruled 1762–1796) admired the philosophes and corresponded with Voltaire and Diderot. However, she did little to end serfdom and strengthened the nobles' power.

Q: Before the Scientific Revolution, most Europeans based their ideas about nature on:
+ ancient authorities such as Aristotle and Ptolemy, and Church teaching
- experiments in laboratories
- observations made with telescopes
- Newton's laws
E: In the Middle Ages, people accepted ideas from ancient Greek authorities and the Church. The Scientific Revolution replaced this with observation, experiment and mathematics.

+ Selim I
- Suleyman I
- Osman I

+ Sinan
- Akbar

Q: Who was the Japanese leader who completed the unification of Japan and founded a shogunate in 1603 with its capital at Edo?
+ Tokugawa Ieyasu
- Oda Nobunaga
- Toyotomi Hideyoshi
- Kangxi
E: Tokugawa Ieyasu won the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600 and became shogun in 1603. The Tokugawa family ruled Japan from Edo (Tokyo) until 1867. Nobunaga and Hideyoshi were the earlier unifiers.

Q: Why did the Tokugawa shoguns ban Christianity and expel most Europeans in the 1630s?
+ They feared that Christianity and foreign influence threatened their control of Japan.
- They wanted to make Japan a Christian country.
- Europeans refused to trade with Japan.
- The emperor had become Christian.
E: The shoguns believed Christian converts might be loyal to foreign powers. After the Shimabara rebellion (1637–1638), Japan closed itself to most foreign contact.

Q: Japanese warriors followed a code of honour and loyalty known as:
+ bushido
- Confucianism
- devshirme
- Hangul
E: Bushido ("the way of the warrior") stressed loyalty to one's lord, courage and honour. Confucianism is a Chinese philosophy, devshirme an Ottoman system, and Hangul the Korean alphabet.

Q: Which philosophy, stressing respect for parents, rulers and social order, was the official ideology of Ming and Qing China and Choson Korea?
+ Confucianism
- Christianity
- Shi'a Islam
- Hinduism
E: Confucianism taught that society works best when people respect family and rulers and behave correctly in their relationships. Government officials were chosen through civil service examinations based on Confucian texts.

Q: How were officials chosen for government service in Ming and Qing China?
+ through civil service examinations based on Confucian classics
- by buying their positions from Europeans
- by winning military battles only
- by election by all citizens
E: Candidates studied the Confucian classics for years and took difficult examinations. Those who passed could become scholar-officials, the most respected group in Chinese society.

@ hist10-u11
Q: The German astronomer who discovered that planets move in elliptical orbits was:
+ Johannes Kepler
- Nicolaus Copernicus
- Galileo Galilei
- Isaac Newton
E: Kepler (early 1600s) used the observations of Tycho Brahe to show that planets travel in ellipses, not perfect circles. Copernicus proposed the sun-centred model, and Newton later explained the orbits with gravity.

Q: Why did the Catholic Church oppose the heliocentric (sun-centred) theory?
+ It seemed to contradict Church teaching that the Earth was the centre of creation.
- It was proposed by a Muslim scholar.
- It was proved false by experiments.
- The Church had no opinion on science.
E: The Church supported the geocentric view of Ptolemy and Aristotle, which fitted its interpretation of scripture. Galileo was tried in 1633 and forced to withdraw his support for heliocentrism.

Q: The French writers and thinkers of the Enlightenment, such as Voltaire and Diderot, were called:
+ philosophes
- samurai
- janissaries
- Huguenots
E: The philosophes believed that reason could be used to improve society, government and education. Diderot edited the Encyclopedia to spread their ideas.

Q: Which invention, developed by the Dutch in the late 1500s and early 1600s, allowed scientists to see tiny living things such as bacteria?
+ the microscope
- the telescope
- the printing press
- the compass
E: The microscope was developed in the Netherlands. Antonie van Leeuwenhoek later used it to observe bacteria and other micro-organisms in the 1670s. The telescope is for distant objects.
