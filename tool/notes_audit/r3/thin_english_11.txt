@lesson eng11-u2-l2-2
TITLE: Tricky cases of subject–verb agreement
S: Find the real subject (the head noun) and ignore phrases between it and the verb: "The box of pens IS on the desk." | Words joined by "and" are plural: "Ali and Sara ARE here." – but "each/every + noun and noun" is singular. | "Either…or / neither…nor / or / not only…but also": the verb agrees with the nearer subject: "Neither the teacher nor the students WERE late." | Indefinite pronouns everyone, somebody, nobody, each, either, neither take a singular verb; both, few, many, several take a plural verb. | Collective nouns (team, family, government) are usually singular when acting as one unit; amounts of money, time and distance are singular: "Ten kilometres IS a long walk."
E: "The quality of the mangoes (is/are) good." Head noun = quality (singular) → "is". "Neither the coach nor the players (was/were) ready." Nearer subject = players → "were".
M: "Cross out, then count" — cross out the phrase after the head noun, then count the subject that is left (one = -s verb).
T: In exam sentences, the noun just before the verb is often a trap ("of the students"). Always trace the verb back to the word that does the action.

@lesson eng11-u3-l3-1
TITLE: Core modals
S: Modals (can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, will, would) are followed by the bare infinitive: "She must go" (not "must to go" or "must goes"). | Choose by meaning: ability (can, could), permission (can, may, could), possibility (may, might, could), obligation (must, have to), advice (should, ought to), prohibition (mustn't). | "Mustn't" = it is forbidden; "don't have to / needn't" = it is not necessary. | Past forms: could (past ability), had to (past obligation); speculation about the past uses modal + have + V3: "He must have left." | Modals never take -s and form questions/negatives without "do": "Can you swim?", "You should not shout."
E: "You ___ wear a uniform; it's optional." → "don't have to" (no obligation). "You ___ smoke in the laboratory." → "mustn't" (prohibition). "She ___ have taken the bus; her car is here." → "must" (logical certainty about the past).
M: "A-P-P-O-A" — Ability, Permission, Possibility, Obligation, Advice: name the meaning first, then pick the modal.
T: The favourite exam trap is mustn't vs don't have to. Ask: is it forbidden (mustn't) or just unnecessary (don't have to)?

@lesson eng11-u4-l4-1
TITLE: Forming the passive
S: Take the object of the active sentence and make it the subject: "Workers built the bridge" → "The bridge…". | Put "be" in the same tense as the active verb (built = past simple → was/were). | Add the past participle (V3) of the main verb: "The bridge was built". | Add "by + agent" only if the doer is important or new information: "…by Italian engineers." | Only transitive verbs (verbs with an object) can become passive: "arrive, happen, die" have no passive.
E: Active: "The government opened a new school in Keren." → Passive: "A new school was opened in Keren (by the government)." Present: "They grow coffee here." → "Coffee is grown here."
M: "O-B-3" — Object first, Be in the same tense, V3 last.
T: Check the tense of "be" against the active verb – most mistakes are tense mismatches ("is built" for a past action).

@lesson eng11-u4-l4-2
TITLE: Progressive and perfect passives
S: Progressive passive = be (am/is/are/was/were) + being + V3: "The road is being repaired now." | Perfect passive = have/has/had + been + V3: "The letters have been sent." | Future and modal passives = will/can/must + be + V3: "The results will be announced tomorrow." | Find the tense of the active verb first, then keep the same tense in the passive auxiliary. | Never drop "be/been/being": "The letter sent" is wrong; "The letter was sent" is right.
E: Active: "They are building a new hospital." → "A new hospital is being built." Active: "Someone has stolen my bag." → "My bag has been stolen." Active: "They had finished the work." → "The work had been finished."
M: "-ing tense → BEING, perfect tense → BEEN" — the passive marker copies the active tense.
T: Write the active tense name beside the sentence (present continuous, present perfect…) before converting; then add being or been accordingly.

@lesson eng11-u5-l5-1
TITLE: Backshift in reported speech
S: When the reporting verb is past (said, told, asked), move each tense one step back. | present simple → past simple; present continuous → past continuous; present perfect / past simple → past perfect; will → would; can → could; may → might. | Change pronouns to fit the new speaker: "I" → he/she, "my" → his/her. | Change time and place words: now → then, today → that day, tomorrow → the next day, yesterday → the day before, here → there. | Questions become statement order with if/whether or the wh-word; commands become told/asked + (not) to + verb.
E: Direct: Saba said, "I am studying for my exam tomorrow." → Reported: Saba said (that) she was studying for her exam the next day. Direct: He asked, "Where do you live?" → He asked where I lived.
M: "Tense back, Person shift, Time and place change" — T-P-T.
T: Do all three changes in order: tense, then pronoun, then time/place word. Examiners mark each change separately.

@lesson eng11-u6-l6-1
TITLE: Conditional types 0–3
S: Type 0 (always true facts): If + present simple, present simple – "If you heat ice, it melts." | Type 1 (real/possible future): If + present simple, will + verb – "If it rains, we will stay home." | Type 2 (unreal or unlikely present/future): If + past simple, would + verb – "If I had wings, I would fly." (use "were" for all persons: If I were you…). | Type 3 (unreal past, regret): If + had + V3, would have + V3 – "If he had studied, he would have passed." | Never put "will" or "would" in the if-clause; "unless" = if not.
E: "If she ___ (know) your number, she would call you." → knew (Type 2). "If they had left earlier, they ___ (catch) the bus." → would have caught (Type 3).
M: "0 = fact, 1 = likely, 2 = dream, 3 = regret" — and the verb in the if-clause goes one step back each time (present → past → past perfect).
T: Identify the type from the tense already given in one clause, then build the other clause to match.

@lesson eng11-u7-l7-1
TITLE: Verb patterns
S: Some verbs are followed by -ing (gerund): enjoy, avoid, finish, mind, suggest, consider, keep, practise, deny, admit, imagine, risk. | Some verbs are followed by to-infinitive: want, decide, hope, plan, agree, promise, refuse, offer, expect, manage, learn. | Some take object + to-infinitive: ask, tell, advise, allow, encourage, warn, want + someone + to do. | Some change meaning: stop doing (quit) vs stop to do (stop in order to); remember doing (memory of the past) vs remember to do (not forget a duty); try doing (experiment) vs try to do (make an effort). | "Make" and "let" take object + bare infinitive: "She made me wait."
E: "He avoided ___ (answer) the question." → answering. "They decided ___ (build) a school." → to build. "I stopped ___ (buy) bread on the way home." → to buy (stopped in order to buy).
M: "Enjoy-Avoid-Finish = -ING" (E.A.F.) and "Want-Decide-Hope = TO" (W.D.H.).
T: For stop/remember/try, decide the time order: if the action happened before → -ing; if it comes after (a purpose or duty) → to.

@lesson eng11-u7-l7-2
TITLE: Adjective + infinitive; preposition + -ing
S: Many adjectives describing feelings or judgement take to-infinitive: happy to help, easy to learn, difficult to understand, ready to go, sorry to hear. | Pattern "It is + adjective + (for someone) + to-infinitive": "It is important for students to read daily." | After any preposition (in, on, at, of, about, for, without, before, after) the verb takes -ing: "interested in studying", "good at drawing", "without saying". | Some phrases end in the preposition "to", so they also take -ing: look forward to meeting, be used to working, object to paying. | "Too + adjective + to" and "adjective + enough + to": "too tired to walk", "old enough to vote".
E: "She is afraid ___ (fly)." → of flying (preposition). "This question is easy ___ (answer)." → to answer. "I look forward to ___ (hear) from you." → hearing.
M: "Preposition? Put -ING in position."
T: In letter endings the classic error is "look forward to hear". The "to" here is a preposition, so always write "look forward to hearing".

@lesson eng11-u8-l8-1
TITLE: Linking words of contrast and purpose
S: Although / even though / though + subject + verb: "Although it rained, we played." | Despite / in spite of + noun or -ing: "Despite the rain, we played." / "In spite of being tired, she worked." | However / nevertheless start a new sentence or follow a semicolon and take a comma: "It rained; however, we played." | Whereas / while compare two contrasting facts: "Asmara is cool, whereas Massawa is hot." | Purpose: to / in order to / so as to + verb; so that + subject + can/could/will/would + verb; "in order not to" for negative purpose.
E: Join: "He was ill. He went to school." → "Although he was ill, he went to school." / "Despite being ill, he went to school." / "He was ill; however, he went to school." Purpose: "She saved money so that she could buy a bicycle."
M: "Although has a clause, Despite has a noun" — A-C, D-N.
T: Never write "despite of" or "although…but". Use one contrast word per sentence.
