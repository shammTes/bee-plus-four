@lesson chem11-u3-l3-4
TITLE: Sulphur and its compounds
S: Sulphur is a yellow, brittle non-metal, insoluble in water but soluble in carbon disulphide; it has allotropes (rhombic, stable below 96 °C, and monoclinic). | It burns with a blue flame to form sulphur dioxide: S + O₂ → SO₂. | In the laboratory SO₂ is made from sodium sulphite and dilute HCl: Na₂SO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + SO₂; it is a dense gas collected by downward delivery. SO₂ is acidic and a bleaching/reducing agent. | Contact process: (1) burn S → SO₂; (2) 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ over V₂O₅ catalyst at about 450 °C and 1–2 atm; (3) absorb SO₃ in concentrated H₂SO₄ to form oleum H₂S₂O₇; (4) dilute: H₂S₂O₇ + H₂O → 2H₂SO₄. | Concentrated H₂SO₄ is a dehydrating agent (chars sugar), an oxidising agent and a strong acid when dilute; uses include fertilizers, batteries and detergents.
E: Why not dissolve SO₃ directly in water? The reaction is so exothermic that it forms a fine acid mist that is hard to collect, so SO₃ is absorbed in concentrated H₂SO₄ first, then diluted.
M: "Burn, Catalyse, Absorb, Dilute" — B-C-A-D for the Contact process (catalyst V₂O₅ at about 450 °C).
T: Always dilute concentrated sulphuric acid by adding the ACID slowly to WATER, never water to acid – the heat released can boil and splash.

@lesson chem11-u4-l4-3
TITLE: Aluminium
S: Occurrence: most abundant metal in the Earth's crust (about 8 %); main ore bauxite Al₂O₃·2H₂O. | Purification (Bayer process): bauxite is dissolved in hot NaOH, impurities (Fe₂O₃) are filtered off, Al(OH)₃ is precipitated and heated to pure Al₂O₃ (alumina). | Extraction (Hall–Héroult): alumina is dissolved in molten cryolite (Na₃AlF₆) to lower the melting point from about 2050 °C to about 950 °C, then electrolysed with carbon electrodes. | Cathode: Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al; anode: 2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻, and the oxygen burns the carbon anodes (so they are replaced regularly). | Properties and uses: light, good conductor, resists corrosion because of a thin protective Al₂O₃ layer; amphoteric oxide. Used in power cables, aircraft, cans and window frames.
E: Mass of Al from 102 kg of Al₂O₃: 2Al₂O₃ → 4Al + 3O₂. M(Al₂O₃) = 102 g/mol, so 102 kg = 1000 mol Al₂O₃ → 2000 mol Al × 27 g/mol = 54 kg of aluminium.
M: "Bayer Purifies, Hall Electrolyses, Cryolite Cools" — B-P, H-E, C-C.
T: Explain why aluminium is extracted by electrolysis, not carbon: it is above carbon in the reactivity series, so carbon cannot reduce Al₂O₃.

@lesson chem11-u4-l4-6
TITLE: Zinc
S: Ores: zinc blende (ZnS, main ore), calamine (ZnCO₃) and zincite (ZnO); zinc is also produced at Eritrea's Bisha mine. | Concentrate the ore by froth flotation, then roast in air: 2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂ (the SO₂ can be used to make sulphuric acid). | Reduce the oxide with carbon (coke) in a furnace: ZnO + C → Zn + CO; zinc vapour is condensed – or dissolve ZnO in H₂SO₄ and electrolyse the ZnSO₄ solution (purer zinc). | Properties: bluish-white metal, fairly reactive, reacts with dilute acids to give H₂; ZnO and Zn(OH)₂ are amphoteric. | Uses: galvanizing iron (sacrificial protection), brass (Cu + Zn), dry-cell cases, ZnO in paints and creams.
E: Galvanized iron sheets on Eritrean roofs: even when scratched, zinc corrodes first because it is more reactive than iron, so the iron underneath stays protected (sacrificial protection).
M: "Float, Roast, Reduce" — F-R-R turns zinc blende into zinc.
T: For any sulphide ore remember: first roast to the oxide (releasing SO₂), then reduce the oxide. Write both equations.

@lesson chem11-u4-l4-7
TITLE: Gold
S: Gold is very unreactive (at the bottom of the reactivity series), so it occurs native as free metal in quartz veins and river sands; Eritrea has gold mining at Bisha and Zara and an ancient tradition of panning. | Concentration: crushed ore or sand is washed (panning) because gold is very dense (19.3 g/cm³). | Cyanide process: gold dissolves in sodium cyanide solution in the presence of air, forming the soluble complex [Au(CN)₂]⁻. | Recovery: zinc (or activated carbon then electrolysis) displaces gold from the solution: 2[Au(CN)₂]⁻ + Zn → [Zn(CN)₄]²⁻ + 2Au. | Properties and uses: shiny, does not tarnish, most malleable and ductile metal, good conductor; used in jewellery, electronics, dentistry and as a reserve of wealth. Purity is measured in carats (24 carat = pure).
E: An 18-carat ring contains 18/24 = 75 % gold; the other 25 % is usually copper and silver to make it harder.
M: "Native, Dense, Cyanide, Zinc" — N-D-C-Z: occurs free, washed by density, dissolved by cyanide, recovered by zinc.
T: Gold dissolves in aqua regia (3 HCl : 1 HNO₃) but not in a single ordinary acid – a common exam question.

@lesson chem11-u4-l4-8
TITLE: Alloys
S: An alloy is a mixture of a metal with one or more other elements (usually metals, sometimes carbon), made by melting them together. | Atoms of different sizes disturb the regular layers of the metal lattice, so the layers cannot slide easily – the alloy is harder and stronger than the pure metal. | Alloys may also resist corrosion better or have a lower melting point. | Common alloys: brass (Cu + Zn), bronze (Cu + Sn), steel (Fe + C), stainless steel (Fe + Cr + Ni), solder (Sn + Pb), duralumin (Al + Cu + Mg). | Choose the alloy by the property needed: strength, corrosion resistance, low melting point or appearance.
E: Pure aluminium is soft; duralumin (about 95 % Al with Cu, Mg, Mn) stays light but is much stronger, so it is used for aircraft bodies.
M: "Brass has Zinc, Bronze has Tin" — Bra-Z, Bro-T.
T: In "why are alloys harder?" questions draw two diagrams: neat rows of identical atoms (layers slide) vs rows disturbed by bigger/smaller atoms (layers cannot slide).

@lesson chem11-u5-l5-1
TITLE: The gaseous state
S: Gas particles are far apart and move fast and randomly, so gases have no fixed shape or volume, are easily compressed and fill their container. | A gas sample is described by four variables: pressure P (Pa, kPa, atm, mmHg), volume V (L, m³), temperature T (kelvin) and amount n (mol). | Convert temperature: T(K) = t(°C) + 273. Standard temperature and pressure (STP): 273 K and 1 atm (101.3 kPa); 1 mol of ideal gas occupies 22.4 L at STP. | Pressure units: 1 atm = 760 mmHg = 101.3 kPa. | Gases diffuse: lighter gases diffuse faster (Graham's law: rate ∝ 1/√M).
E: How many moles are in 11.2 L of O₂ at STP? n = 11.2 L ÷ 22.4 L/mol = 0.50 mol, mass = 0.50 × 32 = 16 g.
M: "P-V-T-n: Please Verify Temperature Now" — and Temperature is always in kelvin.
T: Write the units next to every number before substituting; most errors come from °C instead of K or mixing kPa with atm.

@lesson chem11-u5-l5-3
TITLE: Kinetic molecular theory and the gas laws
S: Postulates: gas particles are tiny compared with the distances between them; they move randomly in straight lines; collisions are perfectly elastic; there are no attractive forces; average kinetic energy ∝ absolute temperature. | Boyle's law (T, n constant): P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ – squeezing particles into less space makes more collisions with the walls. | Charles's law (P, n constant): V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ – hotter particles move faster and push the walls out. | Combined and ideal gas laws: P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ and PV = nRT (R = 0.0821 L·atm/(mol·K) = 8.314 J/(mol·K)). | Real gases deviate at high pressure and low temperature, because particles then have volume and attract each other.
E: A gas occupies 2.0 L at 27 °C. Volume at 127 °C (same P)? T₁ = 300 K, T₂ = 400 K. V₂ = V₁ × T₂/T₁ = 2.0 × 400/300 = 2.67 L.
M: "Boyle Pushes, Charles Heats" — Boyle: P×V constant; Charles: V/T constant.
T: Solve gas-law problems in four lines: list the data with units, convert °C to K, write the law, substitute and give the answer with its unit.
