@lesson geo9-u1-l1-2
TITLE: Fields of modern geography
S: Start from the definition: geography studies the Earth's surface – where things are and why they are there. | Split it into two big branches. Physical geography studies natural features: landforms (geomorphology), climate (climatology), soils, water (hydrology), plants and animals (biogeography). | Human geography studies people and their activities: population, settlement, economic activities (agriculture, industry, trade), political and cultural geography. | Add the tools that serve both branches: maps (cartography), surveys, statistics, aerial photographs, remote sensing and GIS. | Finally, regional geography combines physical and human facts to describe one area as a whole.
E: A study of why farmers in the Eritrean highlands terrace their slopes uses physical geography (slope, rainfall, soil erosion) and human geography (population pressure, farming methods) together – that combination is what makes modern geography unique.
M: P-H-R-T — Physical, Human, Regional, Tools. Name the branch, give one sub-field and one example for each.
T: When a question asks you to classify a topic, ask "is it made by nature or by people?" Nature → physical geography; people → human geography; if the topic needs both for one place → regional geography.

@lesson geo9-u2-l2-2
TITLE: Rotation of the Earth and its effects
S: Rotation is the spinning of the Earth on its own axis, from west to east (anticlockwise seen from above the North Pole). | One complete rotation takes about 24 hours (23 h 56 min against the stars), which gives one day. | Effect 1 – day and night: the half facing the Sun has day, the other half night; the circle of illumination separates them. | Effect 2 – time differences: 360° ÷ 24 h = 15° of longitude per hour, so places east of you are ahead in time. | Effect 3 – deflection of winds and ocean currents (Coriolis effect): to the right in the Northern Hemisphere, to the left in the Southern. | Effect 4 – the slight flattening of the poles and bulging at the equator, and the daily rise and fall of tides.
E: Asmara is at about 39°E and Greenwich at 0°. Difference = 39° ÷ 15° per hour ≈ 2.6 h, so local solar time in Asmara is about 2 h 36 min ahead of Greenwich (official Eritrean time is GMT+3).
M: D-T-D-F — Day and night, Time difference, Deflection, Flattening: the four effects of rotation.
T: Do not mix rotation with revolution. Rotation (24 h, on the axis) → day/night and time. Revolution (365¼ days, around the Sun) → seasons and leap years.

@lesson geo9-u3-l3-2
TITLE: Weather and climate
S: Weather is the condition of the atmosphere at a place at a particular time (hours or days). | Climate is the average weather of a place over a long period, usually at least 30 years. | Both are described by the same elements: temperature, rainfall (precipitation), humidity, air pressure, wind, cloud and sunshine. | Weather is studied by meteorology and recorded at weather stations; climate is studied by climatology using long records of those observations. | So the link is: climate is built from many years of weather records.
E: "It is raining heavily in Asmara this afternoon" describes weather. "Asmara gets most of its rain in July and August and has mild temperatures all year" describes climate.
M: Weather = Today, Climate = Thirty years — "W-T, C-30".
T: Look for time words in the question. "Today, this week, yesterday" → weather. "Usually, average, every year, for many years" → climate.

@lesson geo9-u3-l3-4
TITLE: Air temperature
S: The Sun heats the ground first (radiation); the ground then heats the air touching it (conduction), and warm air rises carrying heat upward (convection). | Temperature is measured with a thermometer kept in a Stevenson screen, about 1.2 m above the ground, in the shade. | Daily mean = (maximum + minimum) ÷ 2; daily range = maximum − minimum. | Main controls: latitude (angle of the Sun), altitude (about 6.5 °C cooler per 1000 m), distance from the sea, ocean currents, aspect and cloud cover. | Places of equal temperature are joined on maps by isotherms.
E: Massawa (sea level) has a mean of about 30 °C. Asmara is about 2350 m higher: 2.35 × 6.5 ≈ 15 °C cooler, so about 15–17 °C – which matches Asmara's mild climate.
M: L-A-D-O-C — Latitude, Altitude, Distance from sea, Ocean currents, Cloud: the controls of temperature.
T: In calculations always show the formula first: mean = (max + min) ÷ 2, range = max − min, cooling = height (km) × 6.5 °C. Write the unit °C with every answer.

@lesson geo9-u3-l3-5
TITLE: Air pressure
S: Air pressure is the weight of the column of air pressing on a unit area of the Earth's surface. | It is measured with a barometer (mercury or aneroid) in millibars (mb); average sea-level pressure is about 1013 mb. | Pressure falls with height, because there is less air above you. | Heated air expands, becomes lighter and rises → low pressure; cooled air contracts, becomes heavier and sinks → high pressure. | Lines joining places of equal pressure are isobars; air always moves from high to low pressure, and that movement is wind.
E: On a hot afternoon the land near Massawa heats quickly, air rises and pressure falls; the cooler Red Sea keeps higher pressure, so a sea breeze blows from the sea onto the land.
M: Hot air goes Higher → Low pressure; Cold air Collapses → High pressure ("hot-high-low, cold-down-high").
T: For any wind question, first locate the high and the low – wind blows from high to low, and the closer the isobars, the stronger the wind.

@lesson geo9-u3-l3-6
TITLE: Winds and wind belts
S: Wind is air moving horizontally from high to low pressure; it is named after the direction it comes from (a north wind blows from the north). | Wind direction is shown by a wind vane; wind speed is measured with an anemometer. | The Earth has pressure belts: equatorial low (doldrums), subtropical highs (about 30°N/S), subpolar lows (about 60°) and polar highs. | Planetary winds blow between them: trade winds (30° → equator), westerlies (30° → 60°) and polar easterlies (poles → 60°). | Rotation deflects them (Coriolis effect), so the north-east trades blow in the Northern Hemisphere and the south-east trades in the Southern.
E: In Eritrea's lowlands in winter the north-east trade winds blow from the Arabian side towards the Red Sea coast and bring the coastal winter rains around Massawa.
M: "Trade, West, East" from the equator to the pole — TWE: Trades, Westerlies, Easterlies.
T: When naming a wind, always say where it comes from, not where it goes. A wind blowing from the sea is an onshore (sea) wind.

@lesson geo9-u3-l3-7
TITLE: Precipitation
S: Water evaporates and the air holds it as water vapour; humidity is measured with a hygrometer. | When moist air rises it cools; at the dew point it becomes saturated and condensation forms clouds. | When cloud droplets join and become too heavy, they fall as precipitation: rain, snow, sleet or hail. | Three ways air is forced to rise give three types of rain: convectional (heating of the ground), relief or orographic (air forced over mountains) and frontal or cyclonic (warm air rising over cold air). | Rainfall is measured with a rain gauge in millimetres; places of equal rainfall are joined by isohyets.
E: Moist summer winds from the south-west are forced up the Eritrean escarpment and highlands; they cool, condense and give relief rain in July–August, while the leeward side stays drier (rain shadow).
M: C-R-F — Convection, Relief, Front: three ways to lift air, three types of rain.
T: In a rain-type question, find the cause of the rising air: heat → convectional; a mountain → relief; meeting air masses → frontal.

@lesson geo9-u4-l4-2
TITLE: The concept of a region
S: A region is an area of the Earth's surface that shares one or more common characteristics which make it different from surrounding areas. | The characteristic can be physical (climate, relief, vegetation) or human (language, economy, administration). | Every region has a location, an extent (area) and boundaries; the boundaries may be sharp (a political border) or transitional (a gradual change in climate). | Geographers divide the world into regions to simplify and compare places. | The size depends on the purpose: a small region like a zoba or a large one like the Sahel.
E: The Eritrean coastal lowland is a region because places in it share low altitude, hot temperature and low rainfall, which make it different from the cool highland region.
M: L-E-B-C — Location, Extent, Boundary, Common characteristic: the four things that make an area a region.
T: When asked to define a region, always name the shared characteristic (e.g. "same climate"), otherwise the definition is incomplete.

@lesson geo9-u4-l4-3
TITLE: Common characteristics of regions
S: Regions have location: they can be placed on a map using latitude, longitude or relative position. | Regions have area or extent: they cover a measurable space. | Regions have boundaries, which may be clear lines or broad transition zones. | Regions have homogeneity: inside the region places are similar in the chosen criterion (climate, language, farming). | Functional regions instead share interaction (spatial interaction) with a centre, such as trade, transport or services.
E: A climate region such as the tropical desert is defined by homogeneity (all hot and dry), while the area served by the port of Massawa is defined by interaction with the port.
M: "Same stuff, same job" — homogeneity makes formal regions; interaction makes functional regions.
T: If a question mentions flows, movement, a centre or services, think functional region; if it mentions one shared feature, think formal region.

@lesson geo9-u4-l4-4
TITLE: Types of regions
S: Formal (uniform) regions: every place shares the same feature – e.g. a climate region, a language region, a cotton-growing region. | Functional (nodal) regions: areas linked to a central place (node) by movement – e.g. the market area of a town, a bus route network, a port's hinterland. | Vernacular (perceptual) regions: areas that people believe exist, based on feeling or culture – e.g. "the Horn of Africa", "the Middle East". | Regions can also be grouped by criterion: physical (natural) regions and human (cultural, economic, administrative) regions. | Functional regions change when patterns of interaction change, so new ones appear and old ones disappear.
E: Eritrea's six zobas are administrative (formal) regions; the area whose people shop in Asmara's markets is a functional region; "the Horn of Africa" is a vernacular region.
M: F-F-V — Formal = same Feature, Functional = same Flow, Vernacular = same Vision (people's view).
T: Give one Eritrean or African example for each type – examiners reward a correct local example more than a definition alone.

@lesson geo9-u5-l5-2
TITLE: Landforms of the oceanic crust
S: The ocean floor has three main zones from the coast outward. | Continental shelf: the gently sloping, shallow edge of the continent (to about 200 m deep) – rich in fish and oil. | Continental slope: the steep drop from the shelf edge to the deep ocean floor, often cut by submarine canyons. | Deep-sea (abyssal) plain: the wide, flat ocean floor about 3000–6000 m deep, covered with fine sediments. | On it rise mid-ocean ridges (where plates move apart), seamounts and volcanic islands, and it is cut by deep trenches (where plates go down), such as the Mariana Trench (about 11 000 m).
E: The Red Sea is a young ocean basin: its axis is a spreading ridge where the African and Arabian plates move apart, and its shallow shelves around the Dahlak Islands are important fishing grounds.
M: "Shelf, Slope, Plain, Ridge, Trench" — "Some Students Prefer Reading Texts" (S-S-P-R-T), from the shore outward.
T: Ridges = plates moving apart (new crust); trenches = plates colliding (crust destroyed). Link each ocean landform to its plate movement in your answer.

@lesson geo9-u6-l6-2
TITLE: Finding the scale when it is not given
S: Choose two points on the map whose real ground distance you know (from a road sign, a known distance or the grid/latitude). | Measure the map distance between them in centimetres. | Convert the ground distance into the same unit (km → cm: × 100 000). | Scale = map distance ÷ ground distance; write it as 1 : (ground ÷ map). | Check: a bigger denominator means a smaller-scale map with less detail.
E: Two towns are 30 km apart and 6 cm apart on the map. 30 km = 3 000 000 cm. 3 000 000 ÷ 6 = 500 000, so the scale is 1 : 500 000 (1 cm represents 5 km).
M: "Same units, then divide" — S.U.D.: Same Units, Divide ground by map.
T: The most common mistake is forgetting to convert km to cm. Write 1 km = 100 000 cm at the top of your working.
