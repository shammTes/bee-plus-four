TITLE: Forces of attraction
S: Intramolecular forces (chemical bonds) hold atoms together inside a substance: ionic (transfer of electrons, metal + non-metal), covalent (sharing of electron pairs, non-metals) and metallic (cations in a sea of delocalized electrons). | Intermolecular forces act between separate molecules and are much weaker than bonds. | London (dispersion) forces exist between all molecules and grow with molecular size. | Dipole–dipole forces act between polar molecules (e.g. HCl); hydrogen bonds are especially strong dipole forces when H is bonded to N, O or F (e.g. H₂O, NH₃, HF). | Stronger intermolecular forces → higher melting and boiling points.
E: Water (M = 18) boils at 100 °C while hydrogen sulphide H₂S (M = 34) boils at −60 °C: water molecules are held by hydrogen bonds, H₂S molecules only by weaker dipole and London forces.
M: "Hydrogen bonds need F-O-N" — H bonded to Fluorine, Oxygen or Nitrogen.
T: Boiling a molecular substance breaks intermolecular forces, not covalent bonds. Say this clearly in explanation questions.

@lesson chem10-u3-l3-2
TITLE: Calculations involving solutions
S: Molarity (M) = moles of solute ÷ volume of solution in litres (mol/L). | Moles = mass ÷ molar mass, so mass needed = M × V(L) × molar mass. | Mass percent = (mass of solute ÷ mass of solution) × 100 %; mass of solution = solute + solvent. | Dilution: the moles of solute stay the same, so M₁V₁ = M₂V₂. | Always convert mL to L (÷ 1000) before using molarity.
E: Mass of NaOH (M = 40 g/mol) to make 250 mL of 0.20 M solution: n = 0.20 mol/L × 0.250 L = 0.050 mol; mass = 0.050 × 40 = 2.0 g. Dilution: 50 mL of 2.0 M HCl diluted to 500 mL → M₂ = 2.0 × 50/500 = 0.20 M.
M: "Moles = Molarity × Litres" — M-M-L ("Many Mice Leap").
T: Write the formula, then the numbers with units, then the answer with units – most marks are lost by leaving volume in mL.

@lesson chem10-u4-l4-2
TITLE: Bases
S: Arrhenius: a base produces OH⁻ ions in water (NaOH → Na⁺ + OH⁻); a soluble base is called an alkali. | Brønsted–Lowry: a base is a proton (H⁺) acceptor: NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻. | Properties: bitter taste, slippery feel, turn red litmus blue, phenolphthalein pink, pH above 7. | Bases neutralize acids to form salt and water: NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O; with ammonium salts they release ammonia gas. | Strong bases ionize completely (NaOH, KOH); weak bases ionize partly (NH₃).
E: Titration: 25.0 mL of NaOH is neutralized by 20.0 mL of 0.10 M HCl. n(HCl) = 0.10 × 0.0200 = 0.0020 mol = n(NaOH) (1 : 1). M(NaOH) = 0.0020 ÷ 0.0250 = 0.080 M.
M: "Bases are Bitter, turn litmus Blue" — B-B-B.
T: In a titration answer, write the balanced equation first to get the mole ratio; do not assume 1 : 1 (e.g. H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH is 1 : 2).

@lesson chem10-u4-l4-5
TITLE: The pH scale
S: pH measures acidity: pH = −log[H⁺]. The scale runs from 0 to 14 at 25 °C. | pH < 7 acidic, pH = 7 neutral (pure water), pH > 7 basic (alkaline). | Each unit of pH is a factor of 10 in [H⁺]: pH 3 is ten times more acidic than pH 4. | pH + pOH = 14, where pOH = −log[OH⁻]. | Measure pH with universal indicator (colour chart), pH paper or a pH meter (most accurate).
E: [H⁺] = 1 × 10⁻³ M → pH = 3 (acidic). A 0.01 M NaOH solution: [OH⁻] = 10⁻² → pOH = 2 → pH = 14 − 2 = 12 (basic).
M: "Low pH = Lots of H⁺" — L-L.
T: Remember the scale is logarithmic: a change from pH 2 to pH 5 is a 1000-times (10³) decrease in [H⁺], not 3 times.
