X: The three desert surfaces
B: Wind erosion, transport and deposition give hot deserts three main kinds of surface. Only about a fifth of the world's deserts is sand; most is stone or bare rock.
B: **Sand desert (erg):** a wide, undulating sea of sand sheets and dunes built by wind deposition, such as the great ergs of the Sahara. Dunes move slowly downwind and can bury roads and farms.
B: **Stony desert (reg in Algeria, serir in Libya):** a plain covered with angular gravel and pebbles. Deflation has blown the fine sand and dust away, leaving stones too heavy for the wind to lift – a "desert pavement".
B: **Rocky desert (hamada):** bare rock surfaces swept clean by the wind, often with rock pedestals, zeugen and yardangs carved by abrasion.
B: In Eritrea, sand and gravel surfaces occur in the hot, dry coastal plains and the Danakil, where wind and occasional flash floods shape the land.

@lesson geo11-u3-l3-2
X: Four ways waves wear away the coast
B: **Corrasion (abrasion):** waves hurl boulders, pebbles and sand against the base of a cliff. The base is undercut, the rock above collapses, and tides and currents carry the debris away.
B: **Hydraulic action:** a breaking wave traps and compresses air in cracks. When the wave retreats the air expands violently; repeated thousands of times this widens the cracks until blocks break off.
B: **Attrition:** the rock fragments knock against the shore and against each other, becoming smaller and rounder (boulders → pebbles → sand). The *shore* is the strip between high and low tide; the *shoreline* is where shore and water meet.
B: **Solvent action (solution):** sea water slowly dissolves calcium carbonate, so limestone and chalk coasts are weakened chemically.
B: Erosion is fastest where waves are high (long fetch, strong winds), where rocks are weak or jointed, and on headlands, where wave energy is concentrated.

X: Major landforms and how contours show them
B: **Hill:** rounded land higher than its surroundings, broad base and narrow top. Contours are roughly circular and evenly spaced, with values **increasing towards the centre**. A conical hill rises about 300 m above its surroundings. A **volcanic hill** looks the same but has a **crater** (a ring of lower contour) at the summit.
B: **Mountain:** larger and higher than a hill (over about 600 m above its surroundings), with a small summit area. Contours are closed rings, closely spaced on steep sides.
B: **Mountain ridge:** a long, narrow stretch of high ground with a series of peaks separated by lower sections (saddles, cols). Contours are **elongated and close together**. The steeper side is the **scarp slope**; the gentler side is the **dip slope**.
B: **Plateau:** high land with a broad, nearly level top and steep edges (escarpments). Contours are **crowded at the sides** and almost **absent in the middle**.

@lesson geo11-u8-l8-2
X: Valleys, spurs, saddles, passes and cols
B: **V-shaped valley:** narrow and deep, cut by a river. Contours form a V whose **point aims upstream** (towards higher ground); the river runs through the V.
B: **U-shaped valley:** flat floor and steep sides, carved by a glacier. Steep sides = closely spaced U-shaped contours; the flat floor has almost no contours.
B: **Spur:** a tongue of high land sticking out into lower ground. Its contours also form a V, but the **V points downhill** (towards lower ground). Valleys and spurs usually alternate along a hillside.
B: **Saddle:** a low area between two hills, only slightly lower than them; it does not form a route through the mountains. **Pass:** a lower gap between two mountain peaks that does provide a route. **Col:** a term used for a pass or saddle across a ridge.

C: On a contour map, V-shaped contours pointing towards higher ground show a ____.
O: *valley | spur | plateau | hill top
W: In a valley the contours bend upstream, so the V points to higher ground. | A spur's V points to lower ground. | Plateaus and hilltops are shown by closed contours.

@lesson geo11-u8-l8-3
X: Calculating and using gradients
B: **Gradient** compares the vertical distance (difference in height, VI) with the horizontal distance (ground distance from the map scale, HE). Always use the same units.
B: It can be written as a **ratio** (VI : HE, e.g. 1 in 10), a **percentage** (VI ÷ HE × 100, e.g. 10%) or in **degrees** (the angle of slope; a 1 in 10 slope is about 6°).
B: Example: A at 1,300 m, B at 1,000 m, 4 cm apart on a map where 1 cm = 500 m. VI = 300 m; HE = 4 × 500 = 2,000 m; gradient = 300 : 2,000 = 1 in 6.7 ≈ 15%.
B: Gradients matter for transport: a train can climb only about **1 in 50 (2%)**, a bicycle about 1 in 20 (5%) and an ordinary car about 1 in 5 (20%). That is why the Massawa–Asmara road (about 115 km) and railway zigzag up the escarpment instead of taking the direct 70 km.

C: Point A is at 1,300 m and point B at 1,000 m. They are 4 cm apart on a map where 1 cm represents 500 m. What is the average gradient from A to B?
O: *15% | 3% | 30% | 0.5%
W: Vertical interval = 1,300 − 1,000 = 300 m. | Horizontal distance = 4 × 500 = 2,000 m. | Gradient = 300 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = 15%.

C: Why do the road and railway from Massawa to Asmara climb the escarpment in zigzags?
O: *To reduce the gradient so vehicles and trains can climb | To make the route shorter | To avoid the coast | Because the land is flat
W: The escarpment is very steep. | Zigzags make the route longer but the slope gentler. | Trains need gradients of about 2% or less, so the railway zigzags even more than the road.

C: The average gradient between two points is 1 in 25. What is it as a percentage?
O: *4% | 25% | 2.5% | 0.4%
W: Gradient % = vertical ÷ horizontal × 100. | 1 ÷ 25 × 100 = 4%. | 4% is too steep for a train (about 2% limit) but easy for a car.
