C: Which material is a good electrical insulator?
O: *Rubber | Copper | Aluminium | Silver
W: Conductors have many free electrons; insulators have almost none. | Metals such as copper, aluminium and silver are good conductors. | Rubber holds its electrons tightly, so it is an insulator.

@lesson phys10-u3-l3-3
C: A plastic rod rubbed with wool becomes negatively charged because ____.
O: *electrons move from the wool to the rod | protons move from the wool to the rod | electrons move from the rod to the wool | the rod creates new charge
W: Only electrons move when objects are charged by friction. | The rod gains electrons, so it becomes negative; the wool loses them and becomes positive. | Charge is transferred, never created.

@lesson phys10-u3-l3-4
C: Two point charges are separated by a distance $r$. If the distance is doubled, the force between them becomes ____.
O: *one quarter as large | half as large | twice as large | four times as large
W: Coulomb's law: $F = k q_1 q_2 / r^2$. | Doubling $r$ multiplies $r^2$ by 4. | So the force becomes $F/4$.

@lesson phys10-u3-l3-5
C: A charge of $2\times10^{-6}$ C experiences a force of 0.01 N in an electric field. What is the field strength?
O: *5000 N/C | 0.02 N/C | 200 N/C | 2×10⁻⁸ N/C
W: Electric field strength $E = F/q$. | $E = 0.01 / (2\times10^{-6})$. | $= 5\times10^{3}$ N/C = 5000 N/C.

@lesson phys10-u3-l3-6
C: Electric field lines around an isolated positive charge point ____.
O: *radially outward from the charge | radially inward toward the charge | in circles around the charge | nowhere – there are no field lines
W: Field lines show the direction of force on a small positive test charge. | A positive test charge is repelled by a positive charge. | So the lines point straight outward.

@lesson phys10-u3-l3-7
C: How much work is done moving a charge of 3 C through a potential difference of 12 V?
O: *36 J | 4 J | 15 J | 0.25 J
W: Work done $W = qV$. | $W = 3 \times 12$. | $= 36$ J.

@lesson phys10-u3-l3-8
WK: How a Van de Graaff generator builds up charge
P: Explain how a Van de Graaff generator produces a very high voltage on its metal dome.
ST: **Step 1: Charging the belt.** A motor drives a rubber belt over rollers; friction (or a comb at high voltage) puts charge on the belt. | **Step 2: Carrying the charge.** The belt carries the charge up inside the hollow column. | **Step 3: Transfer to the dome.** A metal comb near the top collects the charge, which moves to the outside surface of the dome (charge on a conductor sits on its outer surface). | **Step 4: Build-up.** Because the charge keeps arriving and spreads over the outside, the dome reaches a potential of hundreds of thousands of volts, until sparks discharge it.
A: A moving belt carries charge to a comb inside the dome; the charge moves to the dome's outer surface and builds up to a very high potential.

C: In a Van de Graaff generator, the charge is carried up to the dome by ____.
O: *a moving rubber belt | a copper wire from a battery | the air inside the column | magnets
W: A motor drives an insulating belt over two rollers. | Charge sprayed or rubbed onto the belt is carried upward. | A comb at the top transfers it to the dome.

C: Why does the charge on a Van de Graaff dome collect on its outer surface?
O: *Like charges repel and move as far apart as possible on a conductor | The inside of the dome is an insulator | Gravity pulls the charge outward | The belt pushes the charge outward
W: Charges on a conductor are free to move. | Like charges repel each other. | They spread to the outer surface, where they are farthest apart.

C: A person touching a charged Van de Graaff dome (while standing on an insulator) finds their hair stands up. Why?
O: *Each hair gets the same charge and the hairs repel each other | The hairs become magnetised | The hairs lose all charge | Air pressure pushes the hairs up
W: Charge flows from the dome onto the person and spreads over the body and hair. | All the hairs carry the same sign of charge. | Like charges repel, so the hairs push away from each other and stand up.

@lesson phys10-u4-l4-2
C: A charge of 120 C flows through a lamp in 1 minute. What is the current?
O: *2 A | 120 A | 0.5 A | 7200 A
W: Current $I = Q/t$; time must be in seconds: 1 min = 60 s. | $I = 120 / 60$. | $= 2$ A.

@lesson phys10-u4-l4-3
C: In a circuit diagram, the symbol of two parallel lines of different lengths represents a ____.
O: *cell | resistor | switch | lamp
W: A cell is drawn as a long thin line (positive) and a short thick line (negative). | A resistor is a rectangle; a lamp is a circle with a cross. | So the symbol is a cell.

@lesson phys10-u4-l4-4
C: A current of 0.5 A flows through a resistor with 6 V across it. What is its resistance?
O: *12 Ω | 3 Ω | 0.083 Ω | 6.5 Ω
W: Ohm's law: $V = IR$, so $R = V/I$. | $R = 6 / 0.5$. | $= 12$ Ω.

@lesson phys10-u4-l4-5
C: Two 6 Ω resistors are connected in parallel. What is the total resistance?
O: *3 Ω | 12 Ω | 6 Ω | 36 Ω
W: For parallel resistors: $1/R = 1/R_1 + 1/R_2$. | $1/R = 1/6 + 1/6 = 2/6$. | $R = 3$ Ω (equal resistors in parallel give half).

@lesson phys10-u4-l4-6
C: A 2 kW heater is used for 3 hours. How much electrical energy does it use?
O: *6 kWh | 1.5 kWh | 5 kWh | 600 kWh
W: Energy $E = Pt$. | $E = 2 \text{ kW} \times 3 \text{ h}$. | $= 6$ kWh (6 units).

@lesson phys10-u4-l4-7
C: Why are household appliances connected in parallel?
O: *Each gets the full mains voltage and can be switched on or off independently | It reduces the voltage for each appliance | It makes the current the same in all appliances | It uses fewer wires so no fuse is needed
W: In parallel every branch is connected across the supply. | So each appliance receives the full mains voltage. | One appliance can be switched off without affecting the others.

@lesson phys10-u4-l4-8
WK: Choose the correct fuse
P: A 2,300 W kettle runs on a 230 V supply. Fuses of 3 A, 5 A, 13 A and 30 A are available. Which should be fitted, and why?
ST: **Step 1: Find the normal current.** $I = P/V = 2300 / 230 = 10$ A. | **Step 2: Choose a fuse slightly above it.** 3 A and 5 A would melt in normal use. | **Step 3: Reject too large a fuse.** 30 A would let a dangerous fault current flow without melting. | **Step 4: Answer.** 13 A – just above the working current.
A: The 13 A fuse (normal current = 10 A).

C: The earth wire in a plug is connected to ____.
O: *the metal case of the appliance | the switch | the fuse only | the neutral terminal
W: If the live wire touches a metal case, the case becomes live. | The earth wire gives the current a low-resistance path to the ground. | A large current flows and blows the fuse, protecting the user.

C: A fuse protects a circuit by ____.
O: *melting and breaking the circuit when the current is too large | increasing the voltage | storing extra charge | keeping the current constant
W: A fuse is a thin wire that heats up as current flows. | If the current exceeds its rating, it melts. | The circuit breaks, preventing overheating and fire.

C: Which is a safe practice with electricity?
O: *Dry your hands before touching switches | Overload one socket with many adaptors | Use appliances with damaged cables | Push metal objects into sockets
W: Water conducts electricity and lowers skin resistance. | Overloading sockets causes overheating and fire. | Damaged cables and metal objects in sockets can cause shocks.

@lesson phys10-u5-l5-5
C: An object is placed beyond the centre of curvature of a concave mirror. The image is ____.
O: *real, inverted and diminished, between F and C | virtual, upright and magnified | real, inverted and the same size at C | virtual and diminished behind the mirror
W: Draw rays: one parallel to the axis reflects through F; one through C returns along itself. | They meet in front of the mirror between F and C. | So the image is real, inverted and smaller.
