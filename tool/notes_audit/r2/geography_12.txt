@lesson geo12-u3-l3-8
X: What a rainfall deficit means for Eritrea
B: A region has a **rainfall (water) deficit** when its rainfall is less than its **potential evapotranspiration (PET)** – the water that would evaporate from the soil and transpire from plants if water were always available. Deforestation and misuse of land make the deficit worse.
B: Eritrea's rainfall is **seasonal and variable** because the **Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ)** shifts unevenly from year to year. Massawa, for example, receives only about 190 mm of rain a year but has a PET of about 1,900 mm.
B: When rain falls, part evaporates, part soaks into the ground and part runs off. The share that infiltrates depends on rainfall intensity, vegetation, slope and soil. Heavy storms on bare, steep land mostly run off.
B: Effects: low soil moisture, falling groundwater, poor harvests and shortages of domestic water. Eritrea suffered major droughts in **1972 and 1984**. Responses include dams and ponds, terraces, tree planting, water harvesting and drought-tolerant crops.

A: Yes; the deficit is 850 mm a year (rain meets only about 35% of PET), so farming needs irrigation, water harvesting or drought-tolerant crops.

@lesson geo12-u2-l2-1
R: The water cycle in five steps
B: **1. Evaporation** from oceans, lakes, rivers and wet ground, and **transpiration** from plants, put water vapour into the air.
B: **2. Condensation:** rising vapour cools and forms clouds. **3. Precipitation:** water falls back as rain (or hail).
B: **4. Infiltration and percolation:** part of the rain soaks into the soil and down into rocks to become groundwater. **5. Runoff:** the rest flows over the surface into streams, rivers and the sea, and the cycle repeats. The study of water on the earth's surface is called **hydrology**.

@lesson geo12-u2-l2-2
R: Three groundwater zones
B: **Zone of aeration** (top): pore spaces hold both air and water; moisture varies.
B: **Zone of saturation:** every pore and crack is full of water – the source of wells and springs. Its top is the **water table**, which lies closer to the surface in valleys than under hills, so wells are cheaper to dig in valley bottoms. Pumping faster than water flows in makes the water table sink.
B: **Zone of confined water:** a permeable **aquifer** (often sand and gravel) lies between impermeable **aquicludes**. A well drilled into it rises under pressure: an **artesian well** flows to the surface by itself; a **sub-artesian well** rises only part of the way.

@lesson geo12-u2-l2-4
R: Comparing the three water-resource zones
B: **Highlands** (1,500–3,013 m): rain 500–700 mm, PET about 1,700 mm, groundwater 7–150 m deep. Problems: over-pumping for irrigation and siltation of dams. Development: dug wells, boreholes, river diversion, small dams and cisterns.
B: **Western lowlands and escarpments** (500–1,500 m): rain 200–500 mm (over 1,000 mm on the Green Belt), seasonal rivers (Anseba, Barka, Mereb-Gash), groundwater 5–100 m. Problems: low recharge, saline water, siltation, high PET. Development: hand-dug wells, sub-surface dams, drilling, river diversion.
B: **Eastern lowlands** (below 500 m): rain under 200 mm, PET over 1,900 mm, groundwater only 3–7 m deep, recharged mainly by floods from the highlands. Problems: low rain and recharge, saline water. Development: hand-dug wells and sub-surface dams in sandy river beds, boreholes outside river courses.

@lesson geo12-u3-l3-4
R: Seasonal and local winds
B: **Winter:** dry **north-east trade winds** blow from Asia and Arabia towards the ITCZ, then over southern Africa. Crossing the Red Sea they pick up a little moisture, giving winter rain to the coast and eastern escarpment.
B: **Summer:** wet **south-westerly winds** from the Atlantic (south-east trades deflected by the earth's rotation) blow towards the ITCZ north of Eritrea and bring the main kiremti rains.
B: **Local winds:** by day, heated slopes produce **anabatic (upslope)** winds; at night, cooled air drains down as **katabatic (downslope)** winds. Both affect local temperature and rainfall.

@lesson geo12-u4-l4-4
R: Why forests matter
B: **Economic:** timber, fuel wood, food, medicines, oils, gums and fibres. **Recreation and beauty:** walking, camping and relaxation.
B: **Environmental:** leaves break the force of rain; litter and humus absorb water, reduce runoff and flooding, and recharge groundwater. Trees take in carbon dioxide and give out oxygen, and they keep the local climate milder and more humid.
B: **Agro-forestry** combines trees with crops or livestock, giving fuel, fruit, fodder and soil nitrogen. Planting trees creates rural jobs, uses land unsuitable for crops and protects soil from erosion.

@lesson geo12-u4-l4-5
R: Deforestation: causes and consequences
B: **Causes:** thirty years of war (bombing, trenches, buildings), clearing land for farming, felling trees for fuel and building, wood and charcoal concessions since the Italian period, recurrent drought, overgrazing and rapid urban growth.
B: **Consequences:** fuel shortages, so dung and crop residues are burned instead of returning to the soil; soil erosion and falling yields; more flooding, siltation of dams and desertification; drier soils; timber shortages; loss of wildlife habitat and endangered species.
B: **Responses:** tree planting, area closures (enclosures), terracing, fuel-saving stoves and protected areas.

@lesson geo12-u5-l5-2
R: Causes of soil degradation
B: Soil degradation is the loss of fertile topsoil to water and wind. Eritrea loses about **15–20 tonnes of soil per hectare per year**, and up to 100 t/ha in some places.
B: **Steep slopes:** long cultivation of mountain slopes speeds up erosion. **Deforestation:** without roots and litter, runoff carries topsoil away, rills grow into gullies and leaching increases. Less than 1% of the land remains forested.
B: **Overgrazing:** about 80% of the land is used for grazing; overstocking strips the grass, starting near water points, so the soil is exposed and rain runs off instead of soaking in. **Results:** lower fertility and moisture, silted dams and lower food production.

@lesson geo12-u3-l3-6
R: Humidity in Eritrea at a glance
B: **Absolute humidity** is the mass of water vapour in a given volume of air; **specific humidity** is the mass of vapour per mass of air; **relative humidity (RH)** compares the vapour present with the most the air could hold at that temperature, as a percentage.
B: RH is highest along the Red Sea coast (about 60%, from Assab to north of Massawa) and highest of all from the coast up into the Green Belt (about 75%). It is lowest in the western lowlands near the Sudan border.
B: Seasonally, RH is highest from **August to November** and lowest from **January to April**. Warm air can hold more vapour, so the same amount of vapour gives a lower RH on a hot afternoon than on a cool morning.
