geo10-u1-q01|Climate is the average weather of a place over a long period (about 30 years); weather is the state of the atmosphere at a given time, which can change within hours.
geo10-u1-q02|Climate classifications (such as Köppen's) are based mainly on average temperature and the amount and season of precipitation, because these control vegetation and soils.
geo10-u1-q03|In the tropics the noon Sun is high all year, so solar radiation is concentrated on a small area and there is no cold season.
geo10-u1-q04|The Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone is the low-pressure belt near the equator where the north-east and south-east trade winds meet; the air rises, cools and gives heavy convectional rain.
geo10-u1-q05|The tropical wet (equatorial) climate lies in a belt about 5° (up to 10°) north and south of the equator, where the ITCZ brings rain in almost every month.
geo10-u1-q06|Equatorial climates are found in the Amazon basin, the Congo basin and Indonesia/Malaysia — all low-lying areas on or near the equator.
geo10-u1-q07|Savanna areas receive rain when the ITCZ moves over them in the hot season and have a long dry season when it moves away.
geo10-u1-q08|On many west coasts in the tropics (Namib, Atacama) cold ocean currents cool the air so it cannot rise, and subtropical high pressure brings sinking dry air, so very little rain falls.
geo10-u1-q09|In summer the subtropical high moves over Mediterranean regions, bringing sinking air, clear skies and drought; rain comes in the mild winter.
geo10-u1-q10|Both cities are at about 15°N, but Asmara is about 2 300 m higher; temperature falls about 6.5 °C per 1 000 m, so Asmara is roughly 15 °C cooler.
geo10-u1-q11|Moist air rises and drops its rain on the windward slope; descending on the leeward side it warms and becomes drier, so that side receives little rain.
geo10-u1-q12|Near the poles the Sun is always low, so its energy is spread over a large area; winters have months of darkness, and snow and ice reflect much of the incoming radiation.
geo10-u1-q13|Constant heat and rain all year allow tall, dense evergreen rainforest with several layers (canopy, emergents) and great biodiversity.
geo10-u1-q14|The long dry season and frequent fires prevent dense forest, so savanna is tall grassland with scattered drought-resistant trees such as acacia and baobab.
geo10-u1-q15|Land heats and cools faster than water, so places far inland have very hot summers and cold winters (large range), while coastal places have milder, more even temperatures.
geo10-u1-q16|A monsoon is a seasonal reversal of winds: in summer moist winds blow from the sea onto the heated land, bringing a heavy rainy season; in winter dry winds blow from the land.
geo10-u1-q17|The broad climate groups are tropical (hot and wet), dry (deserts and steppes), warm temperate, cool temperate and polar (cold) climates.
geo10-u1-q21|The tropical wet climate lies near the equator, where the ITCZ gives rain in most months; poles, ice caps and rain shadows are dry or cold.
geo10-u2-q01|Soil consists of mineral particles from weathered rock (about 45%), organic matter/humus (about 5%), water (about 25%) and air (about 25%), plus living organisms.
geo10-u2-q02|Humus is the dark, decomposed remains of plants and animals; it holds water and nutrients and improves soil structure.
geo10-u2-q04|The C horizon lies below the subsoil and consists of partly weathered parent rock, which gradually breaks down to form new soil.
geo10-u2-q05|The five soil-forming factors are climate, living organisms, relief (slope), parent material (rock) and time.
geo10-u2-q06|On steep slopes running water and gravity remove soil faster than weathering can form it, so soils are thin and stony.
geo10-u2-q07|Leaching is the downward washing of soluble minerals and nutrients through the soil by rainwater, which makes the topsoil less fertile in very wet areas.
geo10-u2-q10|Plants and animals add organic matter (humus); earthworms and termites mix and aerate the soil; roots bind it and protect it from erosion; bacteria recycle nutrients.
geo10-u2-q12|Weathering and humus formation are slow; a deep, well-developed soil profile can take thousands of years to form, so lost soil is very hard to replace.
geo10-u2-q13|Sand has large particles and large pores, so water drains through quickly; clay has tiny particles and tiny pores, so it holds water and can become waterlogged.
geo10-u2-q15|Too many animals remove the plant cover and trample the soil; bare ground is easily eroded by wind and water, which can lead to desertification.
geo10-u2-q18|Climate controls weathering and leaching: hot, wet climates give deep, leached, iron-rich red soils (laterites); cold or dry climates give thin, slowly formed soils.
geo10-u3-q01|Population density = number of people ÷ area (people per km²), e.g. 100 000 people in 500 km² = 200 people/km².
geo10-u3-q02|Deserts have very little water and extreme temperatures, so farming and settlement are difficult and few people live there.
geo10-u3-q03|River valleys offer water for drinking and irrigation, fertile alluvial soil, flat land for building and routes for transport, so people concentrate there (e.g. the Nile).
geo10-u3-q04|Natural increase = birth rate − death rate (migration is not included); it is often given per 1 000 people or as a percentage.
geo10-u3-q05|Better medical care, vaccination, clean water, sanitation and a more reliable food supply have greatly reduced deaths, especially of children.
geo10-u3-q06|A wide base shows a large proportion of children; when they reach child-bearing age the population is likely to grow quickly.
geo10-u3-q07|An ageing population has many elderly and fewer working-age people, so each worker supports more retired people, and pension and health-care costs rise.
geo10-u3-q08|Push factors drive people away from a place: drought, crop failure, unemployment, poverty, conflict, lack of services.
geo10-u3-q09|Pull factors attract people to a place: jobs, higher wages, schools, hospitals, safety and better living conditions in cities.
geo10-u3-q12|An area is underpopulated when it has too few people to develop its resources fully (e.g. not enough workers for the available land and minerals).
geo10-u3-q14|Migration is a permanent or long-term change of usual residence; commuting is daily travel to work or school while keeping the same home.
geo10-u3-q16|When fewer babies die but the birth rate stays high, natural increase rises, so population growth speeds up at first.
geo10-u3-q18|Voluntary migration is chosen (for work or study); forced migration happens because people have no choice — war, persecution, natural disasters.
geo10-u3-q19|Distribution describes how people are spread over an area (clustered, scattered); density is a number giving people per unit area.
geo10-u3-q20|Climate (Unit 1) and soils (Unit 2) decide how much food and water an area can provide (carrying capacity), which affects where people live and why they move.
geo10-u3-q21|A wide base means many children, so the population is young and likely to grow; old populations have narrow bases.
geo10-u4-q02|Life expectancy is the average number of years a newborn can expect to live; it reflects health care, nutrition, clean water and peace.
geo10-u4-q03|Infant mortality (deaths under one year per 1 000 live births) shows the quality of health care for mothers and babies, clean water and nutrition.
geo10-u4-q05|Sustainable development meets the needs of the present without reducing the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
geo10-u4-q09|The tertiary sector provides services rather than goods: teaching, health care, banking, transport, trade and tourism.
geo10-u4-q10|As countries develop, mechanisation reduces farm jobs (primary), industry grows (secondary), and later most people work in services (tertiary).
geo10-u4-q11|Infrastructure is the basic facilities a country needs: roads, railways, ports, electricity, water supply, telecommunications, schools and hospitals.
geo10-u4-q12|Roads link farmers to markets, lower transport costs, attract investment and let health and education services reach rural people.
geo10-u4-q14|Depending on one raw export is risky: prices are often low and change sharply, so a fall in the world price can badly hurt the economy.
geo10-u5-q01|A contour line joins points on a map that have the same height above sea level.
geo10-u5-q02|The contour interval is the constant vertical difference in height between neighbouring contour lines, e.g. 20 m.
geo10-u5-q03|Close contours mean height changes a lot over a short horizontal distance — a steep slope.
geo10-u5-q04|Widely spaced contours mean height changes little over a long distance — a gentle slope.
geo10-u5-q05|A set of closed contours with values increasing towards the centre shows a hill (summit at the centre); values decreasing inward would show a depression.
geo10-u5-q06|A spot height is a dot with a number showing the exact height of that point above sea level.
geo10-u5-q08|Hachures are short lines drawn in the direction of the slope; they are thicker and closer together where the slope is steeper.
geo10-u5-q09|Gradient = vertical interval (difference in height) ÷ horizontal equivalent (ground distance), both in the same units, usually written as 1 : n.
geo10-u5-q10|Distances measured on the map must be converted to real ground distances with the scale before dividing into the height difference.
geo10-u5-q12|A cross-section is a side view of the land surface along a line between two points, drawn from the heights where the line crosses contours.
geo10-u5-q13|Intervisibility asks whether a straight line of sight between two points is blocked by higher land between them.
geo10-u5-q14|If higher ground (a ridge or hill) lies above the straight line joining the two points on the cross-section, it blocks the view.
geo10-u5-q15|Evenly spaced contours show a uniform (even) slope.
geo10-u5-q17|Hill shading shades slopes as if light came from one direction (usually the north-west), so the relief looks three-dimensional.
geo10-u5-q18|Convert to the same unit: 2 km = 2 000 m; gradient = 100 ÷ 2 000 = 1/20 (1 in 20).
geo10-u5-q19|Different maps use different intervals; the interval is printed in the legend or can be worked out from labelled contours — guessing gives wrong heights and gradients.
geo10-u5-q20|Relief describes the shape of the land surface — its heights, slopes and landforms such as hills, valleys and plains.
geo10-u5-q21|Contours that are close together mean a large change in height over a short distance: a steep slope.
