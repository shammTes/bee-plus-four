bio10-u1-q01|Carbohydrates (starch, sugars) are digested to glucose, which cells oxidise in respiration to release energy as ATP — they are the body's main fuel.
bio10-u1-q02|Fibre (cellulose) is not digested, but it holds water and adds bulk, so the gut muscles can grip and push food along by peristalsis; this prevents constipation.
bio10-u1-q03|Proteins are long chains of amino acids joined by peptide bonds; digestion (pepsin, trypsin, peptidases) breaks them back into amino acids.
bio10-u1-q04|Enzymes break chemical bonds. Bile only breaks large fat drops into tiny droplets (emulsification), giving lipase more surface — a physical change, so bile is not an enzyme.
bio10-u1-q05|Chemical digestion of protein starts in the stomach, where pepsin works in the acid (HCl) conditions; the mouth has no protease.
bio10-u1-q06|The small intestine (ileum) is lined with millions of villi with thin walls and capillaries, giving a huge surface for digested food to pass into the blood.
bio10-u1-q07|By the time food reaches the large intestine nearly all nutrients are absorbed; the colon mainly reabsorbs water and some salts, making the faeces firmer.
bio10-u1-q08|Peristalsis is a wave of contraction of the circular muscles behind the bolus (with relaxation in front) that pushes food along the gut.
bio10-u1-q09|The hepatic portal vein carries blood rich in absorbed food from the intestine to the liver, which stores, converts or detoxifies it before it reaches the rest of the body.
bio10-u1-q10|Vitamin C is needed to make collagen in skin, gums and blood vessels; lack of it causes scurvy (bleeding gums, slow wound healing).
bio10-u1-q11|Iron is part of haemoglobin. Too little iron means too little haemoglobin to carry oxygen: anaemia, with tiredness and pale skin.
bio10-u1-q13|Vitamins A, D, E and K dissolve in fat (not water), are absorbed with fats and can be stored in the liver.
bio10-u1-q14|Chewing is mechanical digestion: it breaks food into small pieces, increasing the surface area on which enzymes such as amylase can act, and makes swallowing easier.
bio10-u1-q15|Stomach acid (HCl) kills most bacteria swallowed with food, gives pepsin its best (acid) pH and helps unfold proteins.
bio10-u1-q16|The pancreatic juice (and bile) is alkaline and neutralises the acid chyme from the stomach, so the enzymes of the small intestine work at their best (slightly alkaline) pH.
bio10-u1-q18|Egestion is removing undigested food (faeces) that never entered body cells; excretion is removing waste made by metabolism in cells, such as urea and CO₂.
bio10-u1-q20|The liver (makes bile), gall bladder (stores bile) and pancreas (makes enzymes) are accessory organs: food never passes through them, but their secretions enter the gut.
bio10-u1-q21|Pepsin in the acidic stomach begins protein digestion. The large intestine absorbs water; the alveolus and cochlea are not digestive organs.
bio10-u2-q01|The left ventricle pumps blood all around the body (systemic circulation) at high pressure, so it has a much thicker muscular wall than the right ventricle, which pumps only to the nearby lungs.
bio10-u2-q02|Arteries carry blood away from the heart; usually it is oxygenated, but the pulmonary artery takes deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle to the lungs.
bio10-u2-q03|Veins carry blood to the heart; usually it is deoxygenated, but the pulmonary vein brings oxygenated blood from the lungs to the left atrium.
bio10-u2-q04|The left atrioventricular valve has two flaps (cusps), so it is called the bicuspid (mitral) valve; the right one has three (tricuspid).
bio10-u2-q05|Semilunar valves at the bases of the aorta and pulmonary artery close when the ventricles relax, stopping blood flowing back into the ventricles.
bio10-u2-q06|Arteries carry blood away from the heart, veins carry it back, and capillaries are the tiny vessels between them where exchange with the tissues takes place.
bio10-u2-q07|Capillary walls are only one cell thick, so oxygen, glucose and wastes have a very short distance to diffuse between blood and tissue cells.
bio10-u2-q08|Plasma is about 90% water, in which glucose, amino acids, salts, hormones, urea, CO₂ and plasma proteins are dissolved and carried.
bio10-u2-q09|Haemoglobin in red blood cells combines with oxygen where it is plentiful (lungs) to form oxyhaemoglobin and releases it where oxygen is low (respiring tissues).
bio10-u2-q10|At a cut, platelets stick together and release chemicals that turn soluble fibrinogen into fibrin threads, which trap blood cells and form a clot.
bio10-u2-q11|If donor red cells carry an antigen that the recipient's plasma has antibodies against, the cells clump together (agglutination) and can block vessels.
bio10-u2-q12|Group O red cells carry neither A nor B antigens, so the recipient's anti-A or anti-B antibodies have nothing to attack.
bio10-u2-q13|Group AB plasma has no anti-A or anti-B antibodies, so it does not clump donated A, B, AB or O cells.
bio10-u2-q14|The heart muscle needs its own supply of oxygen and glucose; the coronary arteries branch from the aorta and run over the heart wall to supply it.
bio10-u2-q15|Each heartbeat sends a pressure surge through the elastic arteries; where an artery is near the surface (wrist, neck) it can be felt as the pulse. Veins have low, steady pressure.
bio10-u2-q16|In double circulation blood passes through the heart twice for each complete circuit: heart → lungs → heart (pulmonary) and heart → body → heart (systemic).
bio10-u2-q17|The left side receives oxygenated blood from the lungs (pulmonary vein) and pumps it into the aorta; the right side handles deoxygenated blood.
bio10-u2-q18|Blood in veins is under low pressure and can flow backwards, especially in the legs; pocket valves close to stop back-flow, helped by squeezing muscles.
bio10-u2-q19|Blood carries urea (made in the liver), excess salts and excess water to the kidneys, where they are filtered out into urine.
bio10-u2-q20|A stroke happens when a clot blocks (or a vessel bursts in) an artery to the brain, so brain cells beyond it are starved of oxygen and die.
bio10-u2-q21|The pulmonary vein returns oxygenated blood from the lungs to the left atrium. Urea goes to the kidney in the renal artery, and lymph and air are not carried by veins.
bio10-u3-q01|High blood pressure at the arterial end of capillaries forces plasma (without red cells and large proteins) out through the walls; this is tissue fluid.
bio10-u3-q02|Some tissue fluid does not return to the capillaries; it drains into blind-ended lymph capillaries and is then called lymph.
bio10-u3-q03|Lymph nodes contain many lymphocytes; during infection these multiply rapidly to make antibodies and trap pathogens, so the nodes swell and become tender.
bio10-u3-q04|Each villus has a lacteal (lymph vessel); digested fats (fatty acids and glycerol, re-formed into droplets) enter the lacteals rather than the blood capillaries.
bio10-u3-q05|The lymph system has no pump. Lymph is moved when surrounding skeletal muscles squeeze the vessels and by breathing movements; valves make it flow one way.
bio10-u3-q06|Lymph vessels join into larger ducts (thoracic duct) that empty into the subclavian veins near the collarbone, returning the fluid to the blood.
bio10-u3-q07|Red blood cells are too large to leave the capillaries, so tissue fluid and lymph contain almost none; lymph contains mainly water, solutes and white cells (lymphocytes).
bio10-u3-q08|Oedema (swelling) occurs when tissue fluid builds up — because lymph vessels are blocked or because blood pressure in the capillaries is too high.
bio10-u3-q09|Lymph nodes are grouped in the neck, armpits, groin and around the intestine (mesentery); these are where swelling is felt during infection.
bio10-u3-q10|The spleen is the largest lymphoid organ: it contains many lymphocytes, filters the blood and removes old red cells.
bio10-u3-q11|Yes — like veins, lymph vessels have valves that stop lymph flowing backwards, because the pressure in them is very low.
bio10-u3-q12|Lymph capillaries begin as closed (blind) tubes among the cells; tissue fluid enters through their walls but there is no opening at the start.
bio10-u3-q13|After a fatty meal the lacteals carry many tiny fat droplets, which make the lymph from the intestine look milky (chyle).
bio10-u3-q14|Blood is pumped by the heart. Lymph has no pump; it relies on muscle contraction, breathing movements and valves.
bio10-u3-q15|As lymph passes through a node, lymphocytes and phagocytes trap and destroy bacteria, viruses and debris before the lymph returns to the blood.
bio10-u3-q16|Tissue fluid surrounds every cell, bringing oxygen and nutrients from the blood and taking away CO₂ and wastes.
bio10-u3-q17|At the venous end of the capillary the blood pressure is lower, so most tissue fluid moves back into the capillary by osmosis.
bio10-u3-q18|The small amount of tissue fluid that does not re-enter the blood capillaries drains into lymph capillaries and becomes lymph.
bio10-u3-q19|Tonsils (and adenoids) are masses of lymphoid tissue at the back of the throat that trap pathogens entering through the mouth and nose.
bio10-u3-q20|The lymphatic system returns leaked fluid to the blood (link to circulation, Unit 2) and carries lymphocytes that defend the body (link to immunity, Unit 4).
bio10-u4-q02|An antibody is a Y-shaped protein made by lymphocytes; its binding sites fit one specific antigen, so it marks or clumps that pathogen for destruction.
bio10-u4-q04|Lymphocytes give specific immunity: B lymphocytes make antibodies against one antigen, T lymphocytes attack infected cells, and some become memory cells.
bio10-u4-q05|In active immunity your own lymphocytes meet the antigen and make antibodies and memory cells, so protection is long-lasting.
bio10-u4-q06|In passive immunity ready-made antibodies come from outside (mother, antiserum); your body makes no memory cells, so protection fades quickly.
bio10-u4-q07|A vaccine contains a harmless form of the antigen; your own body responds and makes memory cells — active immunity given artificially.
bio10-u4-q08|Antibodies pass naturally from mother to baby in breast milk (and across the placenta); the baby did not make them, so the immunity is passive and natural.
bio10-u4-q09|Memory cells from the first dose slowly decline; a booster dose re-exposes the body to the antigen so more memory cells and antibodies are produced.
bio10-u4-q10|Skin is a physical barrier that stops most microbes entering — part of the first line of defence, which acts against all pathogens (non-specific).
bio10-u4-q12|Redness, heat and swelling come from widened blood vessels at the injured site, bringing more blood, phagocytes and fluid to fight infection and repair tissue.
bio10-u4-q13|After the first infection memory cells remain; on a second exposure they recognise the antigen and produce antibodies quickly, often before you feel ill.
bio10-u4-q14|Antiserum gives ready-made antibodies that are gradually broken down within weeks; your own lymphocytes made no memory cells, so protection does not last.
bio10-u4-q15|In an allergy the immune system reacts strongly to a harmless substance (pollen, dust, some foods), releasing histamine that causes sneezing, rashes or swelling.
bio10-u4-q16|Catching measles makes your own body produce antibodies and memory cells — active, natural immunity that usually lasts for life.
bio10-u4-q17|Lysozyme in tears and saliva digests bacterial walls; stomach acid and sticky mucus in the airways are other non-specific chemical defences.
bio10-u4-q18|White blood cells travel in the blood and move between blood, tissue fluid and lymph; lymphocytes collect in lymph nodes.
bio10-u4-q20|Vaccination gives people memory cells without suffering the disease; when most people are immune the pathogen cannot spread, which also protects the unvaccinated (herd immunity).
bio10-u4-q21|A vaccine stimulates your own lymphocytes to make antibodies and memory cells (active immunity). Borrowed antibodies would be passive; bile and urea are unrelated.
bio10-u5-q01|The nasal cavity has hairs and mucus that trap dust and microbes, and a good blood supply that warms and moistens the air before it reaches the lungs.
bio10-u5-q02|C-shaped rings of cartilage in the trachea keep it open when air pressure inside falls during breathing in; the open side lets the oesophagus expand.
bio10-u5-q03|Alveoli have a huge total surface area, walls one cell thick, a moist lining for gases to dissolve, and a dense capillary network that keeps a steep concentration gradient.
bio10-u5-q04|When breathing in, the diaphragm muscle contracts and flattens (moves down), and the external intercostal muscles lift the ribs, increasing the chest volume.
bio10-u5-q06|Increasing the volume of the chest lowers the pressure inside the lungs below atmospheric pressure, so air flows in.
bio10-u5-q07|The oxygen concentration in the alveolus is higher than in the blood arriving at the lungs, so oxygen diffuses across the thin walls into the blood.
bio10-u5-q08|Blood reaching the lungs has a higher CO₂ concentration than alveolar air, so CO₂ diffuses from the blood into the alveolus and is breathed out.
bio10-u5-q09|Breathing (ventilation) is the movement of air into and out of the lungs; respiration is the chemical reaction in cells that releases energy from food.
bio10-u5-q10|Aerobic respiration uses oxygen to break glucose completely: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy (ATP).
bio10-u5-q11|When muscles lack oxygen, glucose is broken down only partly to lactic acid, releasing much less ATP than aerobic respiration.
bio10-u5-q12|After hard exercise extra oxygen is needed to oxidise (break down) the lactic acid built up during anaerobic respiration; this is why you keep panting.
bio10-u5-q13|Working muscles respire faster and produce more CO₂; the brain detects the rise and increases breathing rate and depth to bring in O₂ and remove CO₂.
bio10-u5-q14|Tobacco smoke paralyses and destroys cilia (so mucus and dirt stay in the lungs) and damages alveolar walls (emphysema), reducing the surface for gas exchange.
bio10-u5-q16|The trachea divides into two bronchi, one to each lung; these branch further into bronchioles ending in alveoli.
bio10-u5-q17|Humans exchange gases in the lungs, across the walls of the alveoli.
bio10-u5-q19|Expired air has more CO₂ (about 4% instead of 0.04%), less oxygen (about 16% instead of 21%) and more water vapour, because of respiration in the body.
bio10-u5-q21|Gas exchange takes place across the thin, moist walls of the alveoli. The trachea only conducts air; canines and semicircular canals are unrelated.
bio10-u6-q01|Excretion is the removal from the body of waste products of metabolism (urea, CO₂, excess water and salts) and of substances in excess.
bio10-u6-q02|Egestion is passing out undigested food as faeces; this material never entered the body cells, so it is not excretion.
bio10-u6-q03|Excess amino acids are deaminated in the liver; the nitrogen-containing part becomes urea, the main nitrogenous waste of humans.
bio10-u6-q04|The liver removes the amino group from excess amino acids (deamination) and converts the ammonia into urea, which the blood carries to the kidneys.
bio10-u6-q05|The lungs excrete carbon dioxide (and some water vapour), the waste products of respiration.
bio10-u6-q06|Sweat glands remove water, salts (NaCl) and a small amount of urea, so the skin is a minor excretory organ.
bio10-u6-q07|Each kidney contains about a million nephrons; each nephron filters blood and adjusts what is kept and what becomes urine.
bio10-u6-q08|In the glomerulus blood is under high pressure, so water and small molecules (glucose, salts, urea) are forced into Bowman's capsule — ultrafiltration.
bio10-u6-q09|As the filtrate passes along the tubule, useful substances (all glucose, much water, needed salts) are reabsorbed into the blood.
bio10-u6-q10|All glucose is reabsorbed in the tubule, and proteins are too large to be filtered, so neither should appear in healthy urine.
bio10-u6-q11|When blood glucose is very high (diabetes) the tubules cannot reabsorb all of it, so glucose appears in the urine.
bio10-u6-q13|Each ureter carries urine from a kidney to the bladder.
bio10-u6-q14|The urethra carries urine from the bladder out of the body.
bio10-u6-q16|In dialysis blood flows past a partially permeable membrane bathed in dialysis fluid; urea and excess salts diffuse out, doing the filtering job of failed kidneys.
bio10-u6-q17|Faeces are mostly undigested food that passed through the gut without entering the cells, so they are egested, not excreted.
bio10-u6-q18|After drinking a lot the kidneys reabsorb less water; the same amount of urea is dissolved in more water, so urine is pale and plentiful.
bio10-u6-q19|On a hot day much water is lost as sweat, so the kidneys reabsorb more water; urine is less in volume, more concentrated and darker.
bio10-u6-q20|Blood is filtered in the kidney, so the renal vein carries blood with much less urea than the renal artery.
bio10-u6-q21|Glucose is completely reabsorbed in the kidney tubules, so it should not be in urine. Water, urea and some salts are normal parts of urine.
bio10-u7-q01|A receptor (sense cell) detects a stimulus such as light or pressure and converts its energy into an electrical nerve impulse.
bio10-u7-q02|The skin has receptors for touch, pressure, pain, heat and cold.
bio10-u7-q03|Taste buds are mainly on the tongue (on its small bumps, the papillae).
bio10-u7-q04|Smell (olfactory) receptors are in the lining at the top of the nasal cavity; chemicals in the air dissolve in its mucus.
bio10-u7-q05|Light bends most where it passes from air into the curved cornea; the lens only makes the fine adjustment.
bio10-u7-q06|The lens changes shape to focus light from near or distant objects onto the retina (accommodation).
bio10-u7-q07|Rods are very sensitive and work in dim light but do not detect colour, so night vision is black-and-white.
bio10-u7-q08|Cones need bright light and come in types sensitive to different colours, so they give colour vision; they are packed in the fovea.
bio10-u7-q09|The iris is a ring of muscle that changes the size of the pupil: small in bright light (protects the retina), large in dim light.
bio10-u7-q10|At the blind spot the optic nerve leaves the eye; there are no rods or cones there, so light falling on it is not detected.
bio10-u7-q11|In short sight the image forms in front of the retina; a diverging (concave) lens spreads the rays so they focus on the retina.
bio10-u7-q12|The eardrum (tympanic membrane) vibrates when sound waves strike it and passes the vibrations to the ear bones.
bio10-u7-q13|The hammer, anvil and stirrup act as levers that pass the vibrations from the large eardrum to the small oval window, amplifying them.
bio10-u7-q14|The cochlea contains fluid and sensory hair cells; vibrations move the hair cells, which send impulses along the auditory nerve.
bio10-u7-q15|The fluid in the three semicircular canals moves when the head turns; receptors detect this and help keep balance.
bio10-u7-q16|The Eustachian tube links the middle ear to the throat and lets air in or out so the pressure is the same on both sides of the eardrum.
bio10-u7-q17|Internal receptors detect changes inside the body, such as muscle stretch, blood CO₂ or glucose levels, temperature and body position.
bio10-u7-q18|The two eyes see the same object from slightly different angles; the brain combines the views to judge distance (binocular vision), and one eye remains if the other is hurt.
bio10-u7-q19|Very loud sounds over-stimulate and damage the delicate hair cells of the cochlea, which do not regrow, causing permanent hearing loss.
bio10-u7-q20|For a near object the ciliary muscles contract, the suspensory ligaments slacken and the lens becomes fatter, bending light more so the image focuses on the retina.
bio10-u8-q01|Dendrites are short branches of a neuron that receive impulses from other neurons and carry them toward the cell body.
bio10-u8-q02|The axon is the long fibre that carries impulses away from the cell body to the next neuron or to an effector.
bio10-u8-q03|The fatty myelin sheath insulates the axon and makes the impulse jump between gaps (nodes), so it travels much faster.
bio10-u8-q04|A synapse is a tiny gap between neurons; the impulse releases a chemical transmitter that diffuses across and starts an impulse in the next neuron, only in one direction.
bio10-u8-q05|Sensory neurons carry impulses from receptors (sense organs) to the central nervous system.
bio10-u8-q06|Motor neurons carry impulses from the CNS to effectors (muscles or glands).
bio10-u8-q07|Relay (connector) neurons lie inside the brain or spinal cord and pass impulses from sensory to motor neurons.
bio10-u8-q08|The central nervous system is the brain and spinal cord, where information is processed and responses coordinated.
bio10-u8-q09|The peripheral nervous system consists of all the nerves outside the brain and spinal cord (cranial and spinal nerves).
bio10-u8-q10|A reflex is a rapid, automatic response to a stimulus that we are born with; it uses a reflex arc and does not need conscious thought (e.g. knee jerk, blinking).
bio10-u8-q11|Stimulus → receptor → sensory neuron → relay neuron (in the spinal cord) → motor neuron → effector → response.
bio10-u8-q12|The impulse is passed from sensory to motor neuron in the spinal cord; it does not have to travel to the brain and be thought about, so the response is very fast.
bio10-u8-q13|The cerebrum controls conscious thought, memory, intelligence, the senses and voluntary movements.
bio10-u8-q14|The cerebellum coordinates muscle movements and maintains balance and posture.
bio10-u8-q15|The medulla oblongata controls automatic activities such as breathing rate, heartbeat and blood pressure.
bio10-u8-q16|An effector carries out the response: a muscle contracts or a gland secretes.
bio10-u8-q17|Inborn (innate) behaviour is present from birth without learning, such as a baby suckling, blinking or the knee-jerk reflex.
bio10-u8-q18|Learned behaviour develops through experience or practice, such as writing, riding a bicycle or responding to a school bell.
bio10-u8-q19|A nerve impulse is a wave of electrical change caused by ions (Na⁺, K⁺) moving across the neuron membrane, travelling along the axon.
bio10-u8-q20|Transmitter is released only on one side of a synapse, so impulses travel in one direction and messages reach the right place without mixing.
bio10-u8-q21|In a reflex arc the receptor passes the impulse to a sensory neuron, then relay and motor neurons. Red cells and villi are not nerve cells.
