be10-u1-q01|Human wants are unlimited but the resources to satisfy them (land, labour, capital, time) are limited, so people, firms and governments must choose which wants to satisfy.
be10-u1-q02|Opportunity cost is only the single best alternative given up — e.g. the money you could have earned working that evening — not the sum of all alternatives.
be10-u1-q03|A point inside the production possibility curve means the economy is producing less than it could: some resources are unemployed or used inefficiently.
be10-u1-q04|Points outside the PPC cannot be produced with the existing resources and technology; they become possible only if the curve shifts outward.
be10-u1-q05|The PPC shifts outward when the economy can produce more of both goods: more or better resources (labour, capital), improved technology or better education and skills.
be10-u1-q06|The factors of production are land (natural resources), labour (human effort), capital (man-made tools and machines) and enterprise (organising and risk-taking).
be10-u1-q07|In economics capital means man-made goods used to produce other goods — machines, tools, buildings, vehicles — not money itself.
be10-u1-q08|Every economy must decide what to produce, how to produce it (which methods and resources) and for whom (how output is distributed).
be10-u1-q09|In a market economy, prices set by supply and demand signal what consumers want; producers seeking profit respond, and goods go to those willing and able to pay.
be10-u1-q10|In a command (planned) economy the government owns most resources and a central plan decides what, how and for whom to produce.
be10-u1-q11|A mixed economy combines private markets with government involvement — the state provides public services and regulates, while private firms produce most goods.
be10-u1-q12|Positive statements describe what is and can be tested with facts ("prices rose 5%"); normative statements say what ought to be and involve value judgements ("prices should be controlled").
be10-u1-q13|Microeconomics studies individual units — households, firms, single markets; macroeconomics studies the whole economy — national income, inflation, unemployment.
be10-u1-q14|A free good is so plentiful that using more of it costs nobody anything, e.g. air or sunlight; it has no opportunity cost and no price.
be10-u1-q15|An economic good is scarce: producing or using more of it means giving up something else, so it has an opportunity cost and usually a price.
be10-u1-q16|When people and regions specialise in what they do best, they become more skilled and use comparative advantage, so total output rises and goods can be traded.
be10-u1-q17|Consumer goods satisfy wants directly (food, clothes); capital goods are used to produce other goods (tractors, machines).
be10-u1-q18|The entrepreneur organises land, labour and capital and takes the risk of the business; the reward is profit (or a loss if it fails).
be10-u1-q19|A government budget is limited, so money spent on one project (clinics) cannot be used for another (roads); the road not built is the opportunity cost.
be10-u1-q20|Resources have alternative uses but are limited; using them for one good means less of another — the idea of scarcity and trade-off shown by the PPC.
be10-u1-q21|Opportunity cost is only the next-best alternative forgone, not every alternative added together.
be10-u1-q22|Inside the PPC the economy is not using all its resources fully or efficiently; points outside are unattainable.
be10-u2-q01|Barter works only if each trader has what the other wants at the same time (double coincidence of wants), which is rare and makes trade slow.
be10-u2-q02|As a medium of exchange money is generally accepted in payment for goods and services, so people no longer need a double coincidence of wants.
be10-u2-q03|As a unit of account money measures and compares the value of goods (prices in Nakfa) and is used to keep accounts.
be10-u2-q04|Money can be kept and spent later, so it stores value (wealth); inflation reduces what stored money can buy.
be10-u2-q05|Gold is scarce (keeps its value), durable (does not rot), divisible, portable and recognisable — the qualities of good money.
be10-u2-q06|Fiat money (notes and coins) has little value as material; it is money because the government declares it legal tender and people trust it.
be10-u2-q07|Commercial banks accept deposits, lend money to individuals and businesses, and provide payment services (cheques, transfers), earning interest on loans.
be10-u2-q08|Banks know not all depositors withdraw at once, so they keep only a fraction of deposits as cash reserves and lend the rest out at interest.
be10-u2-q09|Interest is the price paid for borrowing money; to the saver it is the reward for lending and postponing spending.
be10-u2-q10|Money in a bank is safer than at home, can earn interest, and can be used for payments by cheque or transfer.
be10-u2-q11|Many people pay small premiums into a common fund; the few who suffer a loss (fire, accident) are compensated from it, so risk is shared.
be10-u2-q12|Liquidity is how quickly an asset can be turned into spending power without loss; cash is perfectly liquid, while selling a house takes time and costs.
be10-u2-q13|When prices rise, each unit of money buys less, so people holding cash or receiving fixed incomes (pensions, fixed wages) lose purchasing power.
be10-u2-q14|The central bank (Bank of Eritrea) issues currency, controls the money supply, is banker to the government and supervises banks; commercial banks serve the public and businesses.
be10-u2-q15|A cheque is a written order from an account holder to their bank to pay a stated sum to a named person from their account.
be10-u2-q16|A note's value comes from its legal-tender status and general acceptance, not from the paper, so a worn note is still accepted at face value.
be10-u2-q17|Microfinance institutions give small loans (and savings services) to poor people and small businesses that ordinary banks often refuse because they lack collateral.
be10-u2-q18|If the money supply grows faster than the output of goods and services, more money chases the same goods and prices rise (inflation).
be10-u2-q19|As a standard of deferred payment money allows debts and contracts to be agreed now and paid in the future in a known amount.
be10-u2-q20|Goods in barter are hard to divide, store and value exactly; money measures value precisely, so wages and taxes can be paid in exact amounts.
be10-u2-q21|Because money is generally acceptable, it removes the need for a double coincidence of wants that barter requires.
be10-u3-q01|Everything a business owns (assets) is financed either by outsiders (liabilities) or by the owner (equity): Assets = Liabilities + Owner's equity.
be10-u3-q02|Assets are resources owned by the business that have value: cash, bank balance, furniture, equipment, stock and money owed by customers (receivables).
be10-u3-q03|Liabilities are amounts the business owes to outsiders: loans, creditors (accounts payable), unpaid bills.
be10-u3-q04|Equity (capital) is the owner's claim on the business after liabilities are paid: capital invested + profit − drawings = assets − liabilities.
be10-u3-q05|Every transaction affects at least two accounts with equal debits and credits, so the accounting equation always stays in balance and errors are easier to find.
be10-u3-q06|Assets have debit balances: an increase in an asset is recorded on the debit side; a decrease on the credit side.
be10-u3-q07|Liabilities have credit balances: an increase is recorded on the credit side; a decrease on the debit side.
be10-u3-q08|Income increases owner's equity, which has a credit balance, so income is credited (and the cash or debtor account is debited).
be10-u3-q09|Expenses reduce owner's equity, so they are recorded on the debit side (and cash or a creditor is credited).
be10-u3-q10|Drawings reduce the owner's equity and the asset taken: debit the Drawings account, credit Cash (or the asset withdrawn).
be10-u3-q11|If total debits equal total credits the double entry is arithmetically correct, but errors such as posting to the wrong account or omitting a transaction are not revealed.
be10-u3-q12|A service business sells services (repairs, teaching) and has no stock of goods for resale, so it has no purchases or cost of goods sold.
be10-u3-q13|When cash is received for a service, the business issues a receipt (or cash voucher); its copy is the source document for the entry.
be10-u3-q14|The journal (book of original entry) records transactions in date order, showing the accounts to be debited and credited, before they are posted to the ledger.
be10-u3-q15|Net profit = total income (revenue) − total expenses for the period; if expenses are greater, the result is a loss.
be10-u3-q16|Equity = assets − liabilities = 8 000 − 3 000 = 5 000.
be10-u3-q17|One asset (furniture) increases and another (cash) decreases by 1 000; total assets, liabilities and equity are unchanged.
be10-u3-q18|Cash (asset) increases → debit Cash; the owner's capital (equity) increases → credit Capital.
be10-u3-q19|Errors of commission, principle, omission or compensating errors affect both sides equally, so the trial balance still agrees even though accounts are wrong.
be10-u3-q20|The statement of financial position (balance sheet) shows assets, liabilities and equity at one date; the income statement shows income and expenses over a period.
be10-u3-q21|By the accounting equation, assets are financed by liabilities and owner's equity.
be10-u3-q22|Cash (asset) increases → debit Cash; income increases equity → credit Service income.
