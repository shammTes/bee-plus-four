chem10-u1-q01|Moseley showed that the atomic number (number of protons) is the true basis of the order; arranging by atomic number removes the anomalies of Mendeleev's mass order (e.g. Ar and K).
chem10-u1-q02|Elements in a group have the same number of valence (outer) electrons, and since reactions involve these electrons they have similar chemical properties.
chem10-u1-q03|Group 1 atoms have one outer electron, which they lose easily to reach a noble-gas arrangement, forming 1+ ions (Na⁺, K⁺).
chem10-u1-q05|Down Group 1 the outer electron is in a shell further from the nucleus and more shielded, so it is lost more easily and reactivity increases (Li < Na < K).
chem10-u1-q06|Halogens react by gaining an electron. Down the group the atoms are larger, so the nucleus attracts an incoming electron less strongly and reactivity decreases (F > Cl > Br > I).
chem10-u1-q07|Noble gases have a complete outer shell (stable octet, or duet for He), so they have no tendency to gain, lose or share electrons.
chem10-u1-q08|The transition metals form the d-block in the middle of the table, between Group 2 and Group 3 (13), from Period 4 downwards.
chem10-u1-q10|Across a period electrons are added to the same shell while the number of protons increases; the stronger nuclear pull draws the electrons closer, so atoms get smaller.
chem10-u1-q11|Down a group each element has one more occupied electron shell, so the outer electrons are further from the nucleus and the atom is larger.
chem10-u1-q12|Across a period atoms hold their electrons more strongly (more protons, smaller size), so they lose electrons less easily: elements change from metals (Na, Mg) to non-metals (Cl, Ar).
chem10-u1-q14|The period number equals the number of occupied electron shells: sodium (2, 8, 1) has three shells and is in Period 3.
chem10-u1-q15|Chlorine (2, 8, 7) has 7 valence electrons, so it is in Group 7 (Group 17), the halogens.
chem10-u1-q16|Hydrogen has one electron like Group 1 and can form H⁺, but it is a non-metal gas that forms covalent bonds and can gain an electron (H⁻) like a halogen, so it does not fit one group neatly.
chem10-u1-q17|Across a period the nuclear charge increases and atoms get smaller, so the outer electrons are held more tightly and more energy is needed to remove one.
chem10-u1-q19|Sodium (Z = 11) has the arrangement 2, 8, 1: three shells (Period 3) and one outer electron (Group 1).
chem10-u1-q20|Potassium is below lithium in Group 1; its outer electron is further from the nucleus and is lost more easily, so K is more reactive.
chem10-u2-q01|An ionic bond forms when a metal atom transfers electrons to a non-metal atom; the resulting positive and negative ions attract each other.
chem10-u2-q05|In a metal, positive ions sit in layers in a sea of delocalised electrons; when force is applied the layers slide over each other, but the electron sea still holds them together, so the metal bends instead of breaking.
chem10-u2-q07|A lone pair is a pair of valence electrons on an atom that is not shared in a bond, e.g. the two lone pairs on the oxygen in water.
chem10-u2-q09|A hydrogen bond forms when H is bonded to a very electronegative atom (N, O or F); the slightly positive H is attracted to a lone pair on an N, O or F of another molecule.
chem10-u2-q10|When water freezes, hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in an open hexagonal lattice with more space between them than in the liquid, so ice is less dense and floats.
chem10-u2-q11|In an ionic lattice each ion is surrounded by ions of opposite charge and attracted equally in all directions, so ionic bonds are non-directional and strong.
chem10-u2-q12|A simple molecular substance consists of separate small molecules (H₂O, CO₂); a giant structure (diamond, NaCl, SiO₂) is a continuous network of bonds with no separate molecules.
chem10-u2-q13|Silicon dioxide is a giant covalent lattice in which every atom is joined by strong covalent bonds; a lot of energy is needed to break these, so its melting point is very high (about 1 700 °C).
chem10-u2-q16|In a polar covalent bond the more electronegative atom attracts the shared electrons more strongly, getting a partial negative charge (δ−) while the other atom is δ+, e.g. H–Cl.
chem10-u2-q19|When water boils the molecules separate from each other, so only the intermolecular forces (hydrogen bonds) are overcome; the O–H covalent bonds inside each molecule stay intact.

chem10-u5-q01|In an exothermic reaction energy is released to the surroundings, which get warmer; the products have less energy than the reactants, so ΔH is negative.
chem10-u5-q02|In an endothermic reaction energy is absorbed from the surroundings, which get colder; the products have more energy than the reactants, so ΔH is positive.
chem10-u5-q03|Energy must be supplied to pull bonded atoms apart, so breaking bonds is always endothermic.
chem10-u5-q04|When new bonds form, energy is released, so bond making is always exothermic.
chem10-u5-q05|Burning methane releases a lot of heat (ΔH ≈ −890 kJ/mol); that is why it is used as a fuel.
chem10-u5-q06|Breaking down a carbonate (e.g. CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂) needs continuous heating — energy is taken in, so it is endothermic.
chem10-u5-q07|When an acid neutralises a base, H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O releases heat (about −57 kJ per mole of water), so the mixture warms up.
chem10-u5-q08|Photosynthesis absorbs light energy and stores it as chemical energy in glucose, so it is endothermic.
chem10-u5-q11|The law of conservation of energy: energy cannot be created or destroyed, only changed from one form to another (e.g. chemical energy to heat).
chem10-u5-q12|Enthalpy changes are given in kilojoules (kJ), usually per mole of reaction as written (kJ/mol, kJ mol⁻¹).
chem10-u5-q13|The heat of combustion is the energy released when one mole of a substance burns completely in excess oxygen under standard conditions.
chem10-u5-q14|Dissolving ammonium nitrate or potassium nitrate in water absorbs heat, so the solution gets colder — an endothermic heat of solution (used in cold packs).
chem10-u5-q15|In an exothermic reaction energy is released, so the products have less energy and are drawn lower than the reactants on the energy diagram.
chem10-u5-q16|Even exothermic reactions need activation energy (Ea) to start breaking bonds; the match supplies this, then the heat released keeps the reaction going.
chem10-u5-q17|Fusion means melting; the heat of fusion is the energy needed to change one mole of solid into liquid at its melting point without a change of temperature.
chem10-u5-q19|A thermochemical equation is a balanced equation with state symbols and the value of ΔH, because the energy depends on the amounts and states of the substances.
chem10-u5-q20|ΔH = energy to break bonds − energy released making bonds; if more is released than absorbed, ΔH is negative and the reaction is exothermic.
chem10-u5-q21|A strong acid is completely ionised in water. Concentration is a different idea; pH 14 is strongly alkaline.
chem10-u5-q22|Exothermic reactions release energy, so the enthalpy of the system decreases and ΔH is negative.
