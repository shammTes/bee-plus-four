chem9-u1-q01|Chemistry is the branch of science that studies the composition, structure and properties of matter and the changes (reactions) it undergoes.
chem9-u1-q02|Matter is anything that has mass and occupies space. A blown-up balloon shows air takes up space, and a pumped-up ball weighs more than a flat one, so air has mass.
chem9-u1-q03|Light is a form of energy, not matter: it does not take up space or have mass that can be weighed.
chem9-u1-q04|Almost everything we use — medicines, fuels, fertilisers, plastics, soaps, building materials — is made or improved by chemical processes, so modern life depends on chemistry.
chem9-u1-q05|Particles in solids and liquids are close together, so these states have a fixed volume; gas particles move freely and spread to fill any container.
chem9-u1-q06|Gas particles are far apart with large empty spaces between them, so pressing pushes them closer; solid and liquid particles are already touching.
chem9-u1-q07|Heating gives the particles more kinetic energy; they vibrate more until at the melting point they overcome the forces holding them in place and start sliding past each other.
chem9-u1-q08|Boiling happens throughout the liquid at one fixed temperature (the boiling point), with bubbles; evaporation happens only at the surface and at any temperature below the boiling point.
chem9-u1-q10|Measuring density does not change the substance into anything new, so density is a physical property.
chem9-u1-q11|Rusting changes iron into a new substance (hydrated iron oxide); the ability to rust describes how iron reacts, so it is a chemical property.
chem9-u1-q12|Melting changes only the state; the water molecules are the same before and after and the ice can be re-formed by cooling, so it is a physical change.
chem9-u1-q13|Burning produces new substances (carbon dioxide, water vapour, ash) and cannot be reversed, so it is a chemical change.
chem9-u1-q14|Signs of a chemical change: a colour change, gas given off, heat or light produced (or absorbed), a precipitate forming, and usually it is difficult to reverse.
chem9-u1-q15|An element contains only one kind of atom and cannot be broken into simpler substances; a compound contains two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio.
chem9-u1-q16|In water, hydrogen and oxygen are chemically bonded in a fixed 2:1 ratio and the properties are different from both gases; they can only be separated by a chemical process such as electrolysis.
chem9-u1-q17|A homogeneous mixture has the same composition throughout and you cannot see separate parts, e.g. salt solution, air, alloys like brass.
chem9-u1-q18|A heterogeneous mixture has visibly different parts, e.g. sand in water, granite (different crystals), smoke (solid particles in air).
chem9-u1-q19|Salt dissolves in water but sand does not: dissolve, filter off the sand (residue), then evaporate the filtrate to obtain the salt.
chem9-u1-q20|In distillation the liquid with the lower boiling point evaporates first; its vapour is cooled in a condenser and collected separately.
chem9-u1-q22|As the solvent moves up the paper, each component is carried at a different rate depending on its solubility and attraction to the paper, so they separate into spots.
chem9-u1-q23|A pure substance melts and boils at fixed, sharp temperatures; impurities lower the melting point and make it melt over a range of temperatures.
chem9-u1-q24|Main branches: organic (carbon compounds), inorganic (other elements), physical (energy, rates), analytical (identifying substances) and biochemistry (chemistry of living things).
chem9-u1-q25|Mass is the amount of matter in an object (kg) and is the same everywhere; weight is the force of gravity on that mass and changes with gravity.
chem9-u1-q26|A gas spreads to fill the whole container, so its volume is the volume of the container.
chem9-u1-q27|Melting, freezing, boiling and condensing change only the arrangement and energy of the particles, not the substance itself, so they are physical changes.
chem9-u1-q29|Chemistry makes fertilisers (e.g. urea, ammonium compounds) and pesticides that increase crop yields, linking chemistry to agriculture and food supply.
chem9-u1-q30|Oxygen gas contains only O atoms (element); air is several gases mixed in no fixed ratio (mixture); rusting forms a new substance, iron oxide (chemical change).
chem9-u1-q51|Burning forms new substances (CO₂, water, ash) and gives out heat and light, so it is a chemical change.
chem9-u2-q01|Protons and neutrons each have a mass of about 1 u and are in the nucleus; electrons are about 1/1836 as heavy, so almost all the mass is in the nucleus.
chem9-u2-q02|A proton has a charge of +1, a neutron has no charge (0) and an electron has a charge of −1.
chem9-u2-q03|The electron's mass is about 1/1836 of a proton's mass, so it is by far the lightest particle.
chem9-u2-q04|The atomic number Z is the number of protons in the nucleus; each element has its own Z (all carbon atoms have 6 protons).
chem9-u2-q05|The mass number A is the total number of protons and neutrons (nucleons) in the nucleus.
chem9-u2-q06|Neutrons = mass number − atomic number = 14 − 6 = 8.
chem9-u2-q07|A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons, so sodium (Z = 11) has 11 electrons.
chem9-u2-q08|In an atom the number of protons (+) equals the number of electrons (−), so the charges cancel.
chem9-u2-q09|Isotopes are atoms of the same element (same number of protons) with different numbers of neutrons, so their mass numbers differ, e.g. carbon-12 and carbon-14.
chem9-u2-q10|Chemical reactions depend on electrons; isotopes have the same number of protons and electrons, so the same electron arrangement and the same chemistry.
chem9-u2-q11|8 electrons: 2 fill the first shell and the remaining 6 go into the second shell — 2, 6.
chem9-u2-q13|Chlorine (Z = 17) has the arrangement 2, 8, 7, so it has 7 electrons in its outer (valence) shell.
chem9-u2-q14|Elements with the same number of valence electrons react in similar ways, so they are placed in the same group of the periodic table.
chem9-u2-q15|Magnesium (2, 8, 2) has 2 valence electrons, so its electron-dot (Lewis) symbol is Mg with two dots.
chem9-u2-q16|Sodium (2, 8, 1) loses its single outer electron to get the stable arrangement 2, 8; with 11 protons and 10 electrons it becomes Na⁺.
chem9-u2-q17|Chlorine (2, 8, 7) gains one electron to complete its outer shell (2, 8, 8); with 17 protons and 18 electrons it becomes Cl⁻.
chem9-u2-q18|Noble gases already have a full outer shell (2 for He, 8 for the others), so they have no tendency to lose, gain or share electrons and are very unreactive.
chem9-u2-q20|The nucleus is tiny compared with the whole atom (like a pea in a stadium); the electrons move in a large volume around it, so most of the atom is empty space.
chem9-u2-q21|Protons and neutrons each have a relative mass of about 1; the electron's relative mass (about 0.0005) is so small that it is ignored when counting the mass number.
chem9-u2-q22|An ion is an atom or group of atoms that has gained or lost electrons, so the numbers of protons and electrons are unequal and it carries a charge.
chem9-u2-q23|Most elements are a mixture of isotopes; the periodic table gives the average (relative atomic) mass weighted by their abundances, e.g. chlorine 35.5.
chem9-u2-q24|The first shell is closest to the nucleus and can hold at most 2 electrons.
chem9-u2-q25|For the first 20 elements the second (and third) shell can hold up to 8 electrons.
chem9-u2-q26|20 electrons: 2 in the first shell, 8 in the second, 8 in the third and the last 2 in the fourth — 2, 8, 8, 2.
chem9-u2-q27|Metals have 1–3 valence electrons; it takes less energy to lose these few electrons than to gain 5–7, so they form positive ions.
chem9-u2-q28|Non-metals have 5–7 valence electrons, so it is easier to gain or share 1–3 electrons to complete the outer shell, forming negative ions or covalent bonds.
chem9-u2-q29|Neutrons = A − Z = 1 − 1 = 0. Ordinary hydrogen (protium) has just one proton and one electron.
chem9-u2-q30|Magnesium-24 has 12 protons and 12 neutrons in the nucleus, and 12 electrons arranged 2, 8, 2 in three shells.
chem9-u2-q51|The atomic number Z is the number of protons. Nucleons (protons + neutrons) give the mass number.
chem9-u3-q01|Symbols with one capital letter and one small letter are elements (Co = cobalt); two capital letters mean two elements combined (CO = carbon + oxygen = carbon monoxide).
chem9-u3-q02|A formula shows which elements are present (symbols) and the number of atoms of each in one molecule or formula unit (subscripts), e.g. H₂O = 2 H + 1 O.
chem9-u3-q03|Magnesium forms Mg²⁺ and chlorine forms Cl⁻; two Cl⁻ ions are needed to balance one Mg²⁺, so the formula is MgCl₂.
chem9-u3-q04|A pure compound always contains the same elements combined in the same proportion by mass, wherever it comes from (water is always 11.1% H and 88.9% O by mass).
chem9-u3-q05|% O = (mass of O in formula ÷ Mᵣ) × 100 = 16/18 × 100 ≈ 88.9%.
chem9-u3-q06|% H = (2 × 1)/18 × 100 ≈ 11.1% (and 11.1% + 88.9% = 100%).
chem9-u3-q07|One mole is the amount of substance containing 6.02 × 10²³ particles (Avogadro's number) — the same number as atoms in 12 g of carbon-12.
chem9-u3-q08|The molar mass in grams equals the relative formula mass: CO₂ = 12 + 2 × 16 = 44, so 1 mol has a mass of 44 g.
chem9-u3-q09|Moles = mass ÷ molar mass = 22 g ÷ 44 g/mol = 0.50 mol.
chem9-u3-q10|Number of particles = moles × Avogadro's number = 0.50 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 3.01 × 10²³ molecules.
chem9-u3-q11|Atoms are far too small and numerous to count one by one; the mole links a mass we can weigh to a known number of particles.
chem9-u3-q12|2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O has 4 H and 2 O on each side; coefficients are added in front, formulas are not changed.
chem9-u3-q13|Oxygen exists as diatomic molecules (O₂); writing O would describe single atoms, which is not what reacts. Balance with coefficients instead.
chem9-u3-q14|Atoms are not created or destroyed in a reaction, so a balanced equation has the same number of each kind of atom on both sides; therefore total mass is conserved.
chem9-u3-q15|(aq) stands for aqueous — the substance is dissolved in water. Other state symbols: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas.
chem9-u3-q16|In a combination (synthesis) reaction two or more substances join to form one product: magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide.
chem9-u3-q18|In single replacement a more reactive element displaces a less reactive one from a compound: zinc displaces hydrogen from hydrochloric acid.
chem9-u3-q19|In double replacement two compounds exchange ions: Ag⁺ pairs with Cl⁻ to form insoluble AgCl (precipitate), and Na⁺ pairs with NO₃⁻.
chem9-u3-q20|Hydrocarbons burn in oxygen to form CO₂ and water: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O (1 C, 4 H, 4 O on each side).
chem9-u3-q21|Qualitatively an equation tells which substances react and form; quantitatively the coefficients give the ratio of moles (or molecules) that react and are produced.
chem9-u3-q22|The empirical formula gives the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms in a compound, e.g. CH₂O for glucose.
chem9-u3-q23|The molecular formula shows the actual number of each atom in a molecule; it is a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula (C₆H₁₂O₆ = 6 × CH₂O).
chem9-u3-q25|Ionic compounds are named cation first, then anion with -ide/-ate; the charges fix the ratio, so prefixes (mono-, di-) are used only for covalent compounds such as CO₂ (carbon dioxide).
chem9-u3-q28|Mᵣ of Ca(OH)₂ = 40 + 2 × (16 + 1) = 74, so 74 g is 74 ÷ 74 = 1.00 mol.
chem9-u3-q29|The equation 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O shows a 2 : 2 (1 : 1) mole ratio of H₂ to H₂O, so 2 mol H₂ gives 2 mol water.
chem9-u3-q30|Changing a subscript makes a different substance; correct formulas must be kept and only the coefficients in front adjusted.
chem9-u3-q51|Matter is anything that has mass and takes up space (volume). Colour, smell and charge are not required.
chem9-u3-q52|A mole is 6.02 × 10²³ particles. Its mass depends on the substance (12 g only for carbon-12).
chem9-u3-q53|Only coefficients may change when balancing; changing formulas would change the substances themselves.
