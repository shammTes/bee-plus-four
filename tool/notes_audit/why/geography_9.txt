geo9-u1-q02|Physical geography studies the natural environment: landforms (geomorphology), weather and climate, soils, vegetation and animals, and water bodies.
geo9-u1-q03|Human geography studies people and their activities: population, settlements and towns, agriculture, industry, trade, culture and political units.
geo9-u1-q04|Cartography is the science and art of making maps — collecting data, choosing scale and symbols, and drawing the map clearly.
geo9-u1-q05|Climatology studies climate (the average weather of a place over about 30 years); meteorology studies the day-to-day weather and forecasting.
geo9-u1-q06|Geomorphology studies the form of landforms (mountains, valleys, plains) and the processes — tectonic forces, weathering, erosion and deposition — that create them.
geo9-u1-q10|A region is an area of the Earth's surface that has one or more common characteristics (climate, landform, language) that make it different from surrounding areas.
geo9-u1-q12|The human–environment theme looks at how people adapt to, use and modify their environment (terracing, dams) and how the environment limits or helps them (drought, fertile soil).
geo9-u1-q14|Biogeography studies the distribution of plants and animals over the Earth and the factors (climate, soil, people) that explain it.
geo9-u1-q20|Knowing place names is only a starting point; geography explains where things are, why they are there and how they are connected.
geo9-u1-q24|Farming depends on physical factors (rainfall, temperature, soil, slope) and human factors (markets, labour, capital, technology, land tenure).
geo9-u1-q26|In geography the environment includes the natural surroundings (land, air, water, living things) and the human-made (built) surroundings such as towns, roads and farms.
geo9-u1-q28|A process is the series of actions that produces a pattern, e.g. river erosion forms a valley, migration produces a pattern of population.
geo9-u2-q01|The Earth turns once on its axis in about 24 hours (more exactly 23 h 56 min relative to the stars).
geo9-u2-q02|As the Earth rotates, the side facing the Sun has day and the side facing away has night; each place passes through both every 24 hours.
geo9-u2-q03|The Earth takes about 365¼ days to orbit the Sun; the extra ¼ day is collected every four years as 29 February.
geo9-u2-q04|The Earth's axis is tilted about 23½° from the perpendicular to the plane of its orbit (66½° to the plane itself).
geo9-u2-q05|Because the axis stays tilted in the same direction as the Earth orbits, each hemisphere leans towards the Sun for part of the year (summer: higher Sun, longer days) and away from it for the other part (winter).
geo9-u2-q06|On about 21 June the Northern Hemisphere leans most towards the Sun, and the noon Sun is overhead at the Tropic of Cancer, 23½°N.
geo9-u2-q07|On about 22 December the Southern Hemisphere leans most towards the Sun, and the noon Sun is overhead at the Tropic of Capricorn, 23½°S.
geo9-u2-q08|At the equinoxes (about 21 March and 23 September) neither hemisphere leans towards the Sun; the noon Sun is overhead at the equator and day and night are about 12 hours everywhere.
geo9-u2-q09|At the Arctic Circle (66½°N) the Sun does not set on the June solstice (24 hours of daylight) and does not rise on the December solstice (24 hours of darkness).
geo9-u2-q10|The Earth bulges slightly at the equator and is flattened at the poles because of its rotation, so it is an oblate spheroid (geoid), not a perfect sphere.
geo9-u2-q11|Latitude is measured north and south from the equator, which is 0°.
geo9-u2-q12|The poles are the furthest points from the equator, a quarter of a circle away: 90°N and 90°S.
geo9-u2-q13|Longitude is measured east and west from the Prime Meridian (0°), which passes through Greenwich, London.
geo9-u2-q14|The Earth rotates from west to east, so places on the Earth turn towards the Sun first in the east; the Sun appears to rise in the east and set in the west.
geo9-u2-q15|The Sun is at the centre of the solar system; the eight planets, including the Earth, orbit it.
geo9-u2-q16|Without tilt the noon Sun would always be overhead at the equator and every place would have the same Sun angle and day length all year, so there would be no seasons.
geo9-u2-q17|Asmara (about 15°N) leans towards the Sun in June, so days are slightly longer than 12 hours; in December they are slightly shorter. Near the equator the difference is small.
geo9-u2-q18|The circle of illumination is the boundary between the lit (day) half and the dark (night) half of the Earth.
geo9-u2-q19|A year is about 365¼ days, but calendars count whole days; adding one day (29 February) every fourth year keeps the calendar in step with the Earth's orbit.
geo9-u2-q20|Parallels are circles of latitude running east–west, parallel to the equator; meridians are half-circles of longitude running from pole to pole.
geo9-u2-q21|The Earth's circumference is about 40 000 km; 40 000 ÷ 360° ≈ 111 km for each degree of latitude.
geo9-u2-q22|The Earth turns 360° in 24 hours, i.e. 15° of longitude per hour, so time zones are about 15° wide.
geo9-u2-q24|The universe is all of space with all galaxies, stars and matter; the solar system is just the Sun and the planets and other bodies that orbit it.
geo9-u2-q25|The Sun seems to move across the sky from east to west because the Earth rotates from west to east; the Sun is not circling the Earth.
geo9-u2-q26|The overhead Sun moves only between 23½°N and 23½°S during the year; 40°N is outside the tropics, so the noon Sun is never directly overhead there.
geo9-u2-q27|We, the air and everything around us move with the Earth at a steady speed, so we do not feel either rotation or revolution.
geo9-u2-q28|The Earth's orbit is almost circular; in fact the Earth is closest to the Sun in January (northern winter). Seasons are caused by the tilt, which changes the Sun's angle and day length.
geo9-u2-q29|Latitude and season control the angle of the Sun and the length of day, which set how much solar energy a place receives — the basis of its climate (Unit 3).
geo9-u2-q30|An exam globe sketch shows the axis and five lines of latitude: equator (0°), Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn (23½°N/S) and the Arctic and Antarctic Circles (66½°N/S).
geo9-u2-q31|Rotation on the axis causes day and night. Seasons are caused by revolution with a tilted axis; tides mainly by the Moon.
geo9-u3-q01|Weather is the condition of the atmosphere at a place over hours or days; climate is the average weather pattern over a long period (about 30 years).
geo9-u3-q02|The troposphere is the lowest layer of the atmosphere (up to about 8–16 km); it contains almost all water vapour and clouds, and temperature falls with height.
geo9-u3-q03|The elements of weather and climate are temperature, air pressure, wind (speed and direction), humidity, cloud cover and precipitation.
geo9-u3-q04|Near the equator the noon Sun is always high in the sky, so its energy is concentrated on a small area and passes through less atmosphere; day length is about 12 hours all year.
geo9-u3-q05|Temperature falls by about 6.5 °C per 1 000 m of height (lapse rate) because the air is thinner and is heated from the ground; Asmara (2 300 m) is much cooler than Massawa.
geo9-u3-q06|Land heats up and cools down faster than water, so places far from the sea have hotter summers, colder winters and a larger temperature range.
geo9-u3-q07|By day the land heats faster than the sea; air over the land rises (low pressure) and cooler air from the sea (higher pressure) blows in as a sea breeze.
geo9-u3-q08|In a high-pressure area air sinks and warms, so clouds do not form; the weather is usually clear, dry and calm.
geo9-u3-q09|In a low-pressure area air rises and cools; water vapour condenses into clouds, and rain is likely.
geo9-u3-q10|Winds are named after the direction they blow from: a north wind blows from the north towards the south.
geo9-u3-q11|Trade winds blow from the subtropical high-pressure belts (about 30°N and S) towards the equatorial low (ITCZ), turned by the Earth's rotation into north-east and south-east trades.
geo9-u3-q12|The Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone is where the trade winds meet near the equator; the warm air rises strongly, forming tall clouds and heavy rain.
geo9-u3-q13|When moist air is forced to rise over a mountain it cools, water vapour condenses and rain falls on the windward side — orographic (relief) rain.
geo9-u3-q14|After crossing the mountain the air descends on the leeward side, warms and becomes drier, so little rain falls there — the rain shadow.
geo9-u3-q15|Strong heating of the ground warms the air above it; the air rises, cools and forms cumulonimbus clouds, giving heavy afternoon showers, often with thunder.
geo9-u3-q16|When a warm and a cold air mass meet at a front, the lighter warm air is lifted over the denser cold air, cools and gives frontal rain.
geo9-u3-q17|Humidity is the amount of water vapour in the air; relative humidity compares it with the maximum the air could hold at that temperature (as a percentage).
geo9-u3-q18|The dew point is the temperature at which air, if cooled, becomes saturated; further cooling causes condensation as dew, fog or cloud.
geo9-u3-q19|An isobar is a line on a weather map joining places with the same air pressure (reduced to sea level).
geo9-u3-q20|Closely spaced isobars mean pressure changes quickly over a short distance (steep pressure gradient), so winds are strong.
geo9-u3-q21|A thermometer measures air temperature; maximum and minimum thermometers record the highest and lowest temperatures of the day.
geo9-u3-q22|A rain gauge collects rain falling on a known area; the depth of water collected (in mm) is the rainfall.
geo9-u3-q23|Air rising at the equator descends around 30°N and S; sinking air warms and dries, creating subtropical high pressure and deserts such as the Sahara.
geo9-u3-q24|Cumulus (heap) clouds form from rising currents of air (convection); stratus (layer) clouds form when a wide layer of air is cooled gently.
geo9-u3-q25|Evaporation is liquid water changing to water vapour (needs heat); condensation is water vapour changing back to liquid when air is cooled to its dew point.
geo9-u3-q26|Water evaporates from seas and land, condenses into clouds, falls as precipitation and returns to the sea by run-off and groundwater — the water (hydrological) cycle.
geo9-u3-q27|Eritrea is in the tropics, but the high altitude of the highlands lowers temperatures (lapse rate), giving Asmara a mild climate.
geo9-u3-q28|The ITCZ follows the overhead Sun: it moves north in June–August (bringing summer rain to the Eritrean highlands) and south in December–February.
geo9-u3-q29|An anemometer measures wind speed with spinning cups; a wind vane shows wind direction.
geo9-u3-q30|A climate graph shows average monthly temperature as a line and average monthly rainfall as bars, for the twelve months of the year.
geo9-u4-q01|A region is an area that has one or more common features (physical or human) that make it different from neighbouring areas.
geo9-u4-q02|Dividing the world into regions simplifies its complexity, so places can be described, compared, studied and planned for.
geo9-u4-q03|A formal (uniform) region has a common characteristic throughout, e.g. the Sahel climatic belt or an administrative region such as Maekel.
geo9-u4-q04|A functional (nodal) region is an area linked to a central place by its activities: people from surrounding towns travel to Asmara for markets, jobs and services.
geo9-u4-q05|A vernacular (perceptual) region exists in people's minds and everyday speech, such as "the coast" or "the lowlands", with no exact boundary.
geo9-u4-q06|Homogeneous means the same throughout; a formal region is homogeneous for the feature chosen (e.g. same climate or language).
geo9-u4-q07|A node is the central place (city, port, market) around which a functional region is organised.
geo9-u4-q08|Different criteria give different regions, so a place can belong to several at once (e.g. a highland climate region and a Tigrinya-language region).
geo9-u4-q09|Regions are nested: a sub-zoba lies within a zoba, which lies within Eritrea, which lies within the Horn of Africa.
geo9-u4-q10|Coastlines and some political boundaries are sharp; most physical and cultural regions change gradually across transition zones.
geo9-u4-q12|A formal region is drawn by grouping neighbouring places that are similar in the chosen feature.
geo9-u4-q13|Places similar in one feature may differ in another, so you must state which criterion (or criteria) define the region.
geo9-u4-q15|Depending on the criterion, Eritrea can be placed in the Horn of Africa (location), East Africa, the Sahel or the Red Sea region.
geo9-u4-q17|A transition zone is the area between two regions where the characteristics of one change gradually into those of the other.
geo9-u4-q18|At a small scale (world map) regions are large and general, such as East Africa; at a large scale (local map) a region may be a single valley.
geo9-u4-q19|Cultural regions are defined by human features shared by people: language, religion, customs, way of life.
geo9-u4-q21|Physical regions are defined by natural features such as landforms (highlands, plains), climate or vegetation.
geo9-u4-q22|Administrative regions (zobas, sub-zobas) are drawn by governments for administration and services; their boundaries may cut across physical regions.
geo9-u4-q24|Mapmakers may use different criteria, data from different years or different scales, so regional maps of the same area can disagree.
geo9-u4-q25|A hinterland is the area served by a port or town, supplying it with goods and using its services — e.g. the hinterland of Massawa port.
geo9-u4-q27|Regions are mental tools: geographers choose criteria and draw boundaries for a purpose; the lines are not visible in nature.
geo9-u4-q28|Climate regions are formal regions defined using temperature and rainfall data from Unit 3.
geo9-u4-q29|Landform (relief) regions group areas by shape and height of the land: highlands, escarpments, rift valley, lowland plains.
geo9-u4-q30|A good answer names the type of region (formal, functional, vernacular), gives the criterion used and one real example.
geo9-u4-q31|A functional region is organised around a node (city, port) and includes the surrounding area connected to it.
geo9-u5-q01|A landform is a natural feature of the Earth's surface with a characteristic shape, such as a mountain, plateau, plain or valley.
geo9-u5-q02|Continental crust is thicker (30–70 km), less dense and mostly granitic; oceanic crust is thinner (5–10 km), denser and basaltic.
geo9-u5-q03|A mountain rises steeply and high (usually over 600 m) above the surrounding land and often has peaks.
geo9-u5-q04|A plateau is a large area of high land with a fairly flat top, often bounded by steep slopes (escarpments), e.g. the central highlands of Eritrea.
geo9-u5-q05|A plain is an extensive area of low, flat or gently rolling land, e.g. the coastal plains and western lowlands.
geo9-u5-q06|A valley is a long low area between hills or mountains, usually with a river flowing through it.
geo9-u5-q07|A rift valley forms when the crust is pulled apart; a block of land between two parallel faults sinks, forming a long steep-sided valley (e.g. the Danakil depression).
geo9-u5-q09|Weathering is the breakdown of rocks in place by weather, plants and chemicals; erosion is the removal and transport of the broken material by water, wind, ice or waves.
geo9-u5-q10|Agents of erosion are moving forces that carry material away: running water (rivers), wind, glaciers and sea waves.
geo9-u5-q12|The continental shelf is the gently sloping, shallow edge of a continent covered by sea (usually less than 200 m deep), rich in fish.
geo9-u5-q13|Ocean trenches are the deepest parts of the ocean (e.g. Mariana Trench, about 11 km), formed where one plate sinks beneath another.
geo9-u5-q14|A mid-ocean ridge is an underwater mountain chain where plates move apart and molten rock rises to form new ocean floor.
geo9-u5-q15|An abyssal plain is a vast, flat area of deep ocean floor (about 4 000–6 000 m deep) covered with fine sediment.
geo9-u5-q16|The Red Sea is a young ocean forming where the African and Arabian plates are moving apart, so Eritrea's coast shows crust spreading in action.
geo9-u5-q17|Highlands are cooler because of altitude and receive more rain where moist winds rise on windward slopes, which favours farming and settlement.
geo9-u5-q18|Plains usually have deep fertile soils (often river deposits), are easy to plough and irrigate, and are easy to build roads on.
geo9-u5-q19|Fold mountains form where plates push together and compress layers of sedimentary rock, bending them into upfolds and downfolds, e.g. the Alps and Himalayas.
geo9-u5-q20|Block mountains (horsts) form when a block of land between two faults is pushed up, or the land on either side sinks.
geo9-u5-q22|A canyon or gorge is a very deep, narrow valley with steep sides, cut by a river eroding downward, often in a dry region.
geo9-u5-q23|Island arcs are curved chains of volcanic islands that form next to an ocean trench where one oceanic plate sinks under another (e.g. Japan).
geo9-u5-q24|Endogenic (internal) forces such as folding, faulting and volcanism are driven by heat from the Earth's interior.
geo9-u5-q26|Relief is the variation in height and slope of the land surface — highs and lows and how steep the land is — shown on maps by contours and colours.
geo9-u5-q27|New ocean crust forms at ridges and is destroyed in trenches within about 200 million years, while continental crust is too light to sink, so it can be billions of years old.
geo9-u5-q28|Waves erode headlands into cliffs; in sheltered bays they deposit sand and shingle to form beaches.
geo9-u5-q29|Eritrea's landform regions: central highland plateau, eastern escarpment, eastern coastal plain and Danakil depression, western lowlands, and the Dahlak islands on the continental shelf.
geo9-u6-q01|A map scale gives the relationship between a distance on the map and the corresponding distance on the ground.
geo9-u6-q02|Scale can be written as a statement (1 cm represents 1 km), a representative fraction (1:100 000) or a linear (bar) scale.
geo9-u6-q03|A representative fraction such as 1:50 000 means one unit on the map (cm, mm, inch) represents 50 000 of the same unit on the ground.
geo9-u6-q05|4 cm × 50 000 = 200 000 cm on the ground = 2 000 m = 2 km.
geo9-u6-q06|A large-scale map (e.g. 1:10 000) shows a small area in great detail, such as a town plan.
geo9-u6-q07|A small-scale map (e.g. 1:1 000 000) shows a large area, such as a country or continent, with little detail.
geo9-u6-q08|1:10 000 is the larger fraction (1/10 000 > 1/100 000), so objects appear bigger and more detailed — it is the larger scale.
geo9-u6-q10|Convert to the same unit: 10 km = 1 000 000 cm. 5 cm : 1 000 000 cm = 1 : 200 000.
geo9-u6-q11|2 km = 2 × 100 000 cm = 200 000 cm, so 1 cm to 2 km is 1 : 200 000.
geo9-u6-q12|A representative fraction compares the same units; dividing centimetres by kilometres gives a wrong number unless both are converted to the same unit.
geo9-u6-q13|Halving the size of the map makes every map distance half as long, so 1 cm now represents twice the ground distance: 1 : 100 000.
geo9-u6-q14|Doubling the map size makes map distances twice as long, so 1 cm represents half the ground distance: 1 : 50 000.
geo9-u6-q15|A globe keeps the true shape and scale in all directions; a flat map must stretch parts of the curved surface, so its scale is not the same everywhere.
geo9-u6-q16|Walkers need detail (paths, streams, buildings), so they use large-scale maps such as 1:25 000 or 1:50 000.
geo9-u6-q17|An atlas climate map covers a whole continent or the world on one page, so it is a small-scale map.
geo9-u6-q18|In an RF both numbers are in the same unit, so the units cancel; 1:50 000 means 1 cm : 50 000 cm, never 1 cm : 50 000 km.
geo9-u6-q19|Measured map distances only make sense after you know the scale; read it first, then convert the measurement to a real distance.
geo9-u6-q21|A verbal (statement) scale such as "1 cm represents 5 km" is easy to read and understand.
geo9-u6-q22|A verbal scale gives units, so it becomes wrong if the map is enlarged or reduced, and users may confuse cm, m and km.
geo9-u6-q24|At 1:1 the map would be the same size as the area it shows, so it would be impossible to use or carry.
geo9-u6-q26|Shrinking the map changes map distances, so a written scale like "1 cm to 1 km" is no longer true; a bar scale shrinks with the map and stays correct.
geo9-u6-q27|Both parts of the ratio are measured in the same unit, so the units cancel; it works with cm, inches or any unit.
geo9-u6-q28|3 km = 300 000 cm; on a 1:50 000 map: 300 000 ÷ 50 000 = 6 cm.
geo9-u6-q29|Scales are classified as large, medium or small by the size of the RF: large scale shows more detail of a small area, small scale shows a large area with less detail.
