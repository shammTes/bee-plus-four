bio9-u1-q01|Biology (from Greek bios = life, logos = study) is the science that studies living things: their structure, functions, growth, origin and relationships.
bio9-u1-q02|Total magnification = eyepiece magnification × objective magnification = 10 × 40 = 400 times.
bio9-u1-q03|Cell theory: (1) all living things are made of one or more cells; (2) the cell is the basic unit of structure and function; (3) new cells come only from existing cells by division.
bio9-u1-q04|Prokaryotic cells (bacteria) have no nucleus — their DNA lies free in the cytoplasm — and no membrane-bound organelles; eukaryotic cells have a nucleus and organelles such as mitochondria.
bio9-u1-q05|Mitochondria are the site of aerobic respiration, where glucose is oxidised and the energy is stored in ATP.
bio9-u1-q06|Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll, which absorbs light energy used to make glucose from CO₂ and water.
bio9-u1-q07|Plant cells have a cellulose cell wall, chloroplasts and a large permanent sap vacuole; animal cells have none of these.
bio9-u1-q08|The cell membrane is partially permeable: it lets water, oxygen and some small molecules pass but controls the entry and exit of other substances.
bio9-u1-q09|In diffusion particles spread from where they are more concentrated to where they are less concentrated, using their own kinetic energy, so the cell spends no energy.
bio9-u1-q10|Osmosis is the movement of water molecules from a dilute solution (more water) to a concentrated solution (less water) through a partially permeable membrane.
bio9-u1-q11|Active transport moves substances against their concentration gradient (low to high), so the cell must use energy from respiration (ATP) to work carrier proteins.
bio9-u1-q12|Strong salt water is more concentrated than the cell contents, so water leaves the cell by osmosis and the animal cell shrinks and wrinkles (crenation).
bio9-u1-q13|Distilled water is more dilute than the cell sap, so water enters by osmosis; the cell swells and becomes turgid, but the strong cellulose wall prevents it from bursting.
bio9-u1-q14|Homeostasis is keeping the internal environment constant, e.g. body temperature about 37 °C, or blood water and glucose levels kept within narrow limits.
bio9-u1-q15|Aerobic respiration uses oxygen to break down glucose completely, releasing energy: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy.
bio9-u1-q16|Excretion removes waste made by the body's chemical reactions (CO₂, urea); egestion removes undigested food that never entered the cells.
bio9-u1-q17|Bacteria are grouped by shape: cocci (spheres), bacilli (rods) and spirilla (spirals).
bio9-u1-q18|Useful bacteria include Lactobacillus (yoghurt and cheese), nitrogen-fixing bacteria in root nodules, and bacteria that decompose sewage or produce biogas.
bio9-u1-q19|A virus is not a cell: it has no cytoplasm or metabolism and can reproduce only inside a living host cell, so it is on the border between living and non-living.
bio9-u1-q20|A virus is a core of nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) surrounded by a protein coat (capsid); some also have a fatty envelope.
bio9-u1-q21|Diseases caused by viruses include influenza, measles, HIV/AIDS, rabies, polio and the common cold.
bio9-u1-q22|Antibiotics attack structures found only in bacteria (cell wall, bacterial ribosomes); viruses have none of these, so antibiotics do not work against them.
bio9-u1-q23|Most cell parts are transparent; stains such as iodine or methylene blue colour particular structures (e.g. the nucleus) so they show up clearly under the microscope.
bio9-u1-q24|Magnification makes the image larger; resolution is the ability to show two points close together as separate. A larger image is useless if it is blurred (poor resolution).
bio9-u1-q25|Ribosomes join amino acids together to make proteins.
bio9-u1-q26|Cytoplasm is the jelly-like substance inside the cell membrane (outside the nucleus) where most of the cell's chemical reactions take place.
bio9-u1-q27|A permeable membrane (like a cell wall) lets almost everything pass; a partially permeable membrane (cell membrane) lets small molecules like water through but not large ones like starch.
bio9-u1-q28|Bacteria are prokaryotes: their DNA is a circular strand lying in the cytoplasm (the nucleoid region), not enclosed in a nuclear membrane.
bio9-u1-q30|Coughing spreads pathogens in droplets that land on hands and surfaces; washing hands with soap removes them before they reach the mouth, nose or other people.
bio9-u1-q31|Osmosis is specifically the movement of water across a partially permeable membrane. Salt, sand and light do not move by osmosis.
bio9-u1-q32|A virus is just genetic material in a protein coat; it has no organelles and cannot live outside a host cell, so it is not a full cell or part of the five kingdoms.
bio9-u2-q01|Classification sorts the huge variety of organisms into groups, gives each a universal name, makes them easier to study and shows how they are related.
bio9-u2-q02|Binomial nomenclature (Linnaeus) gives each species a two-part Latin name: the genus followed by the species, e.g. Homo sapiens.
bio9-u2-q03|The genus name begins with a capital letter and the species name with a small letter, and both are written in italics (or underlined): Homo sapiens.
bio9-u2-q04|A dichotomous key gives two contrasting statements at each step (e.g. "has wings / has no wings"); following the choices leads to the name of the organism.
bio9-u2-q05|The five-kingdom system: Monera (bacteria), Protista (single-celled eukaryotes), Fungi, Plantae and Animalia.
bio9-u2-q06|Members of Monera are prokaryotes: single cells without a nucleus or membrane-bound organelles.
bio9-u2-q07|Fungal cell walls are made of chitin, while plant cell walls are made of cellulose.
bio9-u2-q08|Fungi cannot photosynthesise; they release digestive enzymes onto dead matter (saprotrophs) or a host (parasites) and absorb the digested food.
bio9-u2-q09|Protista are mostly single-celled eukaryotes, e.g. Amoeba, Paramecium, Plasmodium and many algae.
bio9-u2-q10|Plant cell walls are made of cellulose, which gives the cell strength and shape.
bio9-u2-q11|Animals are heterotrophs: they take food into the body (ingestion) and then digest it internally.
bio9-u2-q12|Kingdom → phylum → class → order → family → genus → species; each level contains fewer, more closely related organisms. ("King Philip Came Over For Good Soup".)
bio9-u2-q13|Species in the same genus share many features and a recent common ancestor, e.g. Panthera leo (lion) and Panthera tigris (tiger).
bio9-u2-q15|Mosses have no true xylem and phloem to carry water, so they stay small and grow in damp places; they also need water for fertilisation.
bio9-u2-q16|Ferns reproduce by spores formed in sori under the leaves; the spores grow into a small gametophyte that produces gametes.
bio9-u2-q17|Gymnosperms (e.g. pines) have seeds that lie exposed on cone scales — "naked seeds" — not enclosed in an ovary or fruit.
bio9-u2-q18|Angiosperms have flowers for reproduction, and their seeds develop inside an ovary that becomes a fruit.
bio9-u2-q19|Vertebrates have a backbone (vertebral column) of vertebrae enclosing the spinal cord; invertebrates do not.
bio9-u2-q20|Mammals have hair or fur and feed their young on milk from mammary glands; they also have external ears and are warm-blooded.
bio9-u2-q21|Only birds have feathers, used for flight and insulation.
bio9-u2-q22|Amphibians have thin, moist skin through which they can exchange gases; they usually lay jelly-coated eggs in water and the larvae (tadpoles) live in water.
bio9-u2-q23|Reptiles lay eggs with a leathery, waterproof shell, so the eggs can develop on land without drying out.
bio9-u2-q24|Fish take oxygen from water using gills, which have many thin filaments with a good blood supply.
bio9-u2-q25|Local names differ between languages and regions; a single Latin scientific name is understood by scientists everywhere and refers to only one species.
bio9-u2-q26|Yeast is a single-celled fungus: it has a nucleus, a chitin wall and feeds by absorption.
bio9-u2-q27|Amoeba is a single-celled eukaryote with a nucleus, so it belongs to Protista.
bio9-u2-q28|E. coli is a bacterium (prokaryote), so it belongs to Monera.
bio9-u2-q29|Keys work only if you choose each statement from features you can actually see or measure (number of legs, wings), not from what the organism seems to resemble.
bio9-u2-q31|Monera (bacteria) are prokaryotes: single cells with no true nucleus. Plants and mammals are eukaryotes; viruses are not placed in any kingdom.
bio9-u3-q01|Glucose gives quick energy in respiration; starch (plants) and glycogen (animals) store glucose; cellulose builds plant cell walls.
bio9-u3-q02|Glucose (also fructose and galactose) is a monosaccharide — a single sugar unit that cannot be split into smaller sugars.
bio9-u3-q03|Iodine solution is yellow-brown and turns blue-black when starch is present.
bio9-u3-q05|Proteins are built from about 20 kinds of amino acids joined in chains.
bio9-u3-q06|Enzymes are proteins that act as biological catalysts: they speed up reactions without being used up.
bio9-u3-q07|DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) carries the genetic code — the instructions for making proteins — in the nucleus.
bio9-u3-q08|Photosynthesis needs carbon dioxide and water as raw materials, light as the energy source and chlorophyll to absorb the light.
bio9-u3-q09|Carbon dioxide + water → (light, chlorophyll) → glucose + oxygen.
bio9-u3-q10|Photosynthesis takes place in the chloroplasts, mainly in the palisade cells of the leaf.
bio9-u3-q11|Respiration breaks down food (glucose) to release energy in the form of ATP, which cells use for movement, growth and active transport.
bio9-u3-q12|Aerobic respiration uses oxygen and releases much energy (CO₂ + water); anaerobic respiration happens without oxygen and releases little energy, producing lactic acid (animals) or ethanol + CO₂ (yeast).
bio9-u3-q13|Photosynthesis uses CO₂ + water and light to make glucose + O₂; respiration uses glucose + O₂ and releases CO₂ + water + energy — the reverse overall equation.
bio9-u3-q14|A producer is an autotroph — usually a green plant — that makes its own food by photosynthesis and starts every food chain.
bio9-u3-q15|A primary consumer is a herbivore that feeds directly on producers, e.g. a goat eating grass.
bio9-u3-q16|Only about 10% of the energy at one trophic level is passed to the next; the rest is lost as heat from respiration, in movement and in undigested waste, so each level holds less energy.
bio9-u3-q18|Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) break down dead organisms and wastes, returning minerals such as nitrates to the soil for plants to reuse.
bio9-u3-q19|Energy enters as sunlight and is lost as heat at each level, so it flows one way; the atoms (carbon, nitrogen) are passed round again by decomposers, so matter cycles.
bio9-u3-q20|A trophic level is the feeding position of an organism in a food chain: producer, primary consumer, secondary consumer, etc.
bio9-u3-q21|Every food chain must start with a producer that captures light energy; a goat is a consumer that eats plants.
bio9-u3-q22|Humans eat both plants and animals: eating bread makes us primary consumers, eating meat makes us secondary (or higher) consumers.
bio9-u3-q23|Humans have no enzyme to digest cellulose, so it passes through as fibre (roughage).
bio9-u3-q24|Fat releases about twice as much energy per gram as carbohydrate, so seeds and animals store energy compactly as oils and fats.
bio9-u3-q25|Metabolism is the sum of all chemical reactions in an organism, both building up and breaking down.
bio9-u3-q26|Anabolism builds large molecules from small ones (e.g. proteins from amino acids, uses energy); catabolism breaks large molecules down (e.g. respiration, releases energy).
bio9-u3-q27|Without decomposers dead bodies and wastes would pile up, and minerals locked in them would never return to the soil, so plants would run short of nutrients.
bio9-u3-q29|Nucleic acids (DNA, RNA) contain phosphorus in their phosphate groups; phospholipids in membranes also contain phosphorus.
bio9-u3-q31|Photosynthesis makes glucose and releases oxygen as a by-product. It does not release nitrogen, oil or protein.
bio9-u4-q01|An adaptation is a feature (structure, function or behaviour) that helps an organism survive and reproduce in its environment.
bio9-u4-q03|Water evaporates from leaf surfaces; small leaves (or spines) have a smaller surface area, so less water is lost by transpiration.
bio9-u4-q04|A thick waxy cuticle is waterproof and reduces water loss from the leaf surface.
bio9-u4-q05|Stomata sunk in pits trap a layer of moist air above them; the smaller concentration gradient slows the diffusion of water vapour out of the leaf.
bio9-u4-q06|Deep tap roots reach water far underground; wide shallow roots quickly absorb the little rain that falls before it evaporates.
bio9-u4-q08|Salt-tolerant plants (halophytes) excrete excess salt through salt glands or store water in fleshy (succulent) tissues to dilute it.
bio9-u4-q09|In windy places plants grow low and flexible with small leaves, so they are less likely to break and lose less water.
bio9-u4-q10|In cold places plants grow close to the ground (out of the wind), complete their life cycle in the short warm season, and some have dark pigments that absorb heat.
bio9-u4-q11|Thick fur traps a layer of still air, a poor conductor of heat, so less body heat is lost.
bio9-u4-q12|Blubber is a thick layer of fat under the skin of marine mammals; it insulates against cold water and also stores energy.
bio9-u4-q13|Small ears have a small surface area, so animals in cold regions (e.g. Arctic fox) lose less heat from them.
bio9-u4-q14|Large thin ears with many blood vessels (e.g. fennec fox) give a large surface for losing heat to the air.
bio9-u4-q15|Being active at night avoids the hottest part of the day, reducing water loss and overheating.
bio9-u4-q16|In hibernation the animal's metabolic rate, heart rate and body temperature fall very low, so stored fat lasts through the cold season when food is scarce.
bio9-u4-q19|Underground the temperature and humidity stay fairly constant, so burrows protect animals from extreme heat, cold and drying out.
bio9-u4-q20|Evaporating water (sweat, or moisture from the tongue and airways when panting) absorbs latent heat from the body, cooling it.
bio9-u4-q24|Succulent plants (e.g. cactus, aloe) have thick fleshy stems or leaves that store water for dry periods.
bio9-u4-q26|A behavioural adaptation is something the organism does (being nocturnal, migrating); a structural adaptation is a body feature (thick fur, spines).
