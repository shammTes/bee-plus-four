# Round 4 - history_11: expanding short lessons (units 10-12)

@lesson hist11-u10-t2
T: From Austria to Poland: the countdown to war, 1938–1939
B: **Step 1 – Austria (13 March 1938).** Hitler pressed Austria to admit German troops and annexed it; Britain and France did nothing serious. | **Step 2 – the Sudetenland (September 1938).** Germany demanded this German-speaking area of Czechoslovakia and threatened war. At the **Munich Conference** Britain and France agreed (appeasement) in return for Hitler's promise to make no more demands. | **Step 3 – the rest of Czechoslovakia (March 1939).** Hitler broke his promise; Britain and France now saw his ambition had no limit. | **Step 4 – Danzig and the guarantee.** When Hitler demanded the Polish port of Danzig, Britain and France promised to protect Poland. | **Step 5 – the Nazi–Soviet Pact (23 August 1939).** To avoid a two-front war, Hitler agreed with Stalin not to attack each other and secretly divided Eastern Europe: Finland, the Baltic states and eastern Poland to the USSR, western Poland to Germany. | **Step 6 – the invasion (1 September 1939).** Germany attacked Poland; two days later (3 September) Britain and France declared war. | **Memory trick:** "Austria, Sudeten, Prague, Pact, Poland" (A-S-P-P-P). | **Exam tip:** The invasion of Poland is the *immediate* cause; appeasement, Versailles and fascist expansion are the *long-term* causes.

@lesson hist11-u10-t5
T: The war in Asia and the Pacific, step by step
B: **Pearl Harbor (7 December 1941).** Japan attacked the US base in Hawaii and, the same day, the Philippines and British Malaya; the USA declared war the next day and Hitler declared war on the USA three days later. | **Japanese expansion.** By spring 1942 Japan held almost all of Southeast Asia (including the Dutch East Indies) and much of the western Pacific. In the USA over 110,000 Japanese Americans were put in camps. | **The turn.** Allied forces stopped the Japanese advance and pushed them back island by island. | **The end.** Truman (president after Roosevelt died on 12 April 1945) feared huge losses in an invasion; atomic bombs fell on **Hiroshima (6 August)** and **Nagasaki (9 August)**, and Japan surrendered on **14 August 1945**. | **Memory trick:** "Pearl Harbor 1941 → Hiroshima 6/8 → Nagasaki 9/8 → Surrender 14/8."

@lesson hist11-u10-t7
T: The economic consequences of WWII in detail
B: **Ruin in Europe and Japan.** Britain, France, Italy and Germany were bankrupted. Industrial regions everywhere except North America were destroyed; cities, roads, railways and communications were levelled, and farming was so damaged that Europe's **45 million homeless survivors** depended on US aid. The war cost more than **one trillion US dollars**. | **Unemployment.** Millions of returning soldiers and prisoners of war needed jobs. | **A new balance of power.** The USA came out of the war rich and strong, and the USSR became a military superpower, while Britain and France were too weak to keep their empires. | **Science pushed forward.** War research produced **radar**, the **jet engine**, mass production of **DDT** against malaria and **penicillin** for wounds – but also the atomic bomb, a terrifying example of technology used for destruction. | **Memory trick:** "Broke Europe, Rich America, Radar–Jet–DDT–Penicillin–Bomb."

@lesson hist11-u10-t8
T: The social consequences of WWII in detail
B: **Human loss.** Over **50 million** soldiers and civilians died, and over **21 million** people were left homeless. | **The Holocaust.** The Nazis believed in "Aryan" superiority and planned to exterminate the Jews. SS death squads shot almost one million; from 1942 Jews were sent by train to six death camps in occupied Poland, the largest being **Auschwitz**. About 30 % of arrivals were forced to work; the rest, especially the old, women and children, were gassed. In all **5–6 million Jews** were killed (about 90 % of the Jews of Poland, the Baltic states and Germany), as well as millions of Roma and Slavs. | **Forced migration.** After the war over **10 million Germans** were expelled from Hungary, Czechoslovakia and Romania into West Germany. | **Women.** Women fought in Soviet combat units and worked in industry and mines elsewhere, increasing their role in economic and military life. | **Memory trick:** "50 million dead, 21 million homeless, 6 million Jews, 10 million expelled."

@lesson hist11-u11-t1
T: The Battle of Keren (15–27 March 1941)
B: **Why the Italians lost.** Weakened by earlier defeats and with low morale, they lost thousands of Eritrean conscripts who defected, despite the strong terrain and reinforcements. After 12 days of the fiercest fighting in East Africa, **Keren fell on 27 March 1941**. | **Memory trick:** "Kassala → Agordat (1 Feb) → Barentu → Keren (27 March)."

@lesson hist11-u12-t3
T: Why the 1952 constitution was called progressive
B: **Its main features.** 99 articles, ratified on **10 July 1952**; a clear **separation of powers** between legislative, executive and judicial branches; Tigrinya and Arabic as official languages; Eritrea's own flag, seal and coat of arms. | **Rights explained.** Equality before the law; life, liberty and security; owning property; free expression and freedom of religion; peaceful assembly and association; no arrest without a court order; a fair trial. These protected citizens against arbitrary government. | **Its limit.** Women were excluded from voting – the rights applied to male citizens. | **Contrast.** Ethiopia was an absolute monarchy, so the Eritrean constitution was far more democratic, which is why Ethiopia worked to dismantle it. | **Memory trick:** "Separate powers, protect people, two languages, one flag."

@lesson hist11-u12-t8
T: The 1957 student protest, step by step
B: **Cause.** Ethiopia introduced **Amharic** as a subject and medium of instruction and banned Tigrinya and Arabic (1956), seen as an attack on Eritrean identity. | **Start (May 1957).** Students of Haile Selassie Secondary School in Asmara boycotted class. | **Inspiration.** The radio broadcasts of **Woldeab Woldemariam** from Cairo calling for independence. | **Spread.** Within days students from other schools and towns joined, protesting against the violation of Eritrean autonomy. | **Response.** The government cracked down by force and jailed the organizers. | **Significance.** The protests showed that young Eritreans rejected annexation and prepared the ground for the ELM. | **Memory trick:** "Language law → Asmara boycott → Cairo radio → crackdown."

@lesson hist11-u12-t9
T: Why the 1958 strike matters
B: **Scale.** Over 20,000 workers marched in Asmara, joined by workers in Assab, Massawa and Keren; shops closed for three days. **Result.** The union was banned and organizers were jailed. **Significance.** With peaceful protest crushed, Eritreans turned to underground organizations such as the ELM (November 1958).

@lesson hist11-u12-t12
T: The ELF and the start of the armed struggle
B: **Founding.** The ELF was formed in **Cairo in July 1960**; founders included Idris Mohammed Adem (former chairman of the Assembly), Taha Mohammed Nur and Idris Gelawdios. **Armed struggle.** It began on **1 September 1961** at **Mount Adal** under **Hamid Idris Awate**. **Annexation.** On 14 November 1962 the Assembly was forced at gunpoint to dissolve the federation – a breach of Resolution 390A(V) that the UN ignored.
