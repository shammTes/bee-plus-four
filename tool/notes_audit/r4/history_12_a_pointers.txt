# Round 4 - history_12: the content that the teacher cards point to, written out in full.

@after hist12-u3-c10
T: Background: what Pan-Africanism was (Grade 11 recap)
B: **Step 1 – the idea.** Pan-Africanism was the idea of uniting all black Africans, whatever their national boundaries, in a common struggle for dignity, freedom and self-rule. | **Step 2 – where it began.** It started among people of African descent in the United States and the Caribbean. Its two best-known leaders were **W.E.B. Du Bois** and **Marcus Garvey**. | **Step 3 – the cultural side.** At the same time the **Negritude** movement (associated with Léopold Senghor) stressed the uniqueness and value of African culture and history. | **Step 4 – into Africa.** Between the wars, a new generation of African leaders educated in Europe and the USA – Jomo Kenyatta (Kenya), Léopold Senghor (Senegal), Nnamdi Azikiwe (Nigeria) and later Kwame Nkrumah (Ghana) – took these ideas home and used them to demand independence. | **Step 5 – the link to the OAU.** Once many states were independent in the early 1960s, the Pan-African dream of unity became a practical question: a full federation at once (Casablanca bloc) or gradual cooperation (Monrovia bloc). The compromise was the OAU, founded in Addis Ababa in 1963. | **Memory trick:** "Du Bois and Garvey dreamed it, Nkrumah pushed it, the OAU built it."

@after hist12-u5-c07
T: 5.7 Socio-economic changes in the liberated areas (1977 onwards)
B: From 1977, as the liberated areas grew, the Eritrean Revolution widened its social, economic and political programmes. There were five main programmes. | **1. Land reform.** In the mid-1970s the ELF and EPLF began land reform in the highlands and the eastern coastal plains, working within the village-owned **Diessa** system, in which every family head had an equal right to use village land. The reform made land holding fairer and more democratic. In the old system land went by family household, and unmarried people and especially women were left out. After the reform the **unmarried, women and non-original inhabitants (maekelay aliet)** gained land rights. On the coastal plains the EPLF distributed land to thousands of landless peasants. | **2. Health.** Between 1975 and 1978 many doctors, nurses and laboratory technicians joined the fronts, so health services expanded quickly. The focus was **prevention**: mobile medical teams and "barefoot doctors" were sent across the liberated and semi-liberated areas, and local midwives were trained. In the 1980s the EPLF ran the **Orotta central hospital**, other hospitals and clinics, and frontline surgical units serving both fighters and civilians. Its frontline surgery became widely respected. | **3. Education.** The EPLF offered primary, secondary, technical and adult education and developed its own national curriculum. Village schools and adult literacy classes were opened, and the **Revolution School** in Sahel housed and taught thousands of students in **Tigrinya, Arabic and Tigre**. In the 1980s boarding schools were opened in several liberated areas, some run by army divisions. The ELF opened **Tsibah School** at the end of 1977, and its Social Affairs Bureau ran adult education and elementary schools. | **4. Women's participation.** The fronts worked to raise women's political awareness and to break traditional norms that kept women out of public life. The EPLF fought for women's equal rights in **marriage, divorce, inheritance, land ownership, politics and education**, under the slogan **"Equality through practical participation."** Women made up **over 30 % of the army by the 1980s**, and hundreds of thousands more served in mass organizations at home and abroad. | **5. Public associations.** By 1979 national unions of **women, farmers, workers and students** had been formed. They organized people, raised political awareness, sent recruits and raised money and materials. Professional bodies such as the Eritrean Medical Association and the Research and Information Center on Eritrea (RICE) also helped. The **Eritrean Relief Association (ERA, founded 1975)** collected and distributed aid to drought and war victims, and played a major role during the **1984/85 famine**. | **Memory trick – "L-H-E-W-P":** Land, Health, Education, Women, Public associations. | **Exam tip:** For "explain the socio-economic changes of the Revolution", give one concrete fact for each of the five programmes (e.g. Diessa land rights for women; Orotta hospital; Revolution School; 30 % women fighters; ERA in the 1984/85 famine).

@after hist12-u5-c09
T: 5.9 Soviet intervention, step by step
B: **Step 1 – the old alliance.** In the Cold War, Haile Selassie's Ethiopia was allied with the United States, and this lasted until 1974. | **Step 2 – the break.** After the Derg overthrew Haile Selassie in 1974 and adopted its own nationalist programme, relations with the USA declined and broke off completely by **July 1977**. | **Step 3 – the new partner.** The Derg turned to the Socialist camp, above all the Soviet Union, which allied with Ethiopia for its own strategic interests. In **May 1977** Mengistu made his first trip to Moscow and was promised military aid worth billions of dollars. | **Step 4 – the scale of the aid.** From spring–summer 1977 the USSR sent arms worth **$1–2 billion**, about **12,000 Cuban combat troops** and **1,500 military advisers**. This support also helped Ethiopia defeat Somalia in the Ethio-Somali (Ogaden) War. Libya gave millions of dollars in military aid, and South Yemen sent pilots and mechanized units. | **Step 5 – the effect on Eritrea.** This intervention tilted the balance of power towards the Ethiopian regime. Facing massive firepower with almost no outside support, the Eritrean fronts made a **strategic withdrawal** from the liberated areas: the **EPLF to the mountains of Sahel** and the **ELF to the western lowlands (Barka)**. | **Step 6 – not a rout.** The withdrawal was fought step by step and inflicted heavy losses on the advancing army – for example at the battles of **Elabered** and **Maemide**. | **Memory trick:** "1974 Derg, 1977 Moscow, 1978 withdrawal." | **Exam tip:** When asked why the fronts withdrew, name the three outside helpers of the Derg (USSR, Cuba, plus Libya/South Yemen) and give the figures for Soviet arms and Cuban troops.

@after hist12-u5-c13
T: 5.12 From defence to offence (1984–1987), step by step
B: **Step 1 – the turning point.** Up to 1983, after the strategic withdrawal, the Ethiopian army attacked and the Eritrean Revolution defended, with the balance of power heavily in Ethiopia's favour. After the defeat of the **Red Star Campaign** and the **Stealth Offensive**, the war reached a **stalemate**. From 1984 to 1987 the EPLF took the initiative while the Derg defended and counter-attacked. | **Step 2 – 1984 victories.** In **January 1984** the EPLF captured **Tessenei and Alighider**. In **March 1984** it routed the **Wuqaw Command** on the north-eastern Sahel front: the Derg lost about **7,000 soldiers** (killed or captured) and over four-fifths of its equipment there. | **Step 3 – the Asmara air base raid.** Two months later EPLF commandos attacked the Ethiopian air base in Asmara and destroyed **33 war planes**. | **Step 4 – Barentu (1985).** On **5 July 1985** the EPLF liberated **Barentu** and a large area around it. The Derg saw this as a strategic threat, pulled forces from the Ogaden and counter-attacked, retaking Barentu, Tessenei and Alighider. | **Step 5 – the last Derg offensive.** On **10 October 1985** the Derg launched its eighth and last offensive, **Operation Bahri Negash**. It failed within two months and cost about **17,000 troops**. | **Step 6 – result.** Growing EPLF guerrilla and commando operations everywhere stopped the Derg from starting new offensives. By the **end of 1987** the liberation war had reached the stage of **strategic offensive**, which led to Afabet (1988) and finally independence. | **Memory trick:** "Tessenei – Wuqaw – Air base – Barentu – Bahri Negash" (T-W-A-B-B), in date order 1984–85. | **Exam tip:** Use the three phases in essays: strategic defence (1978–83) → stalemate (1984–87) → strategic offensive (1988–91).

@after hist12-u5-c18
T: Why 1965–1968 counts as its own phase: the zonal commands
B: **Step 1 – the decision.** On **20 July 1965** the ELF Supreme Council met in **Kassala** (eastern Sudan) and divided the Eritrean Liberation Army (ELA) into zonal (regional) commands, copying the **Algerian Revolution**. There were four zones at first, and a **5th zone (Hamassien)** was added in 1966 for the growing number of Tigrinya-speaking fighters. | **Step 2 – the official reasons.** Most units were stuck in the western lowlands, so they posed little threat to the Ethiopian regime, and feeding them was a heavy burden on the local people. | **Step 3 – the zones.** 1st Barka (Mahmud Dinai), 2nd Sahel and Senhit (Omar Izaz), 3rd Seraye and Akeleguzai (Abdelkerim Ahmed), 4th Semhar and Denkel (Mohammed Ali Omaro), 5th Hamassien (Wolday Kahsai). Every commander was a native of his zone. A **Revolutionary Command** in Kassala, led by Seid Adem, was supposed to link the Supreme Council with the zones. | **Step 4 – positive effects.** Fighting spread over a wider and more populated area, Ethiopian forces were overstretched, the Revolution's reputation grew and more recruits joined. | **Step 5 – negative effects.** The zones were regional and parochial: they hardened ethnic, regional and religious divisions. Commanders kept the taxes they collected, recruited their own fighters despite the 30 % rule, and became like warlords competing with each other. There was no proper channel between the Supreme Council, the Revolutionary Command and the zones, and the Supreme Council itself was split by rivalries. | **Step 6 – why it ended.** These failures provoked the reform movement of fighters (the Anseba Conference of 1968 and the Adobha Conference of 1969), which abolished the zones – so 1965–68 is treated as a separate stage. | **Memory trick:** "Kassala split it, Adobha fixed it."
