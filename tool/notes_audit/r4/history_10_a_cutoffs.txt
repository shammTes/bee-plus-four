# Round 4 - history_10: completing the teacher cards' unfinished points

@after hist10-u1-n01
T: Completing this point: what characterized the Middle Ages
B: **The three features, explained.** (1) **Feudalism** – a system in which kings granted land (fiefs) to lords in return for military service, and serfs worked the land in return for protection. (2) **Slow economic, cultural and intellectual development** in the early centuries, after the fall of the Western Roman Empire in 476: trade and towns declined and learning survived mainly in monasteries. (3) **The rise of strong monarchies** in the later centuries, as kings slowly took power back from the lords. | **The three sub-periods.** Early Middle Ages (476–1000), High Middle Ages (1000–1300) and Late Middle Ages (1300–1450). | **Memory trick:** "F-S-M: Feudalism, Slow growth, Monarchs." | **Exam tip:** "Medieval" comes from Latin *medium aevum*, "the middle age" – the period between ancient Rome and the Renaissance.

@after hist10-u1-n06
T: Completing this point: why Western Europe revived in the High Middle Ages
B: **Cause 1 – security.** The Moor, Magyar and Viking invasions ended, so farming and trade could recover. | **Cause 2 – the agricultural revolution.** New tools and methods (the heavy iron plough, the horse collar, water mills and the **three-field system**) produced more food, and the population grew. | **Cause 3 – trade and towns.** Surplus food and peace revived trade fairs and towns; merchants and craftsmen formed **guilds**. | **Cause 4 – learning.** The first **universities** (Bologna, Paris, Oxford) appeared, and the Crusades brought contact with Byzantine and Muslim learning. | **Memory trick:** "Safe – Fed – Trading – Learning."

@after hist10-u1-n09
T: Completing this point: Gothic cathedrals
B: **When.** The Gothic style appeared in the **12th century** and was perfected in the 13th. The 11th and 12th centuries saw an explosion of cathedral building. | **What makes a building Gothic.** Pointed arches, ribbed vaults and **flying buttresses** carried the weight outward, so walls could be thinner and higher, with tall spires and huge **stained-glass windows** that filled the church with coloured light. | **Why it matters.** The Gothic cathedral is one of the greatest artistic achievements of the High Middle Ages and shows the wealth of the towns and the power of the Church. Famous examples: Notre-Dame in Paris and Chartres cathedral. | **Memory trick:** "Pointed, Ribbed, Buttressed, Glass" (P-R-B-G).

@after hist10-u1-n13
T: Completing this point: the crisis of the Late Middle Ages
B: **A) The Black Death (1347–1351).** A plague carried by fleas on rats killed perhaps one-third of Europe's population. | **B) Economic decline.** Fewer workers and farmers meant less production; trade fell, and peasants who survived demanded higher wages, leading to revolts. | **C) Decline of the Catholic Church.** The popes moved to Avignon and then the **Great Schism** (two and later three rival popes) damaged the Church's prestige. | **D) Political instability.** Long wars, especially the **Hundred Years' War** between England and France (1337–1453), weakened kings and nobles. | **Memory trick:** "Plague, Poverty, Popes, Politics" (the four P's).

@after hist10-u2-n03
T: Completing this point: the Five Pillars and the Shariah
B: **The Five Pillars are the five basic duties of every Muslim.** 1) **Shahadah** – declaring faith in one God, Allah, and Mohammad as his messenger; 2) **Salat** – praying five times a day facing Mecca; 3) **Zakat** – giving a share of one's wealth to the poor; 4) **Sawm** – fasting from dawn to sunset in **Ramadan**; 5) **Hajj** – the pilgrimage to the Kaaba in Mecca at least once in a lifetime, if able. | **The Shariah.** After Mohammad's death, scholars (the **Ulama**) developed the Shariah, the Islamic law code, from the Quran and the sayings and practice of the Prophet (the Hadith and Sunna). It guides daily life, family matters and trade. | **Memory trick – "Say, Pray, Pay, Fast, Go":** Shahadah, Salat, Zakat, Sawm, Hajj.

@after hist10-u2-n04
T: Completing this point: why Islam expanded so fast
B: **Reason 1 – weak neighbours.** The Byzantine and Persian empires had exhausted each other in long wars. | **Reason 2 – welcome from local people.** Many subjects resented high imperial taxes and religious persecution and accepted the new Arab rulers, who often allowed Christians and Jews to keep their faith in return for a tax. | **Reason 3 – religious motivation.** Muslim fighters believed that fighting for the faith was a religious duty. | **Reason 4 – settlement.** Arab populations were moved into conquered lands to secure them. | **Key dates.** Yarmuk 636, Syria 640, Egypt 642, Persia about 650. The Umayyads moved the capital from Medina to **Damascus** (661); the Abbasids later built **Baghdad** (750 onwards). | **Memory trick:** "Weak – Welcome – Willing – Settled."

@after hist10-u2-n09
T: Completing this point: common features of Islamic civilizations
B: **1. Extensive trade.** Arab merchants traded by ship and camel caravan with China (silk, porcelain), India and Southeast Asia (spices, sandalwood), Africa (gold, ivory, slaves) and the Byzantine Empire. Banking and coins made exchange easier. | **2. Great cities.** Baghdad (the "City of Peace") under the Abbasids, Cairo under the Fatimids, and Damascus. Each city had palaces, a great mosque, public baths and a covered market (**bazaar**). | **3. A class society.** In theory all are equal before Allah, but in practice there was an upper class (rulers, officials, rich merchants) and a lower class. Slavery existed, but freeing slaves was considered a good act. | **4. Rights for women.** Women could own and inherit property and had some right to divorce, although men were dominant. | **5. Rich culture and learning.** Muslim scholars preserved and translated Greek works and made advances in mathematics (algebra, Arabic numerals), astronomy, medicine and philosophy. Architecture featured mosques with domes and minarets and geometric decoration. | **Memory trick:** "T-C-S-W-L: Trade, Cities, Society, Women, Learning."

@after hist10-u4-n02
T: Completing this point: the major states of East Africa
B: **A. The coastal city-states.** From the 8th century Muslim traders from Arabia and the Persian Gulf settled along the coast, and trading ports grew up: **Mogadishu** (Somalia), **Mombasa and Malindi** (Kenya), **Kilwa** (Tanzania) and Zanzibar. Kilwa was the richest, with the Great Mosque and the Husuni Kubwa palace (14th century). Intermarriage of Africans and Arabs produced the **Swahili** culture and language – Bantu with many Arabic words, "people of the coast". | **B. The states of the Lakes region.** The Bantu **Chwezi** settled western Uganda 600–700 years ago. Between the 14th and 16th centuries Nilotic herders from Sudan formed the kingdoms of **Bunyoro**, **Ankole** and **Buganda**. Bunyoro became dominant by the 16th century as a loose confederation of chiefdoms that paid tribute to the king. | **Why they rose.** Fertile land for farming and well-developed internal and external trade. | **Memory trick:** "Coast = Kilwa and Swahili; Lakes = Bunyoro and Buganda."

@after hist10-u7-n02
T: Completing this point: Bartholomew Diaz and the route to India
B: **Diaz (1488).** Bartholomew Diaz rounded the southern tip of Africa, which he called the Cape of Storms. The Portuguese king renamed it the **Cape of Good Hope** because it opened the hope of a sea route to India. His tired crew forced him to turn back. | **Why it mattered.** It proved that the Atlantic and Indian Oceans were connected, which made **Vasco da Gama's** voyage to India (1497–98) possible. | **Memory trick:** "Henry trained, Diaz turned the Cape (1488), da Gama reached India (1498)."

@after hist10-u7-n09
T: Completing this point: changes brought by foreign influence in Africa
B: **Change 1 – trade routes moved.** Trade shifted from the Sahara to the coasts, weakening trans-Saharan empires such as **Songhai**, which fell to a Moroccan invasion in 1591. | **Change 2 – religion.** Islam had the greater impact, spreading from North Africa to West and East Africa; Portuguese missionaries spread Christianity on parts of the coast. | **Change 3 – the slave trade.** Most other Europeans were interested in trade and exploitation, above all the **Atlantic slave trade**, which caused wars and population loss. | **Change 4 – new crops.** Maize and cassava were introduced from the Americas. | **Memory trick:** "Routes, Religion, Slaves, Seeds."

@after hist10-u8-n05
T: Completing this point: why Philip II attacked England, and what followed
B: **Philip's three aims.** (1) To protect Spanish treasure ships and colonies in the Americas from English raids (by "sea dogs" such as Francis Drake); (2) to remove the Protestant Queen Elizabeth I; (3) to bring Catholicism back to England. | **The Armada (1588).** More than 200 Spanish warships sailed for England but were defeated in the English Channel by the faster English navy and by storms; more than 100 ships were lost. | **The result.** Spain's power began to decline and leadership passed to **England and France**. | **Memory trick:** "Ships, Queen, Church" – Philip's three reasons.

@after hist10-u8-n06
T: Completing this point: Europe's crisis, 1560–1650
B: **1. Inflation (rising prices).** The flood of gold and silver from the Americas raised prices. | **2. Population pressure, then decline.** Population grew from about 60 million (1500) to 85 million (1600), then fell by 1650 because of war, plague and famine, causing social tension. | **3. The witchcraft trials.** More than 100,000 people were accused of witchcraft and almost 30,000 were executed (burned or hanged) before the trials declined after 1650. | **4. Religious wars.** The **Thirty Years' War (1618–1648)** began as Catholic Habsburgs against Protestant (Calvinist) nobles in Bohemia and drew in Denmark, Sweden, France and Spain. It was the most destructive European war up to then, fought mostly in Germany. | **5. Revolution in England.** The civil war and the revolutions of the 17th century. | **Memory trick:** "Prices, People, Witches, Wars, Revolution" (P-P-W-W-R).

@after hist10-u8-n18
T: Completing this point: Hobbes versus Locke
B: **Hobbes' conclusion.** In the state of nature life was "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish and short". **Thus** people made a **social contract**: to stop destroying each other, they agreed to be ruled by an **absolute ruler** with unlimited power, and rebellion had to be suppressed. Absolute power was the price of order. | **Locke's answer.** People are naturally equal and free, with **natural rights to life, liberty and property**. Government is a contract to protect those rights, and if a ruler breaks it, the people may **overthrow** him. | **Why it matters.** Locke's ideas inspired the American Declaration of Independence and the French Revolution. | **Memory trick:** "Hobbes = Hold power (absolutism); Locke = Liberty (rights)."

@after hist10-u9-n04
T: Completing these points: Babur's conquests and Akbar's golden age
B: **Babur.** After capturing Kabul, Babur entered India with a small army that had **artillery**, took **Delhi** and ruled the plains of North India, continuing his conquests **until his death in 1530**. | **Akbar's golden age – how he achieved it.** (1) **Conquest** – by 1605 he ruled most of India, the greatest empire since the Mauryas. (2) **Religious tolerance** – he respected Hindu practices and even welcomed Jesuit Christian advisers. (3) **Peace and prosperity** – long political stability let trade and manufacturing flourish; textiles, spices, food products and precious stones were exported for gold and silver. | **Memory trick:** "Akbar's three C's: Conquest, Calm (tolerance), Commerce."

@after hist10-u11-n14
T: Completing this point: results of the Seven Years' War (1756–1763)
B: **Treaty of Paris (1763) and Hubertusburg.** (1) **France** lost India to Britain; (2) France gave **Canada** and the land east of the Mississippi to Britain; (3) France gave **Louisiana** to Spain; (4) Spain gave **Florida** to Britain; (5) **Austria** accepted Prussia's permanent control of **Silesia**. | **The big result.** Britain became the strongest colonial and naval power in the world. | **A hidden effect.** The war left Britain heavily in debt; its new taxes on the American colonies led to the American Revolution. | **Memory trick:** "Britain gained India, Canada, Florida – the 'I-C-F' prize of 1763."

@after hist10-u12-n02
T: Completing these points: why Italy expanded easily, and how Wuchale led to Adwa
B: **Why Italy expanded easily into Ailet, Wia and Sahatit (after 5 February 1885).** (1) The **great famine of 1888–1892** weakened Eritreans' ability to resist; (2) the Italians crushed local resistance harshly; (3) some Eritreans accepted Italian protection against raids from Tigray and from Egypt. | **Article 17 of the Treaty of Wuchale (May 1889).** The Italian text said Menelik **must** conduct his foreign affairs through Italy (a protectorate); the Amharic text said he **could** use Italy if he wished. When Menelik discovered this, he rejected the treaty. | **What it led to.** The dispute, together with both sides' expansionist ambitions, led to the **Battle of Adwa (1 March 1896)**, where Italy suffered a crushing defeat. | **The Treaty of Addis Ababa (26 October 1896).** Ethiopia recognized Italian Eritrea and the **Mereb–Belesa–Muna line** as the border; other borders were fixed by agreements with Britain (Sudan) and France (Djibouti). | **Memory trick:** "Must or May? – one word, one war (Adwa 1896)."

@after hist10-u12-n04
T: Completing this point: Bahta Hagos and the Halay revolt (1894)
B: **Who he was.** Degiat **Bahta Hagos** first welcomed the Italians and was made governor of **Segeneyti** in 1889. | **Why he turned.** In 1893 Italy decided to take one-fifth of the highland farmland, then doubled the area seized – almost all the highland's arable land. Bahta warned his brother: "My brother Sengal, do not be too foolish… if a white snake bites, you cannot find medicine for its venom." | **The revolt.** In **December 1894** he declared revolt against "the white snake", imprisoned the Italian officer Sanguinetti and called the highlands to rise: "I will liberate you from the one who came from overseas to take our rights, seize our lands…". | **Halay.** On **18 December 1894** he attacked the Italian force at **Halay** and caused heavy damage, but Italian reinforcements from Asmara and Massawa arrived, and Bahta was wounded and died. It was a peasant revolt against land alienation, one of the notable anti-colonial revolts of north-east Africa. | **Memory trick:** "White snake = land alienation; Halay = 1894."

@after hist10-u12-n05
T: Completing these points: the Italian administrative divisions and legal codes
B: **The commissariati changed over time.** Before 1900 there were three centres (Assab, Keren, Asmara). In **1903** there were **seven**: Hamasien, Serae, Akkele Guzai, Massawa, Assab, Barka and Keren. In **1908** Gash and Setit were added (eight), and in **1931** they were reduced to five. Each was led by an Italian **commissario**; districts were under a **residente**, and the **Governor** in Asmara was at the top. | **Towns.** Seven principal towns had municipalities: Asmara, Massawa, Keren, Dekemhare, Adi Ugri, Adi Keih and Assab. | **Legal codes.** The Italian penal code applied to all residents; the Italian civil code only to Italian citizens; **Sharia and Eritrean customary law** for natives' civil cases – one law for colonizers, another for the colonized. | **Memory trick:** "1903 = 7, 1908 = 8, 1931 = 5."

@after hist10-u12-n08
T: Completing this point: Italy's economic aims in Eritrea
B: **Aim 1 – land for settlers.** Land alienation to settle Italian farmers. | **Aim 2 – raw materials and markets.** Eritrea would supply raw materials and buy Italian manufactured goods. | **Aim 3 – a springboard.** Eritrea would be a base for conquering Ethiopia, which explains the big investment in roads, the Massawa–Asmara railway and the cableway in the 1930s before the 1935 invasion. | **Memory trick:** "Land, Trade, Launchpad."
