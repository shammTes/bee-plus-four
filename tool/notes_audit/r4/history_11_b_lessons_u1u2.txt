# Round 4 - history_11: expanding short lessons (units 1-2)

@lesson hist11-u1-t1
T: Background: Britain and its thirteen colonies
B: **Great Britain.** The United Kingdom of Great Britain was formed in **1707** when England and Scotland united. In the 18th century its trade and industry grew, creating a growing middle class that wanted more trade and a bigger empire. | **William Pitt the Elder** (head of the cabinet from 1757) expanded the empire by winning **Canada and India** in the Seven Years' War. | **The colonies.** Britain ruled Canada and **13 colonies** on the east coast of today's USA. By 1750 they had more than one million people and were prosperous. | **How they were governed.** In theory by the Board of Trade, the Royal Council and Parliament; in practice the colonies had their own **legislatures** that usually acted independently, so for 150 years they grew with little interference. | **Key idea.** The revolution began when Britain tried to end this self-rule after 1763. | **Memory trick:** "1707 union, 1750 one million, 1763 trouble begins."

@lesson hist11-u1-t2
T: The causes, step by step
B: **Step 1 – the end of "leave them alone".** For over 150 years Britain let the colonies develop freely. | **Step 2 – war debt.** After the Seven Years' War (1756–63) Britain needed money to pay war costs and an army to defend the colonies. | **Step 3 – the Stamp Act (1765).** Legal documents and newspapers had to carry a stamp showing a tax was paid; violent opposition forced its cancellation in 1766, but the dispute was not resolved. | **Step 4 – the Tea Act (1773).** A British company got the right to sell tea directly, cutting out colonial merchants; Bostonians dressed as Native Americans dumped the tea into the harbour – the **Boston Tea Party**. | **The principle.** "No taxation without representation" – the colonists had no members in Parliament, so they denied its right to tax them. | **Memory trick:** "Debt → Stamps → Tea → Revolt."

@lesson hist11-u1-t3
T: The Boston Massacre: what happened and why it mattered
B: **Background.** British troops were sent to Boston to enforce the new taxes (the Townshend duties of 1767 on goods such as glass, paper and tea). Their presence caused daily friction with townspeople. | **The event (5 March 1770).** A crowd taunted and threw snowballs and stones at a group of British soldiers guarding the customs house; the soldiers fired, killing **five** colonists. | **Propaganda.** Patriots called it a "massacre" and spread pictures of soldiers shooting peaceful citizens, which turned many colonists against Britain. | **What followed.** Most Townshend duties were cancelled, but the **tax on tea** remained, which led to the Tea Act crisis and the Boston Tea Party (1773). | **Why it matters for the exam.** It is an example of how violence and propaganda pushed the colonies from protest towards revolution. | **Memory trick:** "1765 stamps, 1770 shots, 1773 tea."

@lesson hist11-u1-t6
T: Why Saratoga was the turning point
B: **Military.** A whole British army under Burgoyne surrendered (17 October 1777), proving that the Americans could defeat regular troops. | **Diplomatic.** France, which had secretly supplied arms and money since 1776, openly recognized the USA in **February 1778** and entered the war; Spain and the Dutch Republic followed. | **Result.** Britain now had to fight much of Europe as well as the Americans. | **Memory trick:** "Saratoga brought France."

@lesson hist11-u1-t7
T: Foreign help, Yorktown and the Treaty of Paris
B: **Why the Americans won although Britain was stronger.** Britain was a great military power with huge finances; the Continental Army was made of short-term amateur soldiers. Foreign help made the difference: France gave arms, money, officers and soldiers, then recognized the USA in 1778; Spain and the Dutch Republic joined the war against Britain. | **Yorktown (1781).** The British army surrendered to combined American and French forces under **George Washington**, and Britain decided to end the war. | **The Treaty of Paris (3 September 1783).** Britain recognized the independence of the United States and gave the Americans control of the western territory. | **Memory trick:** "France + Washington = Yorktown 1781; Paris 1783 = freedom."

@lesson hist11-u1-t8
T: From Articles of Confederation to the Bill of Rights
B: **Articles of Confederation (1781).** The states feared strong central power, so the national government was too weak to solve the new nation's problems. | **Constitution (1787).** It created a **federal system** sharing power between national and state governments, with **checks and balances** between the three branches; nine states had to ratify it, and George Washington became the first president. | **Bill of Rights (1789–91).** Congress proposed 12 amendments; the 10 approved guaranteed freedom of religion, speech, press, petition and assembly – rights drawn from Enlightenment ideas. | **Wider impact.** Europeans saw the revolution as Enlightenment ideas made real; it inspired the French Revolution and Latin American independence movements. | **Memory trick:** "Weak Articles → Strong Constitution → Ten Rights."

@lesson hist11-u2-t4
T: The Church reform and the Constitution of 1791, explained
B: **Why the Church was targeted.** Revolutionaries saw it as part of the old order, and the state needed money. | **What changed.** The Assembly seized and sold Church lands and passed the **Civil Constitution of the Clergy**: bishops and priests were elected by the people and paid by the state, so the government controlled the Church. Many Catholics became enemies of the revolution. | **Constitution of 1791.** A **limited monarchy**: the king remained, but a **Legislative Assembly of 745 members** made the laws. All male citizens had equal rights, but only men over 25 who paid a set tax could vote. | **Opponents of the new order.** Priests, nobles, poor people hurt by rising prices, and radicals who wanted more change. | **Next step.** Austria and Prussia threatened to restore the king, and in spring 1792 France declared war on Austria. | **Memory trick:** "Lands sold, priests elected, king limited."

@lesson hist11-u2-t7
T: How the Grand Empire spread revolutionary ideas, and why it fell
B: **The three parts.** French Empire (enlarged France); dependent states ruled by Napoleon's relatives (Spain, Holland, the kingdom of Italy, the Swiss Republic, the Grand Duchy of Warsaw, the Confederation of the Rhine); allied states forced to help him (Prussia, Austria, Russia, Sweden). | **Ideas spread.** Equality before the law, religious toleration, careers open to talent and the end of noble and clerical privileges – a basis for later liberal traditions. | **Reason 1 for the fall – Britain survived.** Its navy destroyed the French-Spanish fleet in 1805 (Trafalgar); Napoleon's ban on British goods (the Continental System) failed because allies resisted and Britain found new markets. | **Reason 2 – nationalism.** The French were seen as oppressors, and their example taught others to build nations with mass armies. | **Memory trick:** "Sea power and national pride beat Napoleon."

@lesson hist11-u2-t8
T: The fall of Napoleon in five dates
B: **June 1812** – the Grand Army of over 600,000 invades Russia; the Russians retreat and burn the land; Borodino is a costly victory; Moscow is found burning. **January 1813** – fewer than 40,000 survivors reach Poland after the winter retreat. **March 1814** – the allies capture Paris; Napoleon is exiled to Elba and Louis XVIII is made king. **20 March 1815** – Napoleon returns to Paris (the Hundred Days). **18 June 1815** – defeated at **Waterloo** by British and Prussian armies; exiled to **St Helena**, where he died in 1821. | **Memory trick:** "Russia 1812, Paris 1814, Waterloo 1815, St Helena 1821."
