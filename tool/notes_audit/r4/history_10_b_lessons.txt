# Round 4 - history_10: expanding short lessons

@lesson hist10-u4-t1
T: Step by step: how farming, iron and the Bantu laid the foundations
B: **Step 1 – hunting and gathering.** The earliest Africans lived by hunting and gathering. | **Step 2 – farming (about 5000 BCE).** Crop farming and herding reached the North African coast, probably from Western Asia. East Africans grew local millet and sorghum; West Africans grew yams and oil palms; farming reached Southern Africa later. | **Step 3 – iron.** Iron working reached Meroe by 500 BCE and spread south; West Africa learned it from Carthage. Iron hoes, axes and spearheads made farming and defence easier. | **Step 4 – the Bantu migrations.** By 200 CE more food meant more Bantu people in West Africa; shortage of land and water pushed them into Central, East and Southern Africa over two thousand years, spreading farming, iron and village life. | **Result.** These foundations allowed civilizations to rise between 500 and 1500 CE. | **Memory trick:** "Food → Iron → Migration → States."

@lesson hist10-u4-t5
T: Great Zimbabwe in numbers
B: **Before states.** Until the 11th century people south of the Zambezi lived in independent villages led by clan heads, with a mixed economy of farming, cattle herding and trade. Villages were walled to protect animals at night. | **The rise.** From the 11th century villages united into the first states. Zimbabwe, with its capital at Great Zimbabwe, was the richest and strongest state in the region from about **1300 to 1450**. | **Wealth.** Gold traded through the Swahili coastal towns, even reaching China. | **The city.** Up to **11,000** people lived there. The **Great Enclosure** was an oval wall **244 m long, 5 m thick and 10 m high**, built of granite blocks stacked **without mortar**. | **The decline.** By the mid-15th century the city was abandoned, probably because overgrazing exhausted the soil, or because of drought and crop failure. | **Memory trick:** "1300–1450, 11,000 people, no mortar."
