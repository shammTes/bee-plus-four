B: **First Estate – the clergy.** About 130,000 people who owned about 10 % of the land and were exempt from the main tax. The higher clergy came from noble families; ordinary parish priests were often poor commoners. | **Second Estate – the nobility.** About 350,000 people who owned 25–30 % of the land, held the top jobs in government, the army, the courts and the Church, and enjoyed tax exemptions. | **Third Estate – everyone else.** The overwhelming majority, from rich bourgeois to peasants. Peasants alone were 75–80 % of the population, yet this estate paid almost all the taxes. | **Memory trick:** "Pray – Fight – Pay": the clergy pray, the nobles fight, the Third Estate pays. | **Exam tip:** The unfair tax burden plus Enlightenment ideas of equality are the two most-asked long-term causes.

B: **Step 1 – the king refuses.** Louis XVI stayed at Versailles and refused to accept the Assembly's decrees abolishing feudalism and the Declaration of Rights. | **Step 2 – the women's march (5 October 1789).** Thousands of Parisian women, angry about bread prices, marched to Versailles; a delegation forced the king to accept the decrees. | **Step 3 – back to Paris (6 October).** The crowd made the royal family move to Paris, where they became virtual prisoners. | **Step 4 – the failed escape (June 1791).** The family tried to flee France but the king was recognized, caught and brought back, which destroyed trust in the monarchy. | **Memory trick:** "Refuse – March – Move – Run."

@after hist11-u2-n05
T: Completing this point: the Reign of Terror (1793–1794)
B: **What it was.** For about a year the Committee of Public Safety ruled France to defend the revolution against foreign armies and internal enemies. Revolutionary courts tried "enemies of the republic". | **The tool.** The **guillotine** – the queen, Marie Antoinette, was among its victims. | **The cost.** Close to 40,000 people were killed. Clergy and nobles were only about 15 % of the victims; most were bourgeois and peasants. Revolutionary armies also crushed rebellious cities. | **The end.** The Terror ended when Robespierre himself was executed in 1794, and the moderate Directory (1795–1799) followed. | **Exam tip:** Remember the numbers "1793–94, about 40,000 dead, 15 % nobles and clergy".

@after hist11-u2-n06
T: Completing this point: Napoleon's domestic and foreign policies
B: **Domestic policy 1 – peace with the Church (1801).** An agreement with the Pope recognized Catholicism as the majority religion; the Pope did not ask for the seized church lands back. | **Domestic policy 2 – the Civil (Napoleonic) Code.** Seven law codes replaced France's many local laws. They kept equality before the law, free choice of profession, religious toleration and the end of serfdom and feudalism, but outlawed trade unions and strikes and made women less equal than men. | **Domestic policy 3 – a centralized administration.** Promotion was based on ability, not birth, and a new aristocracy of merit was created. | **Domestic policy 4 – less liberty.** He shut down 60 of France's 73 newspapers and censored manuscripts. | **Foreign policy – the Grand Empire.** After peace in 1802 and renewed war in 1803, he defeated Austria, Prussia and Russia (1805–1807). From 1807 to 1812 he was master of Europe: the French Empire, dependent states ruled by his relatives, and allied states. | **Memory trick:** "Church, Code, Careers, Censorship – then Conquest."

@after hist11-u3-n04
T: Completing this point: the good and the bad of industrialization
B: **Advantages.** (1) Europe's population nearly doubled, from 140 million in 1750 to 266 million in 1850, because food supply rose and famine largely disappeared. (2) Cities grew quickly as factories moved to them. (3) A new **industrial middle class** of factory owners, bankers and professionals appeared. (4) Cheaper goods and new inventions made life more comfortable for many. | **Disadvantages.** (1) The new **industrial working class** worked 12–16 hours a day, six days a week, with no job security and no minimum wage. (2) Mines and textile factories were unhealthy and dangerous. (3) Women and children made up two-thirds of the British cotton workforce by 1830; women were paid half or less of men's wages. (4) Crowded cities had poor housing and sanitation. | **The result.** These conditions led to labour unions, socialist ideas and, later, reforms. | **Memory trick:** "More people, more goods – but long hours and bad homes."

@after hist11-u3-n28
T: Completing these points: Marx, socialist parties and trade unions
B: **Why workers organized after 1870.** They wanted better working and living conditions and blamed the industrialists for their misery. | **The theory.** *The Communist Manifesto* was published in **1848** by Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels. History is a history of class struggle; the proletariat would overthrow the bourgeoisie, and a classless society would follow in which the state would "wither away". | **Socialist parties.** The **German Social Democratic Party (SPD)** was founded in 1875; despite government efforts to destroy it, it won four million votes in 1912 and became the largest party in Germany. Socialist parties appeared in other countries too. | **Trade unions.** British unions won the right to strike in the 1870s; by 1914 unions across Europe had improved working and living conditions. | **Second International (1889).** An international association of socialist parties against capitalism; the First International had failed in 1872. | **Memory trick:** "1848 Manifesto, 1875 SPD, 1889 Second International."

@after hist11-u3-n29
T: Completing this point: how Europe split into two armed camps
B: **Step 1 – Germany isolates France.** After 1871 Germany was the strongest state and France its chief rival. Bismarck allied with Austria-Hungary (1879); Italy joined in 1882, forming the **Triple Alliance**. | **Step 2 – Bismarck's safety net.** He kept a separate treaty with Russia and stayed friendly with Britain. | **Step 3 – William II changes course.** He dismissed Bismarck in 1890 and ended the treaty with Russia. | **Step 4 – France and Russia join (1894).** They formed a military alliance. | **Step 5 – Britain joins them.** Fear of growing German power pushed Britain towards France, and by 1907 Britain, France and Russia formed the **Triple Entente**. | **Result.** Two suspicious camps by 1914 – one of the causes of the First World War. | **Memory trick:** "Alliance = G-A-I (Germany, Austria, Italy); Entente = B-F-R (Britain, France, Russia)."

@after hist11-u4-n08
T: Completing this point: how Western contact changed China's economy
B: **The three changes.** (1) Westerners brought **modern transport and communications** such as railways, steamships and the telegraph. (2) They created an **export market** for Chinese goods like tea and silk. (3) They **integrated the Chinese market** into the 19th-century world economy, helped by banking and a money economy. | **The harm.** Much local handicraft industry was destroyed by cheap foreign goods, and many profits went abroad rather than to the Chinese. | **Cultural effect.** Educated city people adopted Western ideas and Confucian ideals declined; radical reformers wanted to remove traditional culture. | **Memory trick:** "Transport, Trade, Tied-in – but Traditions and Trades hurt."

@after hist11-u5-n03
T: Completing these points: US dominance and economic change in Latin America
B: **US intervention.** By 1900 the USA was a world power. After the **Spanish–American War (1898)** Cuba became a US protectorate and Puerto Rico was annexed. In **1903** the US backed Panama's break from Colombia and received a 16 km strip where it built the **Panama Canal (opened 1914)**. From 1898 US troops were sent to Cuba, Mexico, Guatemala, Honduras, Nicaragua, Haiti and others to protect American business, which caused growing resentment. | **Export prosperity after 1870.** Each country relied on a few exports: wheat and beef (Argentina), coffee (Brazil), coffee and bananas (Central America), sugar and silver (Peru), exchanged for European and American manufactured goods. | **Industrialization after 1900.** Factories for textiles, food processing and construction materials were built, and a middle class grew. | **Memory trick:** "Cuba 1898, Canal 1914, Coffee and Cattle."

@after hist11-u6-n01
T: Completing this point: the colonization of West Africa
B: **Why.** As the slave trade declined, Europeans traded textiles and manufactured goods for peanuts, timber, hides and palm oil, and pushed for a permanent presence on the coast. | **Britain.** Early in the 19th century it set up settlements on the Gold Coast and in Sierra Leone; in **1874** it annexed coastal states as the colony of **Gold Coast**, and it established a protectorate over **Nigeria**. | **France.** By 1900 it had built **French West Africa**, the largest share of the region. | **Germany.** It controlled Togo, Cameroon, German South-West Africa (Namibia) and German East Africa (Tanzania). | **Memory trick:** "Britain = Gold Coast, Sierra Leone, Nigeria; France = the biggest block; Germany = Togo and Cameroon."

@after hist11-u6-n05
T: Completing this point: from the Great Trek to the Union of South Africa
B: **The Boers move north.** In the 1830s the Boers, descendants of 17th-century Dutch settlers, left the British Cape Colony and moved to the land between the Orange and Vaal rivers and north of the Vaal (the Great Trek). | **Two Boer republics.** There they founded the **Orange Free State** and the **Transvaal** (South African Republic). | **Conflict with the Zulu.** The Zulu kingdom built by **Shaka** stayed powerful after his death but was defeated by the British in the late 1800s. | **Rhodes and the Boer War.** Cecil Rhodes, owner of diamond and gold companies, pushed British expansion and took Rhodesia. After the war with the Boers, Britain created the **Union of South Africa (1910)**, a self-governing state where only whites, plus a few wealthy Africans, could vote. | **Memory trick:** "Trek (1830s) – Republics – Rhodes – Union (1910)."

@after hist11-u7-n09
T: Completing this point: causes and course of the Russian Revolution of 1917
B: **Causes.** (1) Russia was not ready for war – poor leaders and weak industry; two million soldiers died in 1914–1916 and four to six million were wounded or captured. (2) The war wrecked the economy. (3) Tsar Nicholas II ruled by force. | **February/March revolution.** Bread rationing in Petrograd led to protests; on **8 March 1917** (International Women's Day) about 10,000 women marched shouting "Peace and Bread". Soldiers refused to fire and joined them. The Duma set up a provisional government, and the tsar abdicated on 15 March. | **Bolsheviks take over.** Kerensky's provisional government stayed in the war. Lenin returned in April promising "Peace, Land, Bread" and "All Power to the Soviets", and on the night of **6 November 1917** the Bolsheviks seized the Winter Palace. | **Memory trick:** "War + Want + Whip = Revolution."

@after hist11-u8-n02
T: Completing these points: why the League of Nations was weak
B: **Weakness 1 – missing great powers.** The US Senate refused to ratify the Treaty of Versailles, so the USA never joined. Germany and Russia were outside for many years, and Japan, Germany and Italy later walked out. | **Weakness 2 – no army.** It had no power to enforce its decisions or stop aggression; it could only condemn or apply economic sanctions. | **Failures.** Japan's invasion of **Manchuria (1931)**; Italy's invasion of **Ethiopia (1935)** despite sanctions; Germany's rearmament, the Rhineland (1936), Austria and Czechoslovakia (1938) and Poland (1939). | **Memory trick:** "No USA, No Army, No Success."

@after hist11-u8-n03
T: Completing this point: hyperinflation and the Dawes Plan
B: **Hyperinflation.** Germany paid striking Ruhr workers by printing money. In 1914, 1 US dollar = 4.2 marks; by November 1923, 1 dollar = 4.2 trillion marks. Savings were wiped out and workers carried their pay home in wheelbarrows. | **The Dawes Plan (August 1924).** Named after the American banker who chaired the commission: (1) it reduced reparations and linked yearly payments to Germany's ability to pay; (2) it gave a $200 million American loan, and US investment flowed into Europe. | **Result.** Prosperity from 1924 to 1929; Germany joined the League in 1926, and in 1928 sixty-three countries signed an accord rejecting war as national policy. | **Memory trick:** "Print → Price explosion → Plan (Dawes)."

@after hist11-u8-n12
T: Completing this point: what "totalitarian" means
B: **Definition.** A totalitarian government tries to control the political, economic, social, intellectual and cultural lives of its citizens. In the 1920s most European states were democracies, but in the 1930s dictatorships spread in eastern and central Europe. | **The three common features.** (1) Total obedience, won through massive **propaganda**; (2) one **leader** and one **party**; (3) no individual freedom – **secret police** crush opponents. | **The best-known examples.** Fascist Italy (Mussolini), Nazi Germany (Hitler) and Stalin's Soviet Union. | **Memory trick:** "Propaganda, Party, Police" – the three P's of totalitarianism.

@after hist11-u8-n13
T: Completing this point: Stalin's Soviet Union, the third dictatorship
B: **Rise.** After Lenin died in 1924, Stalin pushed out Trotsky and by 1929 had a complete dictatorship. | **Five-Year Plans (from 1928).** They aimed to turn Russia from an agricultural into an industrial country; heavy machinery, steel, coal and chemicals quadrupled, and literacy improved, so the USSR boomed while the West suffered the Great Depression. | **The cost.** Low wages and housing shortages left millions of workers poor, and private farms were abolished through **collectivization**. | **Compare.** Mussolini (Fascist party 1919) wanted to restore Roman glory; Hitler wanted to cancel Versailles and expand; Stalin wanted rapid industrial power – all three used one party, propaganda and terror. | **Memory trick:** "Stalin = Steel and Collective farms."

@after hist11-u8-n20
T: Completing this point: radio and cinema in the interwar years
B: **Radio.** Invented by Marconi at the end of the 19th century, but first used for mass broadcasting in **1920**; stations were built in the USA, Europe and Japan in 1921–22, and radios became common in homes. | **Cinema.** Films appeared in the 1890s, feature-length films just before WWI, and after the war cinema became mass entertainment; by the late 1930s about 40 % of Western adults went to the cinema once a week. | **Politics.** Both were used as **propaganda** tools by governments, especially the dictatorships. | **Mass leisure.** The eight-hour day gave workers free time for sport, travel, concerts and films. | **Memory trick:** "Radio 1920, cinema weekly, propaganda always."

@after hist11-u8-n22
T: Completing this point: why interwar art was so gloomy
B: **The mood.** WWI made many Europeans feel hopeless; people believed that humans were violent and incapable of building a rational world. The Great Depression and fascism deepened this despair. | **Painting.** **Dadaism** – life has no purpose or meaning; **Surrealism** – painting dreams, fantasies and nightmares (Salvador Dali's *Persistence of Memory*, with melting clocks). | **Literature.** Writers explored people's inner thoughts; James Joyce's *Ulysses* follows one day in Dublin through the flow of characters' thoughts. | **Memory trick:** "Dada = no meaning, Surreal = dreams, Joyce = inner thoughts."

@after hist11-u10-n01
T: Completing this point: the road to the Second World War, step by step
B: **Root cause.** WWI and the harsh Treaty of Versailles, plus the aggressive fascist regimes in Germany, Italy and Japan. | **Germany.** Left the League (1933); announced an air force and raised the army from 100,000 to 500,000 (1935); reoccupied the Rhineland (1936); annexed Austria (1938); took the Sudetenland after Munich (1938), the rest of Czechoslovakia (1939); invaded **Poland on 1 September 1939** – Britain and France declared war two days later. | **Italy.** Invaded Ethiopia (1935); the League imposed sanctions. | **Japan.** Invaded **Manchuria in 1931**, left the League in 1933 and began full-scale war with China in **1937**. | **Appeasement and alliances.** Britain and France gave in to Hitler hoping for peace; the Rome–Berlin Axis and Anti-Comintern Pact (1936) and the Nazi–Soviet pact (1939) followed. | **Memory trick:** "Versailles, Villains, Victims, Vacillation (appeasement)."

@after hist11-u10-n03
T: Completing this point: the fall of France and the Vichy regime
B: **The fall.** On 5 June 1940 Germany attacked southern France; five days later Mussolini declared war and invaded from the south. France surrendered on **22 June 1940**. | **Division.** Germany occupied about three-fifths of France; the rest was run by a German-controlled government at **Vichy** under Marshal **Pétain**, a WWI hero. | **Britain alone.** By 1940 Germany controlled western and central Europe except Britain, which asked the US for help; the US stayed officially isolationist but sent food, ships and weapons, and from **March 1941** Lend-Lease aid. | **Memory trick:** "June 22, 1940 – France falls; Vichy = Pétain."

@after hist11-u10-n06
T: Completing this point: the economic and social consequences of WWII
B: **Economic.** Britain, France, Italy and Germany were bankrupted; industry, cities, transport and farms in Europe and Japan were destroyed, and 45 million homeless Europeans depended on US aid. Cost: over one trillion US dollars. | **Bombing of civilians.** London (from September 1940), German cities including **Dresden (1945)** where a firestorm killed about 100,000, Japanese cities from November 1944, and the atomic bombs on **Hiroshima and Nagasaki (August 1945)**. | **Unemployment.** Millions of returning soldiers and prisoners needed jobs. | **Science and technology.** War research produced radar and other new devices. | **Memory trick:** "Broke, Bombed, Jobless – but Inventive."

@after hist11-u11-n09
T: Completing this point: the two camps of Eritrean parties
B: **Background.** Until October 1946 the BMA banned political parties; after the Bet Giorgis meeting failed, parties formed openly. | **Two camps.** Pro-independence – the **Muslim League** (Keren, December 1946, founded by Sheik Ibrahim Sultan, newspaper *Sewt Al Rabita*, the strongest anti-union party), the **Liberal Progressive Party** "Eritrea for Eritreans" (Adi Keih, February 1947, Ras Tessema Asberom), the **Pro-Italy Party** (July 1947, newspaper *Brhan Ertra*). Pro-union – the **Unionist Party** (Asmara, March 1947, Tedla Bairu). | **Small parties.** The National Muslim Party of Massawa, the Italo-Eritrean Association and the Free Unionist Party; at one point there were more than a dozen parties, and all except the Unionists supported independence in some form. | **Memory trick:** "One for union, the rest for independence."

@after hist11-u11-n10

B: **France.** Rejected Britain's partition plan and wanted the colonies returned to **Italy as a trusteeship**. | **USSR.** Its position was ambiguous; expecting Communist victory in Italian elections, it also proposed returning Eritrea to Italy. | **Summary.** Britain = partition; USA = changing (first Assab to Ethiopia with collective trusteeship, then Italian trusteeship); France = Italian trusteeship; USSR = return to Italy. Talks in Potsdam, London and Paris failed. | **The Four Power Commission.** Visited Eritrea from 12 November 1947 to 3 January 1948 to find out the people's wishes; the powers still could not agree and passed the question to the UN on 15 September 1948. | **Memory trick:** "B-Partition, U-Changing, F-Italy, S-Italy."

@after hist11-u12-n02
T: Completing this point: Resolution 390A(V) and the Federal Act in order
B: **The vote.** On **2 December 1950** the UN General Assembly passed Resolution 390A(V), a US-backed federal plan, by 46 votes to 9 with 4 abstentions. It had 15 articles; the first 7 formed the **Federal Act**. | **The seven articles, simplified.** (1) Eritrea is an autonomous unit federated with Ethiopia under the Ethiopian Crown. (2) Eritrea has its own legislative, executive and judicial branches. (3) The Federal Government controls defence, foreign affairs, currency and finance, foreign and interstate trade and communications including ports; Eritrea controls everything else, such as health, education, police, its budget and taxes. (4) Free movement of people and goods within the federation. (5) An Imperial Federal Council with equal Eritrean and Ethiopian members meets at least once a year. (6) One nationality. (7) Equal rights without distinction of race, sex, language or religion. | **Memory trick:** "Autonomy – Branches – Division of powers – Free movement – Council – Nationality – Rights" (one word per article).

@after hist11-u12-n04
T: Completing these points: the 1952 Assembly, the constitution debate and the federation's weaknesses
B: **The March 1952 election.** 68 members (all men) were elected: Unionist Party 34, Eritrean Democratic Front 19, Muslim League of the Western Province 15. Tedla Bairu became President of the Assembly and Ali Radai Vice-President, so pro-Ethiopian members were strong. | **The debate.** Pro-Ethiopians wanted Amharic, no Eritrean flag and an Ethiopian representative; the Democratic Front wanted Tigrinya and Arabic, its own flag and no representative. The Front won on language and flag; the Unionists won on the representative. | **The constitution.** 99 articles, ratified **10 July 1952**; it separated powers and gave wide democratic rights to men (women were excluded). The Emperor ratified it on **11 September 1952**, and the federation began four days later. | **Why the federation was weak.** A democratic Eritrea was tied to an autocratic monarchy; the Emperor headed both the Ethiopian and the federal government; the Imperial Representative was not part of the Federal Act; and there was no clause for UN intervention if the Act was violated. | **Memory trick:** "34 – 19 – 15 = 68 seats."

@after hist11-u12-n05
T: Completing this point: how Ethiopia saw the federation from the start
B: **Ethiopia's view.** It treated the federation as a temporary step towards full union and began violating it almost immediately. | **Economic weakening.** The British handed ports, transport, communications, customs and tax systems to Ethiopia; factories were closed or moved; the FIAT plant at Dekemhare and the SEDAO hydro-electric scheme were cancelled. Unemployment and prices rose, and workers protested only three months after the government was formed. | **The three phases of dismantling.** 1952–1955 (weakening the institutions), 1955–1958 (removing Eritrean identity), 1958–1960 (final dismantling, leading to annexation in 1962). | **Memory trick:** "Weaken – Erase – Abolish."

@after hist11-u12-n06
T: Completing this point: the first phase in more detail (1952–1955)
B: **Attack on the workers' union.** The General Union of Eritrean Labour Syndicates was formed on 7 December 1952 under Woldeab Woldemariam (vice-president Siraj Abdu). In January 1953 he was seriously wounded in an assassination attempt and escaped to Egypt in 1954. | **Attack on the press.** In 1954 *Voice of Eritrea* (Dehai Ertra) was closed and its editors Elias Teklu and Mohammed Saleh were imprisoned. | **Institutions.** The Imperial Federal Council was abolished in 1953; Andargachew Messay interfered constantly; in 1955 Tedla Bairu and Ali Radai were forced out and replaced by Asfaha Woldemichael and Idris Mohammed Adem. | **Protests.** In 1954 Ibrahim Sultan and Omer Qadi petitioned foreign consulates against the violations. | **Memory trick:** "Union, Newspaper, Council, Leaders – four targets."

@after hist11-u12-n07
T: Completing this point: the Eritrean reaction in the second phase
B: **Student protests (May 1957).** Students of Haile Selassie Secondary School in Asmara boycotted class against Amharic as the medium of instruction, inspired by Woldeab Woldemariam's radio broadcasts from Cairo. Other schools and towns joined; the government jailed the organizers. | **Workers' strike (March 1958).** Over 20,000 workers demonstrated in Asmara against a new labour law, joined by Assab, Massawa and Keren, for three days. Police opened fire on the crowds, killing and wounding many, and the union was banned. | **Why it matters.** Peaceful protest was met with force, which convinced many Eritreans that organized resistance was needed – leading to the ELM in November 1958. | **Memory trick:** "Students 1957, Workers 1958, ELM 1958."

@after hist11-u12-n10
T: Completing this point: the end of the federation, in date order
B: **1958.** The Eritrean Workers' Union was abolished and the Eritrean flag was replaced by the Ethiopian flag (December). | **1959.** The Ethiopian Penal Code replaced the Eritrean penal law; Abiye Abebe replaced Andargachew Messay as Imperial Representative. | **1960.** "Eritrean Government" became "Eritrean Administration" and "Chief Executive" became "Chief Administrator"; schools were put under the Ethiopian Ministry of Education and Amharic was imposed. | **1962.** The Eritrean Assembly, under heavy pressure, voted to dissolve the federation and Eritrea was annexed. | **Memory trick:** "Flag (1958) → Law (1959) → Name (1960) → Annexation (1962)."

@after hist11-u12-n11
T: Completing this point: the ELM's founders, objectives and limits
B: **Founding.** November 1958 in Port Sudan; founders included Mohammed Said Nawud (chairman), Saleh Iyay, Mohammed Al-Hassen Osman and Osman Mohammed Osman. Also called **Harakat Tahrir Eritrya** or **Mahber Shewate**. | **Three objectives.** (1) Promote Eritrean national unity; (2) secure independence by ending Ethiopian rule; (3) build a democratic system. | **Organization.** From 1959 secret cells of seven members who did not know other cells; members swore an oath of dedication and secrecy. The first congress met in Asmara in 1960. | **Strategy.** To overthrow the puppet government by a coup d'état using members in the police. | **Limitations.** The leadership was abroad and communication was hard; secrecy blocked coordination; the coup plan was unrealistic; and it failed to start armed struggle quickly, which led Eritreans in Cairo to form a new organization (the ELF, 1960). | **Memory trick:** "Unity, Independence, Democracy – in cells of seven."
