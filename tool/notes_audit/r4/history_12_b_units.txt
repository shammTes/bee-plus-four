@unit hist12-u1-l1-1 exercise.questions[0].
T: The Marshall Plan and the Soviet answer (COMECON)
B: **Step 1 – what it was.** In June 1947, right after the Truman Doctrine, US Secretary of State **George C. Marshall** proposed the **European Recovery Program**, better known as the Marshall Plan. | **Step 2 – what it gave.** About **$13 billion** in aid to rebuild the war-damaged economies of Europe. | **Step 3 – why the USA did it.** Americans believed communist revolutions succeed where people are poor and economies are in crisis. Rebuilding Europe's economy would take away that chance, so the plan was part of **containment**. | **Step 4 – how the Soviets saw it.** The USSR saw the plan as an attempt to **buy the support of European countries** and kept Eastern Europe out of it. | **Step 5 – the Soviet reply.** In **1949** the USSR founded the **Council for Mutual Economic Assistance (COMECON)** for Eastern European states. COMECON largely failed because the Soviet Union could not provide enough money. | **Memory trick:** "Truman promised, Marshall paid, Moscow answered with COMECON." | **Exam tip:** If asked for the main aim of the Marshall Plan, answer "to rebuild Western Europe's economy so that communism could not spread" – both halves earn marks.

@unit hist12-u1-l1-4 exercise.questions[1].
T: Fear of communism at home and the closest nuclear scare
B: **Step 1 – the Red Scare at home.** In the early 1950s Cold War tensions abroad caused widespread fear in the USA that communists had secretly entered American life. Truman's attorney general warned that communists were "everywhere". | **Step 2 – McCarthy.** The main agitator was **Senator Joseph R. McCarthy**, who claimed that large numbers of communists and Soviet spies worked inside the US government. His charges created a massive **"Red Scare"** – fear of communist subversion. Intellectuals and film stars were questioned about communist activities. When he attacked alleged "conspirators" in the US Army, the **Senate condemned him in 1954**. | **Step 3 – the closest nuclear scare abroad.** The real brink of nuclear war came with the **Cuban Missile Crisis (October 1962)**. After Castro's revolution (1959) and the failed CIA-backed **Bay of Pigs invasion (April 1961)**, the USSR began placing nuclear missiles in Cuba. | **Step 4 – how it ended.** The USA blockaded Cuba. **Khrushchev** turned his ships back and removed the missiles in return for a US promise not to invade Cuba, and the USA secretly removed its missiles from Turkey. A **hotline** between the Kremlin and the White House was then set up. | **Memory trick:** "McCarthy scared America; Cuba scared the world." | **Exam tip:** Questions about the "closest nuclear scare" always mean the Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962, not the Red Scare.

@unit hist12-u1-l1-5 exercise.questions[2].
T: Gorbachev's perestroika (and glasnost), step by step
B: **Step 1 – the crisis.** By 1980 the Soviet Union had a declining economy, rising infant mortality and poor working conditions. A small group of reformers appeared in the Communist Party. | **Step 2 – Gorbachev.** In **March 1985** Mikhail Gorbachev became leader and preached reform from the start. | **Step 3 – perestroika means "restructuring".** At first it meant restructuring the economy towards a **market economy** in which consumers influence what is produced, with limited free enterprise and some private property. | **Step 4 – political reform followed.** Gorbachev saw that economic reform was impossible without political reform. In **1988** he created an elected parliament, the **Congress of People's Deputies** (it met in 1989). In early **1990** he allowed non-communist parties and removed the Communist Party's guaranteed leading role from the constitution. | **Step 5 – result.** These reforms released forces that the party could not control and led to the end of the Soviet Union in 1991. | **Memory trick:** "Perestroika = Pieces rebuilt (restructuring); Glasnost = Glass (openness)." | **Exam tip:** Do not translate perestroika as "openness" – that is glasnost.

@unit hist12-u1-l1-2 exercise.questions[6].
T: Why Nasser's foreign policy angered the West
B: **Step 1 – background.** In **1952** army officers overthrew the Egyptian monarchy. Under **Gamal Abdel Nasser** the new government wanted to modernize Egypt, develop the economy and control the Suez Canal. He negotiated loans from the USA, Britain and the World Bank for the **Aswan High Dam**. | **Step 2 – the four policies that alarmed the West.** (1) open support for anti-colonial movements in **Kenya and Algeria**; (2) an **arms deal with communist Czechoslovakia**; (3) attending the **Bandung Conference (1955)** and co-founding the **Non-Aligned Movement**; (4) formal **recognition of the People's Republic of China**. | **Step 3 – the result.** The West cancelled the loans. Nasser **nationalized the Suez Canal (1956)** to pay for the dam, and Britain, France and Israel attacked Egypt – the **Suez Crisis**. | **Memory trick – "K-A-C-B-C":** Kenya and Algeria, Czech arms, Bandung, China. | **Exam tip:** When the answer choices list several of these policies, the correct option is usually "all of the above".

@unit hist12-u1-l1-4 exercise.questions[7].
T: The Prague Spring (1968)
B: **Step 1 – the reforms.** In **January 1968** the Czechoslovak government introduced reforms: freedom of speech and press, freedom to travel abroad, relaxed censorship, a more independent foreign policy and a promise of gradual democratization. | **Step 2 – the name.** The hopeful mood that followed was called the **"Prague Spring"**. | **Step 3 – the end.** The **Soviet army invaded Czechoslovakia in August 1968** and crushed the reform movement. | **Step 4 – compare Hungary (1956).** Twelve years earlier the Soviet army attacked Budapest after Hungary announced it would leave the Warsaw Pact. Both cases show the USSR would not allow its satellites to leave its control. | **Memory trick:** "Hungary 56, Prague 68 – tanks both times." | **Exam tip:** The East European country invaded by the Soviets in 1968 is Czechoslovakia; in 1956 it was Hungary.

T: Unit 2 summary in full – the Middle East and Asia after 1945
B: **Point 1 – colonial background.** Most of the Middle East and Asia had been colonized by European powers. Japan, the only Asian imperialist power, invaded most of Asia during the Second World War. | **Point 2 – a wave of new states.** With the end of the Second World War many newly independent nations appeared in the Middle East and Asia. | **Point 3 – the Arab states.** Nationalist movements won independence for **Syria and Lebanon in 1946**. **Iraq** was declared independent in **1932** but remained under British control even after independence. **South Yemen** was liberated by nationalist liberation movements in **1967**. The small Arab Gulf states became independent one after another. | **Point 4 – South Asia.** British South Asia was divided into **India, Pakistan, Sri Lanka and later Bangladesh**. | **Point 5 – Southeast Asia.** Wars of liberation produced the independent states of **Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia**, among others. | **Key terms to know:** Cultural Revolution, commune, Great Leap Forward, Intifada, occupation, state capitalism. | **Memory trick – the key dates "32-46-67":** Iraq 1932, Syria and Lebanon 1946, South Yemen 1967. | **Exam tip:** The true/false items in this unit change these dates (e.g. "Syria and Lebanon 1992", "South Yemen 1905") – learn the three dates and the false ones are easy to spot.

@unit hist12-u2-l2-1 exercise.questions[0].
T: Camp David: the first Arab–Israeli peace treaty
B: **Step 1 – the wars before.** After the 1967 war Israel occupied Arab land, and in 1973 Egypt led a new attack on Israel. A UN-negotiated cease-fire ended that war in 1974. | **Step 2 – US pressure.** In 1977 US President **Jimmy Carter** began pressing for a compromise peace. | **Step 3 – the meeting.** In **September 1978** Carter met Egypt's President **Anwar Sadat** and Israel's Prime Minister **Menachem Begin** at **Camp David** (USA). The result was the **Camp David Accords**. | **Step 4 – the treaty.** In **March 1979** Sadat and Begin signed an Israeli–Egyptian peace treaty that ended the state of war between them. **Egypt** thus became the first Arab country to recognize Israel. | **Step 5 – the limit.** Many Arab countries continued to refuse to recognize Israel. | **Memory trick:** "Carter + Sadat + Begin = Camp David (1978), treaty 1979." | **Exam tip:** "The only/first Arab country to recognize Israel" (in this course) = Egypt.

@unit hist12-u2-l2-2 exercise.questions[1].
T: Deng Xiaoping and the Four Modernizations
B: **Step 1 – the end of the Mao era.** Mao Zedong died in **September 1976**. A group of reformers led by **Deng Xiaoping** took power and ended the Cultural Revolution. | **Step 2 – the policy.** Deng followed the **Four Modernizations**, focusing on four areas: **industry, agriculture, technology and national defence**. | **Step 3 – opening up.** China had been cut off from world technology for over 20 years, so the government invited **foreign investors** and sent thousands of students abroad to study science and technology. | **Step 4 – result.** China's economy grew fast, but the Communist Party kept political control (as the 1989 Tiananmen Square crackdown later showed). | **Memory trick – "I-A-T-D":** Industry, Agriculture, Technology, Defence. | **Exam tip:** Mao = Great Leap Forward and Cultural Revolution; Deng = Four Modernizations. Do not mix up the leaders.

@unit hist12-u3-l3-3 exercise.questions[1]. exercise.questions[2].
T: Case study: the Algerian War of Independence (1954–1962)
B: **Step 1 – why France refused.** Algeria was France's main colony of white settlement, with about **two million French settlers (colons)** by 1945, so France was determined not to give independence. | **Step 2 – the awakening.** In **May 1945** colonial police fired on a peaceful nationalist demonstration at **Setif**. In the rioting and French reprisals, about a hundred Europeans and up to **8,000 Algerians** were killed. | **Step 3 – the war begins.** French reforms in 1946–47 were no longer enough. The **National Liberation Front (FLN)** launched the war in **November 1954**, starting from isolated rural areas. In August 1955 the uprising reached **Constantine**, and France began sending tens of thousands of troops. | **Step 4 – escalation.** By 1958 there were up to **half a million French troops** in Algeria. Each attempt at repression brought new recruits to the FLN, and hundreds of thousands of Algerians, mostly civilians, were killed. | **Step 5 – independence.** Despite internal divisions in the FLN, by 1962 France had to negotiate. Algeria became independent in **1962** after a war in which about 20,000 French and a million Algerians died. **Ahmed Ben Bella** of the FLN became the first president. | **Step 6 – why it matters.** Algeria was the **only French colony to fight a full-scale armed struggle before independence**, and the war pushed France to grant independence to its other African colonies. | **Memory trick:** "Setif 45 sparked it, FLN 54 fought it, Ben Bella 62 won it."

@unit hist12-u3-l3-6 exercise.questions[1].
T: Eritrea: decolonization denied, armed struggle from 1961
B: **Step 1 – British rule.** After Italy's defeat in the Second World War, Eritrea came under **British occupation in 1941**. | **Step 2 – independence denied.** Most Eritreans wanted full independence, but Ethiopia wanted an outlet to the sea, and powerful states, especially the USA, supported Ethiopia's claim at the UN. The UN denied Eritrea self-determination and joined it to Ethiopia in **1952** through an unworkable **federation**. | **Step 3 – annexation.** Emperor **Haile Selassie** abolished the federation and annexed Eritrea as Ethiopia's fourteenth province in **1962**. | **Step 4 – armed struggle.** The **ELF** began the armed struggle on **1 September 1961**, when **Hamid Idris Awate** fired the first shot against Ethiopian forces near **Mount Adal**. It took thirty years (until 1991) to win independence. | **Memory trick:** "41 British, 52 federated, 61 first shot, 62 annexed, 91 free." | **Exam tip:** The start of the Eritrean armed struggle is 1961 (Awate, Mount Adal) – a year before the annexation of 1962.

@unit hist12-u3-l3-3 exercise.questions[3].
T: Guinea 1958: the one colony that said "No" to France
B: **Step 1 – de Gaulle's offer.** In 1958 France offered its African colonies membership of the **French Community**: each would run its own internal affairs but send representatives to a council in Paris that controlled foreign policy and defence. | **Step 2 – Guinea's choice.** Only **Guinea** chose full independence and cut all ties with France. | **Step 3 – France's punishment.** France withdrew all aid, personnel and equipment – even telephones and light bulbs – destroyed files and dismissed Guinean soldiers without pensions. When the new president, **Sékou Touré**, moved into the former governor's house, everything had been smashed. | **Step 4 – who helped.** The **Soviet Union** stepped in with aid. | **Step 5 – the domino effect.** The other colonies soon wanted the same independence as British West Africa, and by the end of **1960** France agreed to full independence for its colonies in West and Equatorial Africa and Madagascar. | **Memory trick:** "Guinea said No in 58; everyone else got Yes in 60."

@unit hist12-u3-l3-4 exercise.questions[4]. exercise.questions[7].
T: The Belgian legacy in Rwanda and Burundi
B: **Step 1 – divide and rule.** In Rwanda and Burundi the Belgians governed through the **minority Tutsi**, using them in the administration to control the **majority Hutu**. | **Step 2 – after independence.** The resentment between the two groups grew worse once the Belgians left, leading to civil war. | **Step 3 – genocide.** In **1994** ethnic tension in **Rwanda** grew into full-scale genocide: extremist Hutus killed Tutsis and moderate Hutus, and almost **one million people** died. | **Step 4 – compare the Congo.** In the Belgian Congo, Belgium first planned a 30-year transition but suddenly granted independence in **June 1960** after violent protests; the army mutinied and civil war broke out. | **Memory trick:** "Belgium ruled the many through the few – Rwanda paid in 1994." | **Exam tip:** The Hutu–Tutsi conflict is described as the colonial legacy of Belgium.

@unit hist12-u3-l3-7 exercise.questions[5]. exercise.questions[6].
T: Liberation movements and Black Consciousness in Southern Africa
B: **Step 1 – match each movement to its country.** **SWAPO** – Namibia; **ZANU** (and ZAPU) – Zimbabwe; **FRELIMO** – Mozambique; **MPLA** – Angola; **PAIGC** (founded 1956 by **Amílcar Cabral**) – Guinea-Bissau and Cape Verde. | **Step 2 – Namibia.** A former German colony, it was given to South Africa as a mandate in 1920. After 1948 South Africa applied apartheid there and refused to hand it to the UN. The UN recognized SWAPO as the true representative, and after SWAPO's guerrilla war (helped by Cuban forces) and Western pressure, Namibia became independent in **1990**. | **Step 3 – Black Consciousness.** In the early 1970s black workers' strikes spread, and among black students a new Africanist movement, **Black Consciousness**, led by **Steve Biko**, aimed to restore black self-respect and win liberation without relying on white liberals. | **Step 4 – Soweto.** On **16 June 1976** police fired on 15,000 schoolchildren protesting in **Soweto**. Revolt spread across the country, at least 600 people died in 1976–77, and Biko was beaten to death in police custody (1977). | **Step 5 – the ANC story.** ANC founded **1912**; the PAC broke away from it; **Nelson Mandela** became the first black president (1994), followed by **Thabo Mbeki** (1999). | **Memory trick:** "Biko – Black Consciousness; Mbeki – Mandela's successor."

T: Unit 4 summary in full – Africa since independence
B: **Point 1 – different roads.** After independence African countries chose different policies: some, like **Kenya**, took a **capitalist** path; others, like **Tanzania**, followed **socialist** policies. | **Point 2 – opportunities and challenges.** Independence brought opportunities, but also many problems. | **Point 3 – ethnic conflict.** Ethnic conflicts broke out, for example the **Biafran War in Nigeria**. | **Point 4 – political instability.** It led to military coups in several states, such as **Idi Amin's coup in Uganda**. | **Point 5 – colonial economic legacy.** Colonialism left economies unbalanced and **dependent on foreign markets**. | **Point 6 – other burdens.** Drought, disease, high population growth and poverty put the new states at a disadvantage. | **Point 7 – the verdict by the end of the 1970s.** The hope that independence would bring peace and prosperity was not realized: colonial after-effects made states vulnerable to internal conflict, made worse by Cold War economic and political pressures. | **Key terms:** African socialism, capitalism, civil war, coup d'état, debt crisis, democratization, ethnicity, intervention, nation-building, neo-colonialism, Ujamaa, urbanization. | **Memory trick – "P-E-C-E-D":** Paths (capitalist/socialist), Ethnic conflict, Coups, Economy dependent, Drought and disease.

@unit hist12-u4-l4-2 exercise.questions[1]. exercise.questions[2].
T: Nyerere's African Socialism: Ujamaa and the Arusha Declaration
B: **Step 1 – why a new model.** The fall of Nkrumah in Ghana convinced Tanzania's **Julius Nyerere** that a different kind of African socialism was needed. | **Step 2 – the Arusha Declaration (1967).** Nyerere set out the policy in the **Arusha Declaration of 1967**, based on **ujamaa** ("solidarity" or familyhood) villages. The aim was a **self-reliant** Tanzania that would depend on its own resources – mainly peasant labour – instead of foreign aid and investment. | **Step 3 – how it worked.** The state led the economy: land, large companies and banks were nationalized (with compensation), and only small firms stayed private. A **Leadership Code** banned officials from capitalist business, making Tanzania one of the least corrupt countries in Africa at the time. | **Step 4 – Nyerere's idea in his own words.** "Socialism is an attitude of mind. The basis of socialism is a belief in the oneness of man and the common historical destiny of humankind… its basis is human equality." Socialism serves people of every colour – "not the state, not the flag". | **Step 5 – the villages.** Large collective villages with communal land, shared resources and elected leaders replaced scattered farms, improving access to schools and clinics. | **Step 6 – problems.** The policy was imposed from above. Peasants did not want to leave ancestral land or private plots, so compulsory **"villagization"** was introduced (helped by the 1973–74 drought), and production suffered. | **Memory trick:** "Arusha 1967 = Ujamaa = self-reliance."

@unit hist12-u5-l5-2 exercise.questions[0]. exercise.questions[5]. exercise.questions[6].
T: From zones to unity: the Anseba (1968) and Adobha (1969) conferences
B: **Step 1 – Anseba Conference.** On **19 September 1968** the leaders and cadres of the **3rd, 4th and 5th zones** met at **Orotta** in the **Anseba Conference** and formed the **Tripartite Union**, joined by the training and reserve units. It elected a 12-member **Provisional Revolutionary Council** chaired by **Mohammed Ahmed Abdu**. The Supreme Council refused to recognize it, but the Union beat off an Ethiopian offensive and quickly launched its own attacks – showing the advantage of unity. | **Step 2 – Adobha Conference.** In **August 1969** the Tripartite Union and the 1st and 2nd zones met at **Adobha**, **abolished the zonal commands** and formed a united command, completing the unity of the ELA. | **Step 3 – the democratic resolutions of Adobha.** (1) hold a **National Congress within one year**; (2) form a **preparatory committee** for it; (3) appoint a **committee to investigate misdeeds** against fighters and the people; (4) keep the Supreme Council with limited powers until the Congress and disband the Revolutionary Command; (5) zonal commanders and their deputies may hold **no leadership role** until the Congress. | **Step 4 – the General Command.** A **38-member General Command**, chaired by Mohammed Ahmed Abdu, took control of the ELA. It failed to carry out the democratic resolutions, which provoked new opposition. | **Memory trick:** "Anseba = three zones unite (Tripartite); Adobha = all zones abolished (General Command)." | **Exam tip:** The zonal commands were replaced by the General Command after the Adobha Conference; the Tripartite Union came from the Anseba Conference.

@unit hist12-u5-l5-1 exercise.questions[3].
T: Ela Tsaeda (1965): how the ELM was pushed out of the field
B: **Step 1 – two organizations.** The **Eritrean Liberation Movement (ELM)** was founded first (1958); the **ELF** was founded in 1960 and began the armed struggle in 1961. Political differences between their leaders kept growing. | **Step 2 – the ELM tries to fight.** As its political influence weakened, the ELM repeatedly called for unity, but the ELF ignored it. The ELM then sent a force of about **fifty men** from Sudan to **Sahel** to start its own armed struggle. | **Step 3 – the clash.** Declaring that "the Eritrean arena cannot support more than one organization", the ELF attacked the ELM at **Ela Tsaeda in mid-1965**. Four ELM fighters were killed and the rest were forced to lay down their arms. | **Step 4 – result.** This ended the ELM's presence in the field. | **Memory trick:** "One arena, one front – Ela Tsaeda 1965."

@unit hist12-u5-l5-14 exercise.questions[2]. exercise.questions[7].
T: Afabet 1988 and the 1993 referendum
B: **Step 1 – the Nadew Command.** The **Nadew Command** was a 22,000-strong Ethiopian army that had held the **Nakfa front** for nine years. | **Step 2 – the operation.** On **17–19 March 1988** the EPLF attacked from four flanks: the left flank sealed the Felket valley; the central flank surprised the trenches in the Hiday valley; the right flank hit the 18th Division; the fourth flank cut the **Afabet–Keren road** from behind. | **Step 3 – the result.** **Afabet** was liberated within 48 hours. The Derg lost about **18,000 troops (about 20 % of its army)**, three Soviet advisers were captured, and 50 tanks, 100 trucks and 60 heavy guns were seized. The balance of power tipped decisively towards the Eritrean Revolution. | **Step 4 – the referendum.** After liberation in 1991, a Provisional Government passed a citizenship law and set up a referendum commission, with UN and OAU observers. From **23 to 25 April 1993** over **1.1 million Eritreans** voted at **1,010 polling stations** in Eritrea and abroad. | **Step 5 – the result.** On **27 April 1993** the result was announced: **99.8 %** for independence. Eritrea became an internationally recognized sovereign state (independence proclaimed on 24 May 1993). | **Memory trick:** "Afabet 88 broke them, Asmara 91 freed us, the ballot of 93 confirmed it."
