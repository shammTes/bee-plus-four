# Round 4 - history_12: expanding short lessons

@lesson hist12-u1-l1-6
T: How war research changed science, and how to spot bias in Cold War sources
B: **From war to peace.** Governments recruited scientists in WWII to make weapons. The atomic bomb was the most famous result, but **computers** and **jet aircraft** show how wartime technology was adapted for peaceful use. | **"Big science".** Wartime projects needed big teams, huge laboratories and expensive equipment that only governments and large corporations could pay for, and this became the new model for research. | **The space race.** The USSR launched **Sputnik I in 1957**; twelve years later, in **1969**, the USA landed astronauts on the Moon. | **Skill – checking a source for bias.** Ask: Which words make one side sound good or bad? Which facts are included and which are left out? What is exaggerated? Why was the source made, and does the author have a reason to be one-sided or to impress a certain audience? | **Exam tip:** Truman's 1947 speech or a Soviet statement will always show its own side's view – name the loaded words to prove bias. | **Memory trick:** "Bomb → Computer → Sputnik (1957) → Moon (1969)."

@lesson hist12-u3-l3-1
T: Weighing the three factors (exam-style)
B: **A. Decline of the colonial powers.** The world wars left Europe too poor to hold its empires, and the powers realized they could keep economic influence without political rule. Criticism: this view is **Eurocentric**, as if Europeans alone decided. | **B. African nationalism.** After WWII united African movements forced the colonizers out. Criticism: in some places (such as French West Africa) independence came without a strong nationalist movement. | **C. The Cold War.** The USA and USSR both opposed old empires because they wanted influence in the new states. | **Conclusion.** Most historians say independence came from a **combination** of all three. | **Memory trick:** "Weak Europe, Strong Africa, Superpower pressure."

@lesson hist12-u3-l3-5
T: Guinea-Bissau: the case study in steps
B: **1956** – Amilcar Cabral founds the **PAIGC**. **1959** – the Bissau dockworkers' strike is crushed and fifty workers are killed; this turning point leads to armed struggle. **1963** – the liberation war begins; liberated rural areas get schools, clinics, roads and farming projects. **September 1973** – the PAIGC declares an independent state. **January 1974** – Cabral is assassinated. **April 1974** – Portuguese officers, including General Spinola, who had commanded in Guinea and saw the war could not be won, overthrow the dictatorship. **September 1974** – Portuguese troops leave; all Portuguese colonies are independent by **1975**. | **Key point.** The African liberation wars themselves caused the coup in Portugal. | **Memory trick:** "56 party, 59 massacre, 63 war, 73 state, 74 coup."

@lesson hist12-u4-l4-1
T: The two goals of independence, explained
B: **Goal 1 – economic growth.** The 1960s were good years: oil cost only $2 a barrel while prices of African exports (cocoa, coffee, copper) were high, so exports grew by **6 % a year**, and farming and mining expanded. Leaders believed **industrialization** was the key to breaking colonial trade patterns and dependence on the West. | **Goal 2 – national unity and African culture.** Borders drawn by Europeans ignored pre-colonial states and peoples, so leaders stressed **nation building** to unite many ethnic groups. Independence also brought a cultural revival: in **1966 Senegal** hosted the first festival of African arts and culture, and African history and archaeology became serious university subjects. | **Memory trick:** "Grow the economy, Glue the nation." | **Exam tip:** When asked about the "ideals of independence", name both goals and give one fact for each.

@lesson hist12-u4-l4-4
T: Six problems of independent Africa, one fact each
B: **1. Environment.** Poor soils and unreliable rain; the **Sahel drought of 1968–73** cut northern Nigeria's groundnut harvest from 765,000 to 250,000 tons. | **2. Population growth.** Africa's population tripled from 200 million (1950) to 600 million (2000), crowding cities. | **3. Ethnicity.** Politicians sought votes from their own ethnic groups, weakening national unity. | **4. Military coups.** More than 60 between 1963 and 1989; by the late 1980s half of Africa was under military rule (e.g. Bokassa in the Central African Republic). | **5. Debt crisis.** Oil rose from $2 (1973 start) to $34 a barrel (1980); in 1972 a ton of Tanzanian tea bought 60 barrels of oil, in 1980 only 4.5. | **6. The Cold War.** The superpowers armed rival sides, e.g. Soviet aid to Angola, Mozambique, Somalia and Ethiopia. | **Memory trick:** "Earth, People, Tribe, Gun, Debt, Superpowers" – four internal, two external.

@lesson hist12-u5-l5-6
T: The liberation of towns, 1977–78, in order
B: **Background.** By 1976 the revolution held **90 %** of rural Eritrea and followed the strategy of liberating the land and people step by step. | **EPLA victories.** **Karora** (January 1977) was first; **Nakfa** (March 1977) after a six-month siege – Ethiopian relief forces were destroyed at the Anseba River and on the Naro plains; **Afabet** (6 April 1977); **Dekemhare and Keren** (July 1977), followed by Segeneiti and Digsa. By the end of 1977 Asmara was encircled, Ghindae and the Asmara–Massawa road were free and more than half of Massawa was held. | **ELF victories (1977).** Omhajer, Tessenei, Adi Quala, Mendefera and Agordat. | **Early 1978.** Only Assab, Asmara, Adi Keih, Barentu and part of Massawa remained in Ethiopian hands. | **Why Nakfa matters.** It was the turning point towards strategic offensive. | **Memory trick:** "K-N-A: Karora, Nakfa, Afabet – then Keren."

@lesson hist12-u5-l5-8
T: The road to the October 20 Unity Agreement (1977)
B: **1974–75.** After the first civil war, leaders coordinated operations and dialogue committees formed (March 1975), paused for the ELF's Second Congress (May 1975), which ended the civil war officially. | **The Khartoum Agreement (September 1975).** The EPLF's Foreign Mission, bypassing the field leadership, signed a merger with the ELF; the EPLF rejected it at its Semenawi Bahri conference (7–12 November 1975). The Foreign Mission split off in March 1976 as the ELF-PLF. | **The Agreement (20 October 1977, Khartoum).** Caused by the need for unity, fighters' wishes, popular pressure and friendly countries. It provided: (1) unity through joint programmes and gradual coordination; (2) a joint political leadership; (3) a joint military strategy and coordinating committees. | **Memory trick:** "Programmes, Political leadership, Plan of war."

@lesson hist12-u5-l5-11
T: From cooperation to collapse, 1978–1981
B: **Hope.** In April 1978 the fronts agreed on a joint political leadership and joint military, propaganda, economic, foreign and social committees; the joint leadership met repeatedly in 1979–80. | **Breakdown.** Deep political disagreements and local clashes grew, and in **July 1980** all-out war began – the second civil war. | **Tunis talks.** The Arab League brought both sides to Tunis; they agreed to stop fighting and propaganda, but the war continued. | **The end of the ELF.** In **August 1981** ELF units were pushed into Sudan and disarmed by the Sudanese government; they split into hostile factions (such as Sagim) and the ELF ceased to exist as a united force. | **Memory trick:** "1978 agree, 1980 war, 1981 gone."

@lesson hist12-u5-l5-13
T: The Second and Unity Congress (12–19 March 1987) in facts
B: **Why "Unity".** The ELF Central Leadership (**Sagim**) contacted the EPLF in 1983, entered Eritrea on 11 June 1983, ran joint programmes in 1984–85 and agreed in 1986 to unite at the congress. | **Who came.** 1,287 delegates; Woldeab Woldemariam attended and Ibrahim Sultan sent a video message; ELF-RC and ELF-PLF attended, and 25 governments and organizations spoke. | **Decisions.** It approved the National Democratic Program and constitution, and elected 71 Central Committee members and 7 alternates, who elected **Isaias Afwerki** Secretary General. | **The eight programme points.** Democratic government; self-reliant economy; culture, education, technology and health; social rights; equality of nationalities; a strong People's Army; freedom of religion; rights of Eritreans abroad. It also accepted political pluralism, a mixed economy and a non-aligned foreign policy. | **Memory trick:** "1983 contact, 1987 congress, 8 points."
